THE COMPACT — LUNA HAVEN, THE WARD / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.2 min

Mara was at the lock, and she had never once come to the lock.

Elena saw her through the inner port while the seals were still cycling: the braid, the dark suit, one palm flat on the wall beside the hatch. Behind her the Tubes ran away in honey light, and people went past in soft shoes, and none of them looked at the keeper standing in the arrivals bay like someone waiting to take delivery of a part.

The hatch opened. Elena stepped through with her kit bag and the work order at the edge of her eye. Foresight field service. Luna Haven coupling: re-zero before restart. Engineer: E. Vázquez. She asked for it the morning the run reset. Nobody else wanted a day's shuttle for a job a technician could do.

Her lace chimed. Daniel, from Vila Verde.

Hall roof's on. Half of it. My mother says the other half is a matter of faith. It's 38 here and the children are living in the cistern room. A gap, and then: Ask me something.

She put the handheld in her pocket and closed her hand over it.

The Haven pause went by. Mara didn't fill it.

"You're not on my list," Mara said.

"I'm on Foresight's. The drum needs re-zeroing before the restart."

"The drum." Mara took her palm off the wall. She had a handheld in it, a Haven one, plain grey with the edges worn pale. "A post came for me on the sixth. Compact to Compact. Iverson at the Battery has it too, on Charter routing, unless somebody in your Spire read it first."

She held it out. "Listen to it here. Before you touch my wall."

"I'm on a schedule."

"So was he."

Elena took it.

A man's voice, close to the mic, tired, with the Reef Coast flattening the ends of his words. Cobalt Basin, the fifth of June, oh-six-forty. Kaelin Drake, field operations. Behind him, under him, someone was breathing, slow and even, twelve to the minute. Elena counted it without meaning to.

Bad news first.

She listened to all of it. Minds alive in the rock under the station. Martian. Not many. They hid, a long time ago, from the thing that had been sending warnings since January. The voice your world is listening to is ours. It is the hungry one. A warmth made of a great many people, that said come in. It's hollow. The Hollow. Two descents, the twelfth of April and the third of June. Nobody signed either one.

The second time it knew my name before I gave it.

The breathing went on behind him to the end. Stop the calibration. Don't light it. Reply when you can.

The handheld went dark. Elena gave it back.

"He went into an uncontrolled lattice twice with no keeper and no log," she said. "He's bleeding. You can hear it: he stops twice and starts again somewhere else. He has no traces, no names for these minds, no sign-off, and he's asking two blocs to stop on his word. If one of your keepers came to you with that, you'd take them off the wall."

"I would."

"Then."

"I'd also listen to what they heard." Mara put the handheld away. "You're doing the voice. The one for rooms."

"It's the right voice for this."

"It knew his name," Mara said. "You heard that part."

"So did my sister."

She hadn't meant to say it. It sat in the bay between them while a cart went past loaded with crop trays.

Mara looked at her for a long moment.

"Then come to the Ward," she said.

· · ·

The junction slate still said ninety-eight. Under it, in fresher chalk and the same square hand as the scope on the session-room wall, somebody had added a second line.

CHILDREN OFF THE WALLS AT NIGHT. FOLD SOMETHING BEHIND THEM.

The corridor outside the Ward had been the warmest place in Haven. Three children sat along it now with their backs to folded blankets, a coat, a crop sack stuffed with straw, a hand's width of cloth between every one of them and the honey light.

The smallest had slid sideways in her sleep, and her cheek was creeping toward the grain. A man in a keeper's suit came along and moved her back without waking her, and folded the sack again, and stood there a moment with his hand on it.

Asha's daughter was the one at the end. She was awake. She had Ravi's blanket folded in her lap, far too much blanket for her, and she watched Elena go by with the whole of her face.

The Ward kept its light, and it smelled of bread proving, as the deep rows did when the lattice worked hard. The beds were grown into the long wall, so that everyone in them lay with the warm at their back, and seven were full.

At its head, over the medic's station, the monitors ran in a column: seven traces, and under them the day's figures from Earth as Haven's channel took them in, because Haven had family down there and always wanted to know. GULF CRESCENT — HEAT DEATHS, 24 HRS. A number. Under it, yesterday's, larger.

Elena read the traces.

"The calibration ran through our wall from the fourth," Mara said, behind her. "Sweeps every half hour through the night. The run was meant to finish on the sixth. Then it lost its lock on Mars on the fifth, and stopped. By then I had these."

"Who was on the wall?"

"Nobody was on the wall. Nobody needs to be, once your drum's live." Mara's palm went flat above the nearest bed. "The keepers on night duty. A crop hand who fell asleep in the deep rows waiting for a pump. A woman off the night shift at the water plant who sat down in the corridor because her feet hurt. Everyone who leaned."

The medic came down the Ward toward them, reading a slate standing up. She was taller than anyone in the room. She looked at Elena once, as Elena had seen her look at a bad trace, and went to the nearest bed.

"Two," she said. "Turn him."

Mara went to the other side. Between them they rolled the man in the bed onto his other shoulder, the medic's hand under his head so it didn't touch the wall, Mara's under his hip, and laid him down again with a folded towel between him and the grain.

Elena knew the man. He had eaten two bowls of Ravi's mushrooms on the evening of the bounded test with the concentration of a man told he was allowed. He opened his eyes now and looked past her, at the end of the room, and smiled at it.

"The brown one with the collar," he said, in Talk, and her lace gave it to her a fraction behind his lips. "He's going to be twenty."

Then he was gone again, and the trace under his name lay down long and slow.

"He's been counting dogs since the fifth," the medic said. "We don't have dogs. Haven has never had a dog." She wrote a line on her slate. "He's fading. Long absences, then he comes back pleased."

The next bed was a woman Elena didn't know, grey at the temples, in a water-plant coverall nobody had taken off her. The medic leaned over to check her line, and the woman opened her eyes, and said, clearly, crossly, to the medic's face, "Duérmete ya, o me siento encima."

Spanish. The lace had nothing to give. It let it through untouched, and so the words came to Elena in her own head with no voice laid over them but the one that had always said them, from the top bunk in a hot room in Veracruz.

"Does she speak Spanish?" Elena said.

"She speaks Talk and hydraulics." The medic tucked the woman's hand back under the cover. "I speak Spanish. My mother's from Puebla. I didn't teach her that."

The woman turned her head toward the wall. The towel was there. She pressed her face into it anyway, as close to the warm as it let her come.

· · ·

Asha was in the sixth bed, sitting up.

"Look who it is," she said, in Talk, and grinned, and it was Asha's grin, all of it. "The observer. They've given me a bed with a view. It's lovely. I've never had a bed with a wall in it before. At home the wall's just over there doing nothing."

Elena laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, and Asha looked delighted.

"There," said Asha. "I told Ravi. I said she's got a laugh in her somewhere, she just keeps it in a—"

She stopped.

Her face went smooth. Her eyes went to the wall, and through it, and her hand on the cover opened. The trace under her name lay down. Elena counted. Eleven. Twelve. At the end of the room the medic had stopped writing.

At nineteen, Asha came back. She looked at Elena as if Elena had just come in.

"Feet up," she said, in English, kindly. "You'll cry about them."

Elena was standing. Her feet were on the floor.

"Where did you hear that?"

"I don't know." Asha's face went puzzled, and then easy. "It's just a thing people say."

In the bed beyond her lay Ravi.

He lay on his side with his back to the wall. His blanket was in the corridor, in a child's lap. His beard had been combed. His hands were open on the cover. His eyes were open too, and they were not looking at anything. The trace under his name ran flat and even.

Twelve breaths to the minute. Everybody breathed twelve, lying still. She had counted the same twelve in the lock, under a man's voice from Mars, and it meant nothing, and she could not stop hearing it.

"He went quiet on the fifth," Mara said. "At the half-past sweep. In the middle of a sentence about his mushrooms."

"What was the sentence?"

"The trick is the dark." Mara put her palm on the wall over his head. Nobody patted it. "He'd said it before."

The monitors over the station turned their page. The heat count came up again. Elena looked at it, and then she looked at the traces, and then she looked at the traces only.

The wall was a hand's width from her hip. The warmth at the back of her head leaned toward it, as it had on the roof, and somewhere under the Ward's hum it was raining. She stepped back from the wall.

She did what she did with every fault. She built the tree.

Seven people, one wall, one coupling. Each of them saying, out of an absence, a line that belonged to Soria, word for word. And one wall that Elena herself had lain against, on the thirty-first of May, for ten minutes and twenty seconds, weeping, with her sister in her head, while the bleed off her ran higher than the Ward had a line for.

So it was her. She had brought Soria up with her and left her in Haven's grain, like heat in a wall, and on the fourth the sweeps had found it and carried it to everyone who leaned.

That was a mechanism. It was ugly and it was hers, and it needed nothing from Mars: no hungry thing in a rock, no man on a cot with someone breathing behind him.

She held on to it. It was the best thing she had.

Then she remembered the corridor outside this room on the twenty-second of April, and Asha with her daughter on her hip, grinning at her over the child's shoulder. Sleep's only practice, you know. And you need the practice.

The twenty-second of April. Five weeks before Elena had ever lain down on that couch. The day the drum was first live, the only day, until the fourth of June.

It hadn't come from her. It had come in on the sweep.

The sweep came up the long line from Cobalt Basin's rock to the drum on Haven's wall. Whatever was at the far end of it had been in Asha's mouth before Elena ever lay down.

The tree came down. She could see every branch of it go.

"You promised me something," Mara said.

Elena turned.

"In the Rows, before I pulled the lever. Ravi hears it every day, I said, whatever it is. If you see what he's listening to, you tell me. Not the review." Mara's palm was still on the wall over Ravi's head. "You saw. I watched you see. Then you came up and asked for the Lantern."

The medic had come down to the end of the Ward and was standing a little off, holding her slate against her chest, not pretending not to listen.

"He was listening toward her," Elena said. "To my sister. That's where he was looking, when I went in. That's what he hears."

"Then she's what's taking him."

"No."

"He went to her, Elena. Every one of them went somewhere warm and wanted to stay." Mara didn't raise her voice. It was the flat voice she had used to write the scope. "Your sister. Or the thing your man on Mars says is talking. I don't care which. My people are in it."

"My sister died on a lattice line in 2089 because—"

She stopped. The rain was louder. She waited for it as she waited for a page to load, and Mara watched her wait.

"Because a session went wrong," she said. "That's what happened to her. An accident. She went in as herself." Elena heard the review-room voice coming and let it, because it was all she had left. "If she's in there, she went in as Soria. It doesn't make her this."

"Do you know how she died?"

"I was there."

"In the room?" Mara said it without cruelty. "When it went wrong. Were you in the room?"

Elena didn't answer.

Above the station the column turned again, and the heat count came up, and she did not read it.

On the medic's slate, held against her chest, Elena could see the top of the protocol column the Ward kept for every bed. Expected effects: see observer's note, 22 April. No finding. E. Vázquez. Her own name, in her own small grey letters, at the head of seven beds.

She looked up at the woman holding it.

"I've been here twice," Elena said. "I never asked your name."

The medic considered her.

"Rosa," she said. "Medina. My cousin works for your Rachel Iverson. He'll be thrilled. He collects people who've asked."

"Rosa." Elena said it back so she would keep it. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Fix it." Rosa tucked the slate under her arm and went back up the Ward. "Five," she said, to nobody, to the room. "Turn her."

· · ·

The session room was cold, and the scope was still on the wall.

E. VÁZQUEZ. ONE SESSION. TEN MINUTES. Grease pencil, square capitals, at the eye level of someone lying on the couch. Below it, the lines about the lever. Nobody had rubbed it out. Under the scope the couch lay grey where the lever had come down, a body's length of it, and had not come back.

The drum sat clamped at the head of the couch where it always had, squat and grey and Charter, the Foresight mark on its side. Dark. Its cable ran out under the door to the mast and the long line home.

Elena knelt in front of it and opened the maintenance hatch, and the port inside lit up for her, because it had been told to.

Field service. E. Vázquez. Re-zero sequence ready.

Mara stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame. She didn't come in.

Twenty minutes. Load the reference, walk the coupling to null, sign it, and the drum would be ready, and the restart would sweep Haven's wall every half hour through the night, and seven people in the Ward would lean toward it on folded towels.

The reference could wait an hour.

In her pocket the handheld held Daniel's line, Ask me something. She didn't take it out.

Through the drum, the port could see the Spire's maintenance layer. She had built the layer's first door herself, four years ago, through a roof in the Polder. This was the front door, and the work order had opened it to her for the day. Every step she took through it the drum wrote down, Field service: E. Vázquez, as she had built it to.

She went down.

The 2089 line was where she had left it. LATTICE PROGRAMME — SPIRE LINE — 2086–2089. Sealed: M. Bellamy, personal authority. Under the seal the request field waited, polite, outlined in grey. Requests to view are notified to the sealing authority.

She didn't request.

"What are you doing?" said Mara.

"Proving it." Elena's hands were steady. She noticed that. "The records are in the vault. I can't reach them from here. The manifest I can."

"And what does that prove?"

"That she volunteered. That she went in on the schedule we wrote, in the slot she signed for, and the session went wrong. That it was an accident." The seal guarded the request. It didn't guard the layer under it, and the manifest the session schedulers had read every morning sat on the near side of the vault door. "That she went in as herself."

"You're breaking into your director's files," Mara said, "in my session room."

"Yes."

"In front of me."

"You told me nothing in secret." Elena didn't look round. "You can stop me. It's your room."

The Haven pause went out, and came back, and Mara didn't move from the door.

The layer showed her the line's contents as a list of names with sizes beside them, and the first name was the one she wanted. MANIFEST. She had designed the format. She had sat in a room and argued for the columns. Number, name, role, sessions, consent.

She opened it.

It came up in the Spire's grey, and she read down it fast, from the top. She knew these names. Volunteers, every one, in the order they had come in. A teacher from the Shallows. Two brothers from Queens who had done it for the stipend. A pilot. Number thirty-one, VÁZQUEZ, SORIA, VOLUNTEER, and a count of sessions she knew to the last, because she had watched every one of them and then could not watch the last.

Every row said VOLUNTEER.

Except the first.

Number one, above them all, in a row that had been there before the first volunteer signed. Its role column didn't say volunteer. It said PATIENT. Its consent column held another man's name.

BELLAMY, THEODORE.

Theo. The director's son. Everyone at Foresight knew he had a son, and that the boy had been hurt in 2081, and nobody had ever once said his name in the Spire.

Elena knelt on the cold floor of Haven with her hands on a Charter drum and read it again. It said the same thing the second time.
