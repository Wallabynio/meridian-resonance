The Narrow Gate · Chapter 07

The other end

The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

07

10 MarchKaelinVila VerdeMars delay 17.3 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE / Kaelin Drake — Mars 17.3 min

The third doorway up from the platform had its sandbags laid like books on a shelf, every joint sitting on the joint below it, and Kaelin stopped in front of it because he couldn't walk past.

The low street climbed from the old line between houses built on top of houses, brick and old concrete painted every colour that came in a tin. Every door had its wall of bags across the bottom, knee-high and grey with dried mud.

The wet season was nearly done. A thin brown run still came down the gutters from the night, and the street smelled of river mud and diesel, and of coffee from a window where somebody was roasting it in a pan.

Those bags would hold a wet season. They wouldn't hold a bad night. Water went looking for a straight joint, and found one, and took the wall out from the middle.

Two children sat on the step behind them, a girl and a smaller boy, sharing a pastel. They watched him look.

He hadn't come for this. He'd come down overnight on the vac, and then on the old line's battery train, which stopped at every town as if it owed it money.

Mara's post had said Rocha, at the hall, and the hall was further up. His lace had gone at the edge of town, as if someone had switched off a room. The prices and street names stopped floating over the doors, and what was left was the doors.

He leaned the stick against the wall, pointed at the bags, pointed at himself, and made a question of both hands.

The girl looked at the boy. The boy shrugged with the pastel.

Kaelin took the top bag off and set it on the step. Then the next. They were two-thirds full. That was right. They were tied at the neck. That was wrong. He showed the girl how you folded the neck under and laid the bag on its own fold so the weight sealed it, and laid the next one half across the join, like brick. Then he stamped on it.

The girl stamped on the next one harder than it needed. The boy ate the rest of the pastel and started bringing bags.

His knees had views about all of it. He got the row down and the second row on, every joint broken, and stood up in stages. The street was watching now from three windows and a stall. He made the ring with his thumb and forefinger and held it up. Full air.

The girl made it back at him. Then she made it at the boy, and the boy made it at the stall, and a woman at a window made it at nobody at all, laughing, and for a moment half the low street was telling the other half everything was all right.

"Who taught them that?"

A man had come down the hill, in an olive work jacket, with black hair that wouldn't lie flat and the backs of his hands spotted with old solder burns. He spoke English with São Paulo in it.

"Divers," Kaelin said. "Then suit crews. Then me, just now."

"They'll do it at the teacher all week." The man looked at the bags and then at him. "Daniel Rocha."

"Drake."

"I know." Daniel picked up the stick and held it out, handle first. "I watched you get off the train, from the bridge over the line. You counted the steps down from the platform."

"Fourteen."

"Fifteen. One's under the water." Daniel looked down the street toward the line and back. "You came in on the vac."

"Last night."

"In your own name?"

"It's the only one I've got."

Daniel didn't seem to like that, but he didn't argue with it either. He started up the hill at a pace that let a man on a stick keep up without being seen to be let.

The street turned into steps. On the third landing Kaelin got his handheld out. The Continuity's map had cached on the train, and it showed the hillside as a grey blank between two named streets. No addresses. No roofs. He looked up from it. On every roof up the slope, beside every blue tank, someone had painted a circle on the brick, white or yellow or red, some fresh and some worn to a ghost.

"Who knows you're here?" Daniel asked.

"Mara. You. Them." He nodded back at the children. "Aur'k knows where."

"Not why."

"Not why."

Daniel stopped on the next landing and turned round, and Kaelin saw he wasn't stopping for the hill.

"She's been on that roof since six," Daniel said. "She hasn't slept a night since June. She told me she'd asked Mara for you, and that's new, her telling me. So I'm asking. Why are you here?"

Kaelin had answers ready. To say thank you. To see the other end. They were all true, and none of them was it.

"Because nobody in New York knows what I know," he said. "And she does."

Daniel looked at him a while longer. Then he nodded once and went on up.

· · ·

The hall was dim after the street and smelled of the tank, cold stone and wet rope. Paper was everywhere: the mesh drawn up one wall and onto the ceiling, rotas, a child's drawing of a rocket taped over a crack.

The woman at the rota table was Daniel's mother. She had grey hair pulled back and reading glasses on a cord. She looked at him over them for a long time. Then she poured a cup from the jug, ticked a box, and held it out.

"Obrigado," Kaelin said. It was one of his four words.

"Está magro," she said to Daniel.

"She says you're thin."

"Tell her I'm heavy."

Daniel told her. She laughed once, short, and pointed at the ceiling with her pen. The water was warm and tasted of the tank, and he drank all of it, because he'd been raised by a woman with a hotplate and knew what a cup like that was.

The ladder to the roof ran up the hall's outside wall, steel, sixteen rungs. He hooked the stick through his belt and went up on his arms and his good leg, the bad hip taking what was left.

She was at the far end, among the panels, crouched over a node with its lid off under the tank's overhang, beside a white circle painted on the brick. She stood up when his head came over the edge, and didn't come toward him.

He knew her from the photograph at the head of her files, a year out of date: dark hair pinned tight, a narrow face, Spire black. The hair was down now, held back with a strip of cloth, and she wore a man's shirt with the sleeves rolled. She was thinner. She stood where she could see the whole roof, and the ladder, and him.

"Mr Drake."

"Kaelin."

"Elena." She began to fold her hands in front of her, and didn't. "You cut on my count."

"On mine. Near enough yours." He got off the ladder and found his feet. "Your post came in at the frame with my hand on the clamp. A minute later and I'd have pulled the heads."

"I know. Ines wrote. Drake's count." She looked at the stick and away. "How are they?"

He'd carried them all the way here.

"Breathing," he said. "Petra, Hemi, the nine. Nobody's come up. Haven?"

"Eleven. The rest asleep." She didn't look away. "I knew an hour before what a cut would do to them. I sent you the time anyway."

That hadn't been in the files. He stood with it a moment. Under the stick his right knee had started to shake, and he let it.

"So did I," he said. "Know. Aur'k told me at the edge, in a voice that wasn't his, and it was true. I cut anyway. On your time."

Neither of them said anything.

"Sit down," Elena said. "Before you fall down."

He nearly laughed. He sat on a crate in the shade of the tank, where you'd put a man you thought would fall down, and she sat on an upturned bucket by the node, facing him, her back to the panels.

"Your post said minds, alive, in the rock," she said. "It said the Hollow. It didn't give a name for who talked to you."

"No."

"Will you?"

He'd left it out of the post to keep it Aur'k's. But Aur'k had said it to the rock, not to him, and the rock had answered.

"Zhenyth," he said. "That's what they answer to."

She said it once under her breath, testing the shape.

He gave her the part he hadn't put in a post, in as few words as it would go into: the water going out of Mars a sea at a time, the last of them getting into each other to keep warm, one boat, and nobody ever getting off.

"How did the boat start?"

"Wouldn't say." He turned the stick between his hands. "Then the boat noticed me. It knew my name. It laughed." He looked at the panels. "Petra laughs through her nose, a little snort, like she's caught herself at it. It did that."

Elena's face didn't move. It was past two. Over the ridge behind her the sky had gone the colour of dishwater, and down in the street a shutter came down with a bang.

The rain came across the valley in a wave, a curtain dragged over the roofs, and every roof it reached began to roar. Kaelin was half up for the ladder by reflex. Elena reached for the node's open lid as it came, and her hand stopped halfway, and stayed out in the rain with her hair going dark and flat. A minute in, her head turned.

He knew that stop. He'd seen it on Bao at the frame, and in medical on Petra a hundred times before she went still: a turn of the head, a few degrees, as if somebody behind her and to the left had said her name.

She had stood up. She was walking, slowly, across the roof toward its edge, toward her left, the side she had turned her head to. Past the panels. Past the node. The parapet on that side was knee-high, and below it the street was four metres down and running brown.

He looked at the ladder, sixteen rungs running with water, and nobody in this town could fly anyone anywhere. He could have shouted for Daniel and a rope. There wasn't time for a rope.

Kaelin couldn't run. He didn't try. He went across the wet roof on the stick in three bad strides, each one landing on a hip the physio called a year from forgiving him, and got his hand round her wrist a step from the parapet, and held on with all the weight he had.

She stopped. She looked down at his hand on her wrist as if it were somebody else's.

"What do you hear?"

She came back from wherever it was. It took a moment. "Rain."

He walked her back to the tank, his hand still round her wrist, and sat her on the crate in its lee, and got himself down onto the wet roof beside her, which his hips charged him for in full, and didn't let go. The rain was warm, like the rain at home, and it got in his collar.

"Is that all?"

She looked at him properly then. Somebody had asked her before, he thought, and she'd lied to them, and she wasn't going to lie to him, because he'd know.

"It's what I listen for," she said. "That's different." She wiped her face with her wrist. It made no difference. "Last May I went into Haven's wall to find Soria. My sister. She answered before I'd finished asking."

"The rock answers in the same second," Kaelin said.

"She asked me to build the bridge. So I did." Elena's hands found each other in her lap, and this time she let them. "The night we cut it, on this roof, I told it to let go of her. Out loud. It had talked all night. Then nothing."

Below them the gutters were spilling onto the steps, and children were shouting somewhere out of sight. Kaelin watched the water because he couldn't look at her.

"It used Petra on me," he said. "Then it used Aur'k. At the edge, holding, his voice went warm and started saying we. He'd given me a rule an hour before. If I ask you to wait, it isn't me." He breathed out. "It asked me to wait."

"It wears whoever you'd stop for," Elena said.

The rain went on most of an hour, and they didn't move out of it. When it eased it drew off down the valley, the roofs began to steam, and the light came back off the reservoir white. Kaelin reached over and shut the node's lid for her. Under the overhang it had stayed dry, and its light came green.

"I went down under the Battery," Elena said. "I've seen the floor. Four chairs under clocks. I asked what it runs on. Iverson's answer was Ask me in August." She looked at him. "What does it run on?"

There it was.

He took the handheld out of his jacket. The thread to the flat had one new line in it, come in on the train before the lace went, and he read it again though he knew it. Tell her thank you. For the time.

He had brought the answer here in his chest like a stone. Most days since November Aur'k had gone down under the Battery and come home and said it was a good day, and the one thing he'd given Kaelin about the Gate, he'd given on a beach, as a man gives his word.

"He works there," Kaelin said. "It's his. He's never been good at anything before that didn't belong to somebody else. If I tell you, I'm working against him."

"Yes," Elena said. "And before you do. Iverson has a pod keeping my pace in New York. You came here on the vac in your own name. Whatever you say now, you'll have said it to me."

She didn't soften it. He let the stick go still between his knees.

He counted the panels. Twelve. The painted circles on the roofs below, as far as the tank on the corner. Nine. It didn't help, and he'd known it wouldn't. Petra, twelve breaths to the minute and nobody home. The voice in Aur'k's mouth asking for one more minute.

He put the handheld face down on the crate beside him.

"The Spire line," he said.

Elena didn't move at all.

"Mara posted me its register. Off seal since November, in service, in the civic director's name. And Aur'k's four chairs, with a reader at the head of the row."

"Bellamy's line," Elena said. "I drew half of it." Her voice hadn't changed. Her knuckles had. "My sister died on it. They kept her on it six hours as a key. Now there's a floor of people in it every day, under clocks, and they cheer."

She looked down at the valley for a while. "They didn't build another one," she said. "They took mine out of its vault, and put it on the end I cut."

"Aur'k told me nothing of theirs touches the rock," Kaelin said. "Nothing of ours touches the rock. Twice."

"It's probably true."

He stared at her.

"We calibrated that line live against Cobalt Basin's boundary rock, in 2089," Elena said. "My sister's six hours went to that rock's band. The line is built to hear it." She stopped, and said it plainer. "It doesn't have to touch it."

He thought of Aur'k on the beach call, still in the kitchen light. Nothing. He'd chosen to believe him, standing in the water himself, and said so with his fist on his chest. The sentence had been true. It had also been laid as carefully as a bag on its own fold, and he hadn't seen the fold.

"What's it for?" he said. "Not what it runs on. What it's for."

Elena looked away, at the reservoir.

"That isn't mine to tell," she said. "I told everything at once, in June. I know what it does when it lands."

He laughed. It came out short and not funny, and she looked surprised.

"That's what he says. Near enough word for word. Under contract."

"Then you've been told the same thing twice, by two people who know," Elena said. "Do with that what you like."

Down the valley the reservoir had gone from white to pewter. A boy was walking along the top of the low street's sandbags with his arms out, testing them.

"I want the floor rotas," she said.

"The what?"

"The Gate's floor. Who sits in which chair, which days, when they go on, when they log off. The band carries what it carries. I can hear traffic. I can't tell which of it is people. With the rota, I can."

"They print it," he said slowly. "On paper, so the floor runs if the net goes down. He likes paper. He brings the month home on the first."

"Will you bring it to me?"

Her voice was smaller this time. Flat paper, a thing a man could carry in his jacket on the vac in his own name. He turned the handheld over on the crate. Tell her thank you. He turned it back.

He thought of the kitchen table in the flat. The salt, and the printout of Dae-jung's board leaning against it, the scratched grid and the washer in the corner. The month's rota folded in half beside it, where anyone could pick it up, because there had never been anyone in that flat who would.

On the crate the handheld buzzed against the wood. Face down, it had found the hall's node and everything sent since the train, coming down the north line behind it. He turned it over.

Pills at six. We're out of salt. I'll get some on the way home. Good day? A.

He looked at it until the screen dimmed by itself.

"He leaves it on the table," Kaelin said. "Under the salt."
