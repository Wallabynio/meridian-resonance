The tether's arrival window had been posted for nine days, and Eddy Okafor had managed, for eight of them, not to look at it more than twice a day.

He had spent the ninth in the Habitat Corridors instead, walking the residential loop three times on the excuse of tracing a relay conduit that did not need tracing, because three weeks on Luna Haven had taught him that the only honest thing to do with a body that would not settle was to give it somewhere to go. The corridors obliged. They always did. The mycorrhizal network ran through the grown composite on both sides of him in slow ribbons of blue-green, brightening a half-second ahead of his footfall and dimming a half-second behind, and Eddy — who had spent thirty-six years under an actual sky, and had not once, in all of them, thought of light as something that could be polite — had not yet stopped noticing it.

He had also not stopped noticing the other thing, which was that almost nobody in these corridors had chosen to be here. Three weeks of building a shared data environment for five research groups across four blocs had meant three weeks of reading arrival manifests, and the manifests said what the corridors were too gracious to: the residential levels were filling with people whose home institutions had recently become unsafe to be clever in. A colony designed for four thousand was carrying nearer six. The living walls kept breathing their patient light over all of it, beautiful and indifferent, and Eddy had learned to hold both facts at once without needing them reconciled.

Tether Reception's inner door cycled at 09:14, four minutes late, which on Luna Haven counted as a scandal.

Xiàng Fēi came through it with one bag and the particular stiffness of a man who had spent the transit refusing to sleep in front of strangers.

Eddy had rehearsed nothing, which turned out to be a mercy, because everything he might have rehearsed would have been wrong. He did not say welcome. He did not say you made it, which was obvious, or are you all right, which was not a question either of them had ever had a use for. He crossed the six metres of reception floor and stopped inside arm's length and looked at the man properly for the first time since Neo Singapore.

Xiàng looked back. Then he set the bag down on the composite, and the sound it made was very small.

"Your hands are clean," Eddy said.

Xiàng turned them over, palms up, as though he had not thought to check. The pale seam of chalk that had lived in the creases of his fingers for as long as either of them had known the other was gone — worn out somewhere across two continents and eleven days of processing, leaving a physicist's hands with nothing on them at all.

"Six years," Xiàng said. "I did not expect that to be the part that got to me."

"It's always the part that gets to you." Eddy picked up the bag before Xiàng could reach for it, and did not make anything of the fact that Xiàng let him. "The briefing team wants you at fourteen hundred. I told them they could have you at sixteen."

"You didn't need to do that."

"I know." Eddy started walking, and Xiàng came with him, and the corridor lit ahead of the two of them the way it lit ahead of anybody. "I did it anyway. There's a thing I want to show you first, and I'd rather show you before six other people put their questions in front of it, because once they do it stops being ours and starts being the alliance's, and I've been holding it for three weeks specifically so that it could be ours for four hours."

They went down. The residential loop gave onto a service stair rather than a lift — Eddy had learned the stair in his first week and used it ever since, because a lift was a box that told you nothing and a stair let you feel the colony change temperature around you — and the walls went from the warm domestic teal of the habitat levels toward the cooler, denser glow of the research strata, the network growing thicker and quieter the deeper it ran. Somewhere above them a maintenance chime sounded twice and stopped.

"Léa asked whether you would want a room near the Levels or near the habitats," Eddy said. "I said the Levels. If that was presumptuous, it's a form and I can change it."

"It wasn't presumptuous." Two flights of silence. Then, in the flat unadjectived register Xiàng used for the things that cost him most: "I have not had to decide where to sleep since I was twenty-four. I would rather be asked slowly."

Eddy said nothing to that, which he judged to be the correct amount, and they went on down.

At the fourth landing Xiàng stopped and pulled a folder out of the top of the bag Eddy was carrying — paper, of course; six years of correspondence and a defection had not made the man trust a network he had not personally audited — and held it a moment without opening it.

"I did these at the Sydney Hub," he said. "Ten days of provisional refuge with no laboratory access and nothing else to do with my hands. They will be full of errors. I could not check them against anything."

Eddy took the folder out of his hands.

He did it without asking, the way he had once turned a notebook around on a mezzanine table in Neo Singapore, and he watched Xiàng register that it had happened and decide, visibly, not to object. Then Eddy tucked the folder under his arm, shouldered the door at the bottom of the stair, and let the Meridian Levels open in front of them both — the long instrumented dark of the deep research facility, its consoles standing in their own pooled light like lamps along a road, the place where every important thing in either of their lives had been pointing since a fringe pattern in Siberia had refused to behave.

"Then let's check them against something," he said.

The display Eddy chose was the oldest one on the floor — a wide flat table-format panel in a side bay, three generations behind the deep-interface rigs and therefore, Xiàng understood immediately, the only one nobody would come and want back.

Eddy laid the paper out on it. Then he brought up the map.

Xiàng had not seen it whole since the night it was assembled. He had built half of it and had never once held the whole; the assembled version had lived on Lunar Accord infrastructure since the night Eddy routed it out of the Gardens Relay, and Xiàng's copies had always been fragments, printed, memorised, burned. Now it stood in front of him at full scale — his own interference-fringe rotations from the Resonance Laboratory in one layer, Eddy's accumulated open-science anomalies in another, Sydney Hub atmospheric records in a third, and beneath all of them the thing the three of them had converged on when neither man had a name for it.

It had a name now. It had a research programme, a body of hard-bought rules about how it would and would not be approached, and two navies moving toward it.

"Before we start," Eddy said, "I want to try something, and I want to tell you it's a work protocol so that it stays one."

Xiàng looked at him.

"Jīn does it before every deep-interface session. Two minutes, no instruments, nobody talking." Eddy pulled the second stool across and sat. "You already know the part that makes it not nonsense. The thing responds to the state of the attention pointed at it. Not to the equipment, not to the command — to the attention. Nobody down here can make it behave any other way, and after a while you stop trying." He set his hands flat on the table. "What Léa found is that it carries over onto the people. Two of us who settle to the same state before a joint reading agree with each other more, and — this is the part I couldn't argue with — agree with the instrument more. Jīn calls it attunement. I called it nonsense for about a week."

"And then?"

"And then I ran it against my own error rate." Eddy's mouth did the thing that was not quite a smile. "I don't have to like a result to keep it."

So they sat, two minutes, in the pooled light of an obsolete display, doing nothing. Xiàng found that he was very bad at it. His mind went where it had been going for eleven days — a parameter file, a fence line stopping at a shoreline, a pale rectangle burned into a clay pan — and he did not chase any of it away, because Jīn's protocol apparently did not require that, and after some interval he could not measure he became aware of the other man breathing at a rate that was not his own and then, gradually, was.

"All right," Eddy said.

They worked.

It took forty minutes to get Xiàng's paper calculations into the frame, another hour to reconcile his Baikal node indices against the alliance's — the two catalogues had diverged in eleven places since the map was built and every divergence had to be argued out by hand — and by the third hour they had what neither of them had possessed before: the map as the two of them had first built it, and the map as it stood tonight, laid over each other, node for node, then against now.

Xiàng saw it before Eddy did, and wished, immediately and permanently, that he had not.

"Sixteen," he said.

"Sixteen what?"

"Sixteen nodes that were clean." He put a finger on the panel, which he had not done since he was a graduate student, and dragged a selection across the northern arc of the map. "These. Every one of them read as coherent in the old set. Baseline. Boring. I remember arguing with you about whether they were even worth carrying." He pulled the current layer up through them. "They are not clean now. They are carrying fragmentation. Jittery, structured, non-random — that signature."

Eddy leaned in. His face did not change, which Xiàng had learned, in a mezzanine corridor in Neo Singapore, meant he had understood entirely.

"Say it plainly," Eddy said. "For the record, and because I don't want either of us to get there by wanting to."

"Plainly." Xiàng put his hands flat on the table's edge and made himself do it the way he had done everything at the bench: without ornament, claiming nothing an instrument would not support. "The hollow-empathy signature is present in sixteen nodes that did not carry it in the old set. It is present at low amplitude. It is not doing anything yet. And it is not distributed the way contamination from a point source would be distributed — it is not a plume and it is not a diffusion front."

"Then what is it distributed like?"

"Like a route."

Eddy sat back. Then he reached past Xiàng, pulled the console's input toward himself, and began entering coordinates from memory — a long string of them, without reference to any file, which told Xiàng exactly how many times the man had read the thing he was reciting.

"The Baikal summary," Eddy said. "Somebody inside routed it to me at the Hub during the emergency week and I have never asked who. Militarisation orders, technical annex still attached, because whoever assembled the packet didn't imagine anyone outside the programme would ever read it." A ninth coordinate. A tenth. "That's the Coalition's rift-test corridor. Every site they've opened a controlled aperture at since the tests began, in order."

The corridor drew itself across the map in a thin bright line.

It ran through eleven of the sixteen.

"The other five," Xiàng said, and his voice had gone somewhere far back in his throat, "will be Corporate Union."

They were. It took Eddy nine minutes to pull the Corporate Union set out of what Elena had brought with her through three transit jurisdictions, and when the second line drew itself it did not touch the first anywhere — two navies, two doctrines, two entirely separate programmes of secrecy — and between them they accounted for every contaminated node on the map and not one node that was clean.

Nobody said anything for a while. The Levels hummed around them the way the Levels always hummed.

"It isn't the weapons," Xiàng said at last. "That's the part I need you to hear, because it is not what anyone upstairs is going to assume when we tell them. The Coalition is building a weapon out of hollow-empathy templates and it is monstrous and it is not what this map is showing. This is the aperture work. This is what happens to a node when you force it open for a technical objective and give it nothing to synchronise with." He turned his hand over, palm up, an old gesture from an old whiteboard. "Force it and it destabilises. That is the first thing this physics teaches anyone who puts an instrument into it — you cannot compel the thing to open, you can only be in a state it will open to. Every one of us has read that as an operational limit. A nuisance. The reason their tests keep failing." He shook his head, once, slowly. "It is not a limit. It is damage. A node comes back out of a forced opening fragmented — and a fragmented node is precisely the substrate the parasite is best at."

"They think they're failing," Eddy said slowly.

"They think they are failing. Every test that destabilises, they log as an engineering problem and try again with more power." Xiàng looked at the two bright lines crossing a map he had helped build in a corridor in Neo Singapore when both of them were still able to pretend this was curiosity. "They are not failing. They are preparing ground. Two mutually hostile military programmes have spent the better part of a year clearing a road for something neither of them has been told exists, and the more successfully they hate each other, the faster the road gets built."

Eddy put both hands over his face for a moment, then took them away.

"Right," he said. "Then we write it up tonight, we put it into the shared environment where all five groups get it at the same second, and nobody gets to sit on it for a strategic advantage." He was already reaching for a clean file. "How long since you ate?"

"Sydney."

"Sydney was two days ago."

"Yes," Xiàng agreed, and discovered, with something that was not quite surprise, that he had said it lightly — that he had let the man beside him see a small ordinary failure of self-management without first calculating what a file note might do with it, for the first time since he was twenty-four years old. The instrument of that habit had been the Sovereign Coalition, and the Sovereign Coalition was three hundred and eighty thousand kilometres below them, and did not have him.

"Four hours," Eddy said. "Then food. Then the briefing team, who can have whatever's left."

The Verge was the only place on the whole freight chokepoint where a compliance officer could stand and not be inside somebody's camera arc, which was why Amita Rao slipped through the lock door and out into the gallery twice a shift and had done for as long as she had worked the compliance floor, and which was not the reason she gave when anyone asked. The reason she gave was the heat. It was also true, if backwards from how it sounded. Logistics Command ran its floor at a temperature calibrated for servers rather than staff, and the interstitial gallery between the dome shell and the lock wall ran close and unconditioned and thick with a humidity nothing in climate control ever let through, and salt had been eating the handrail since before she was born.

She leaned her forearms on it — the rail's corrosion left orange on her sleeves, which was why the vest was the vest and not a good one — and read the week's routing summary in the gallery's dim, filtered light, the dome shell overhead turning the Gulf's glare into something she could actually look at, and found the thing she had been failing to find a name for since Tuesday.

It was not a shipment. Amita had spent her whole career learning that the interesting thing is almost never a shipment. A shipment can be inspected, declared, mis-declared, seized. What cannot be inspected is a shape.

The shape was in the transfer nodes.

The Gulf Crescent moved freight through nineteen consolidation nodes, and the nineteen had personalities the way people did; she could have drawn their monthly volumes freehand and been within four per cent. Since the state of emergency, six of them had stopped behaving like themselves. Not surging — that would have been ordinary, that would have been war, and war made freight noisy in ways she knew how to read. These six had gone quiet in one direction and heavy in the other. Backhauls that did not balance. Empty containers returning from sites that had received full ones, week on week, with nothing coming back out and nothing declared as consumed.

Something was going somewhere and not coming back, and it was going to six places she could not find an industry for.

She pulled the destination set. High-capacity power conditioning. Cryogenic handling gear. Shielding mass, in tonnages that made no sense for a research station and every kind of sense for something that occasionally needed to be stood behind. Two of the six sites had no declared function at all beyond a survey designation. One was a decommissioned desalination plant that had been consuming more grid capacity dead than it ever had alive.

Amita did not know what any of it meant. She wrote that down first, in the same dry hand she had used for two hundred and eighty-three manifest numbers, because a document that claims more than its author knows is a document a tribunal throws out: Analyst has no visibility of end use. Findings are routing-pattern anomalies only. Then she wrote down what she did know, which was the whole of it — the six nodes, the imbalance figures week by week, the manifest numbers, the declared tonnages, the names of the compliance officers who had signed off on each, including hers.

It took her most of the afternoon. It would have taken anyone else a morning; Amita took longer on purpose, checking each line against the audit protocol the way she had once checked a diagnostic subheader against every rule Logistics Command ran, because the only weapon a port officer had ever had was a paper trail too meticulous to argue with, and a weapon you have not maintained is a weapon that fails in the hand.

At the end of it she packaged the whole set for the research channel — the one that came back to her through the Lunar Accord humanitarian route, a year and a half old now, still holding — and attached one line of covering note, because she had learned that scientists ignore an analysis and read a question.

Six nodes deformed since the emergency declaration. Freight going in, nothing coming out, nothing declared consumed. I don't know what these sites are. If any of your people know what these coordinates are for, they belong to you and not to me.

She sent it, and stood a minute longer in the gallery while condensation beaded along the lock wall beside her and, through the dome shell's angled glazing, the gantries threw their long rusted verticals across the container stacks and the water went the colour of the sky, and thought — without drama, the way she thought about most things — that she had just spent an afternoon of her life describing the shape of a hole, and that somewhere, presumably, there was a person who would know what belonged in it.

Then she went back through the lock door onto the compliance floor, where it was cold and dry and every camera could see her again, and cleared the rest of her queue before the shift ended, because forty tons of desalination parts were still bound for the Mediterranean Zone and somebody had to sign for them.
