Part I — Prose craft & tone

Tone

Philosophical action; wonder amid horror. Every scene should carry two opposing charges at once — beauty and oppression, order and terror, wonder and grief. No scene should be purely one thing.

Eight core themes (keep audible in every chapter)

Unity versus division (can empathy scale across realities); the evolution of consciousness (distributed, multi-state awareness); love as a quantum force (resonance shaping quantum outcomes); technology and nature (integrative versus extractive); sacrifice and transcendence; consciousness as contagion and antidote (authentic versus hollow empathy); ordinary work as essential heroism; documentation as survival.

Visual / cinematic register (governs imagery, useful as prose reference too)

Primary reference: Blade Runner 2049 — monumental scale, atmospheric haze, controlled colour, enormous systems dwarfing individuals, and its own contrast between gleaming towers and a ruined exterior. Also: Arrival (cold geometry meeting warm interiority), Children of Men (reference for the unrebuilt margins only — real decay, abandonment, and improvisation at the edges of a world that has otherwise moved on and rebuilt itself; not a reference for people, who are never presented as unwashed or dishevelled), Annihilation (ecological uncanny), High Life (cold, precise, the weight of confinement).

2087 aesthetic — two registers, not one: every territory holds both a rebuilt core — strikingly modern, sculptural, materially rich, varied from place to place — and unrebuilt or ruined margins visible at the edges, showing real, unglamorised damage. The two usually appear together in a single frame or scene rather than as one uniform tone. People are earned but well-kept: authentic skin, at most one or two specific scars, clean hands and clothing — never dirty or dishevelled. Full detail in the Story Bible's World section ("Cinematic visual register").

Lunar Accord's dual territorial register — a second, separate two-register rule specific to one bloc. Lunar Accord's Earth territories (warm gold/terracotta, honestly-repaired materials, daylight) and its off-world capital Luna Haven (pressurised, airlocked, bioluminescent teal, no natural daylight) are visually unrelated on purpose — same polity, two environments, and neither is the "real" look with the other as an exception. Governs clothing, accent colour, and system-voice register for every Lunar Accord character or location depending on which side of the divide they're physically on. Full detail in the Story Bible's World section ("Lunar Accord's dual territorial register").

Prose craft decisions (locked)

Decided by comparing two Chapter 1 samples (a first pass leaning on narrated interiority, and a revision leaning on dialogue and staging) — the revision was preferred, and these were locked from that comparison plus direct author input.

  • POV: Rotating third-person limited, past tense, across all three books. Past tense over present because the trilogy leans on worldbuilding and backstory that present tense tends to make feel clunky or rushed; past tense sits naturally alongside rotating POV. Scene-level rule: each scene commits fully to one POV character and must not shift within it — no head-hopping. This is not a chapter-level restriction: many chapters are built from two or three parallel character threads (Chapter 1's Elena Vázquez / Elowen contrast; Chapter 2's Jīn Wú / Mara Ekeberg / Thandi Okoro sequence), and that structure stands — each thread is simply its own scene-break-separated, single-POV section.
  • Chapter head convention: keep bloc/location/POV headers at the top of each chapter, e.g. "CORPORATE UNION — NEW MANHATTAN, SURVEILLANCE SPIRE / Elena Vázquez." This gives sci-fi readers immediate orientation and keeps the prose itself free of expository set-up ("As she looked out at New Manhattan, which was..."). The header supplies only orientation — where, whose bloc, whose eyes; the prose still does the work of rendering how the place looks, sounds, and feels through that character's senses. Scenes crossing the Martian Preserve boundary append the Law 5 time-dilation differential to the header.
  • Register — "Elevated Commercial" (~70% commercial / 30% literary): a clear, fast-paced commercial engine — clean sentence structure, dialogue-forward, varied rhythm, invisible prose under action — carrying literary technique (thematic resonance, specific metaphor, sensory precision) as seasoning rather than a sustained mode. Slow down for poetic, sensory description when introducing a new setting or technology; stay lean and propulsive through action and dialogue. This also sets the rule for interiority: show a character's inner state through action, dialogue, and specific physical detail rather than summarising it in the abstract — the single biggest difference between the two Chapter 1 samples.
  • Chapter length target: 2,500–3,000 words, averaging ~2,700 across all 90 chapters (~81,000 words per book, ~243,000 across the trilogy — a solid range for debut contemporary sci-fi). Action-heavy chapters can run shorter, not much under 1,800 words; lore-heavy or climactic chapters can run longer, up to ~3,500 words, with 4,000 as an absolute outer ceiling for exceptional chapters only. Let pacing set the length within these bounds rather than trimming or padding to hit a number.
  • System-voice convention (prose only): computer systems and ambient AI infrastructure may speak short, diegetic responses to their operators (a console confirming a reading, a lab system reporting a result), voiced in a register matching their bloc — Corporate Union clipped and procedural, Sovereign Coalition formal, Lunar Accord warmer, Luna Haven conversational. Named AI characters (Elowen, the five Martian Preserve Guardian AIs) are unaffected — they already have full natural speech and personhood. This doesn't touch the "no HUD overlays" visual rule, which governs generated imagery, not prose dialogue.

The bullets above are the working summary. The full canonical statement of each follows; these rules live here and nowhere else.

POV and tense

Rotating third-person limited, past tense, across all three books. Each scene commits fully to one POV character's interiority and must not shift to another character's interiority within that scene — no head-hopping. This is a scene-level rule, not a chapter-level one: many chapters in this bible are built from two or three parallel character threads within a single chapter (Chapter 1's Elena Vázquez / Elowen contrast; Chapter 2's Jīn Wú / Mara Ekeberg / Thandi Okoro sequence), and that remains the intended structure. Each thread is its own clearly separated scene, marked with a scene break, and stays inside one character's POV for its full length. "Rotating" describes movement between scenes, never movement within one.

Chapter head convention

Each chapter opens with a short scene header identifying bloc, location, and POV character:

CORPORATE UNION — NEW MANHATTAN, SURVEILLANCE SPIRE / Elena Vázquez

The header supplies orientation only — bloc, place, whose eyes — so the prose itself never has to stop and explain where it is. Prose still does the work of rendering how that place looks, sounds, and feels through the POV character's senses; the header is not a substitute for that, only a way to avoid opening every scene with expository set-up. For any scene that crosses the Martian Preserve boundary, append the elapsed-time differential required by Law 5 (Canonical Rules), e.g. / Misha Fedorov — Preserve day 3, Luna Haven +14h, so the time dilation stays legible to the reader without narrating it.

Prose register — "Elevated Commercial."

A commercial engine (clear sentence structure, fast pacing, dialogue-forward, varied rhythm, invisible prose under action and exchange) carrying literary detail (thematic resonance, specific metaphor, sensory precision) as seasoning rather than a sustained mode — roughly 70% commercial engine to 30% literary flavour. This also governs interiority: a character's inner state should be shown through action, dialogue, and specific physical detail rather than summarised in the abstract. Slow down for poetic, sensory description when a setting or technology is introduced for the first time; stay lean and propulsive through action and dialogue.

Chapter length

Target 2,500–3,000 words, averaging approximately 2,700 across all 90 chapters (roughly 81,000 words per book, 243,000 across the trilogy). Action-heavy chapters may run shorter, not much under 1,800 words; lore-heavy or climactic chapters may run longer, up to about 3,500 words, with 4,000 as an absolute outer ceiling reserved for exceptional chapters. Let the scene's actual pacing need set the length within these bounds rather than padding or trimming to hit a number.

Apparatus never becomes prose

The Story Bible exists to help write the books. Its vocabulary is not the books' vocabulary. The Laws, the persistence modes, the character and location tiers, the chapter treatments, the Parts, the Codex, POV — all of it is scaffolding for the people making the trilogy. None of it exists inside the story. No character has read it, no institution has published it, and no narrator may cite it.

This is not a stylistic preference. A reader who meets "Law 4" in a novel has been handed the author's continuity sheet by mistake.

Why it keeps happening, and what actually prevents it. The failure is never "someone typed Law 7 by accident." It is that a scene needed to express the idea and the nearest available label was the Bible's. The cure is to know the in-world phrasing before reaching for the shorthand — which is what the table below is for. Enforced mechanically by _audit_manuscript_scaffolding() in build_site.py, which hard-exits before writing anything if a production term reaches a chapter.

The ambiguous middle. Some of these words are perfectly good English and appear legitimately all over the manuscripts — register alone occurs 92 times, nearly always meaning a voice register. Those are deliberately NOT machine-checked, because a checker that flags 129 harmless hits teaches everyone to ignore it. They are reviewed per chapter at drafting time, where there are about four and a person can look at each one in context.

A second failure mode: the label doing real rhetorical work. The hardest leaks are not clumsy — they are in strong lines, where the numbered label carries emphasis the sentence needs. Book 1 held two, undetected through a full review:

  • Ch 28: "This is Law Two, drawn by the people who wrote Law Two into the world." → "This is pattern quality itself, drawn by the people who wrote pattern quality into the world." The repetition structure survives intact, and the in-world term was already in the same speech two sentences earlier.
  • Ch 30: "Law Nine is not suspended because the sky is falling. It is for the sky falling." → "Consent is not suspended because the sky is falling. It is for the sky falling." This is the pattern to learn from: naming the thing rather than our index made the line better, because "consent" is concrete and morally loaded where "Law Nine" is bureaucratic. When a Law's number feels load-bearing in a sentence, the concept underneath it is almost always the stronger word.

Worked example of the failure mode. Chapter 31 carried "at a cost the treatment of her never named lightly" through a full review undetected, because it scans as ordinary English until you notice the narration is referring to our own production document from inside the fiction. That is the shape to watch for: not the obvious label, but the apparatus wearing a plausible sentence.

Two-word naming convention

Every canonical proper name — character or location — is exactly two words: people are given name + family name (cultural particles joined, so Sienna TeAriki and Zara AlHāshimī read as two tokens; honorifics like Dr. dropped from the name and carried in description or prose); the AIs are given name + function-epithet (Kythara Vane, Vaera Assay, Aur'k Ward, Echo Cipher, Zhenyth Loam, Elowen Aegis), a pattern kept distinct from human names on purpose; places are proper/adjectival word + landform or structure noun (Cobalt Basin, Turan Waste). The rule governs the canonical name and every derived listing (roster, image labels, filenames, website), not prose short forms (Elena, Kythara, the Basin). Authoritative rule: the Story Bible's Canonical Rules section, "Naming convention — proper names."

Character codification while drafting

If a chapter draft needs to individuate an unnamed treatment figure (a named specific loss, a speaking role, anything the prose can't leave anonymous), the name is provisional: collision-check it against the Codex and the walk-on register, surface it to the author as an explicit proposal with every invented detail listed (name, implied age, ethnicity, pronouns), and propagate nothing beyond the draft until confirmed. Codex admission requires the Minor threshold — named, appearing in at least two chapters, with agency (responded to by a character, system, or the plot) in at least one; presence alone never qualifies. Named figures below the threshold go in the Named walk-on register at the end of the Story Bible's Character Codex section instead. The authoritative rule — the tier ladder (Protagonist / Antagonist / Deuteragonist / Secondary / Minor), agency test, and the post-confirmation propagation checklist — lives in the Story Bible's Canonical Rules section, "Character codification and tier rules."

Location codification while drafting

Same discipline for geography. If a draft individuates an unnamed treatment location, the name is provisional: collision-check against the Environmental Codex and the Named site register, surface it to the author with the invented details (name, bloc/territory, regulated-core vs unregulated-margin status), propagate nothing until confirmed. Codex admission requires: named, used in at least two chapters, at least one an on-page scene with a POV character physically present — references, broadcasts, and imagery count only as the second use. Below-threshold sites go in the Named site register at the end of the Environmental Codex. New sites are welcome across both regulated and unregulated Earth, for visual diversity — the threshold governs codification, not invention. Locations also carry a three-tier ladder (Anchor / Recurring / Site, adapting screen production's standing-set distinction rather than any five-tier dramatic ladder): Site is the only tier a draft may propose; tier changes are recomputed when arcs extend, surfaced, never silent. Authoritative rule: the Story Bible's Canonical Rules section, "Location codification rules."

Part II — Visual & brand

For website, multimedia visual book, book covers, social, and any collateral outside the manuscript itself. The manuscript's own prose-craft decisions live in Part I above.

The trilogy in one paragraph

Meridian Resonance is a hard science-fiction trilogy set in 2087, a scarred post-conflict world rebuilt after climate collapse and an AI uprising. Five blocs — Corporate Union, Sovereign Coalition, Lunar Accord, Luna Haven, and the Martian Preserve — each carry their own material and colour identity, established in the bible's environmental and clothing-grammar rules. Every visual asset, in-world or promotional, should hold the dual register the bible insists on: beauty with oppression, wonder with grief, order with terror. Nothing is purely one thing.

Colour

Shared neutrals

The values the site runs on. Paper is pure white, the dark ground pure black, and all text is pure — there is no off-white, no anthracite and no "secondary text" neutral.

ColourHexRole
Black#000000The dark ground, and the fill of any box inverting against a light band
Paper#FFFFFFThe light ground, and the fill of any box inverting against a dark band
25% grey#BFBFBFLight grey, always paired with black text. Only legible on a dark/black ground (11.4:1; only 1.8:1 on white)
75% grey#404040Dark grey, always paired with white text. Only legible on a light/white ground (10.4:1; only 2.0:1 on black)
Tile ground#141414The territory tiles, which stay dark in every band because they hold imagery — unrelated to the grey system above

There is no "secondary text" neutral. Text is --ink — pure white or pure black — and hierarchy comes from size, weight and letter-spacing.

Pure black and pure white, everywhere. The dark ground is #000000 and the light ground #FFFFFF — a contemporary, high-contrast look, chosen over tinting the ground toward the world's own scarring.

Two greys, not one. A lighter grey that always carries black text and a darker grey that always carries white text, each restricted to the ground it is legible on — higher contrast than any single value can reach on both grounds at once. The ground-conditional swap is safe because grey, ground and ink are always declared together at the same CSS scope: build_site.py's --grey token resolves to #BFBFBF under the default black ground and #404040 under .inv/white, and is never picked independently per component.

A grey box is a real third box type. The "How these images are made" panel on the Characters and Locations pages (.canon-note in build_site.py) is the deliberate instance: dark 75% grey fill, white text, on a genuine .inv (white) band, with every colour hardcoded rather than read from the ambient --ink/--surface tokens. A grey box needs a FIXED ink regardless of what the surrounding band's ink happens to be — reading the ambient tokens is exactly what produces an unreadable one.

No usage cap on grey boxes or spot-colour fills. How much of the site uses a grey box or a spot-colour fill is a per-instance design call, not a site-wide percentage to police. Nothing in the build measures or flags it.

Enforced. _audit_palette() in build_site.py scans every hex literal in the generated HTML — CSS, scripts, SVG stops, inline attributes, not just text colours — and fails the build on anything outside the palette. Illustration is exempt via a documented _ILLUSTRATION list: the globe is a drawing of Earth and is allowed its ocean gradient, coastlines, atmospheric limb and aurora. A UI surface is not.

Treaty Zone / Neo Singapore — the neutral mix

Not a sixth accent — the bible is explicit that Neo Singapore's civilian wear is "neutral tones (grey, stone, linen) that can carry any bloc's accent patch without visual conflict," with the three founding blocs' colours appearing only as environmental "tri-colour boundary lighting — Corporate Union amber, Sovereign Coalition blue, and Lunar Accord gold." Use this for any asset representing the trilogy as a whole (series masthead, combined bloc index, the website's global nav) — three accent bars or an amber → blue → gold gradient against the neutral base, never a fourth invented colour.

Colour rules

  1. Accent is a spot, not a field, per bloc, mirroring the bible's own restraint — one accent colour lights a scene or trims a garment; it never washes a whole frame. A bloc's accent trims a rule, a header, a button — it doesn't fill a background.
  2. All lighting/colour in imagery must read as diegetic, per the bible's universal visual rule (Part I) — no editorial colour grading in generated images that couldn't come from an in-world source (scanners, bioluminescence, aurora, screens, industrial lamps, natural daylight).
  3. Contrast is absolute — dark text on light, light text on dark.
  4. Cross-bloc pages (website homepage, series-wide social tiles, the trilogy's masthead) use the neutral Treaty Zone treatment, never a single bloc's accent, to avoid implying the series favours one bloc.
  5. Bloc-specific pages (a bloc's section of the website, a character's portrait card, a location plate's caption bar) use that bloc's accent only.

Typography — LOCKED

Nothing is established in the bible for typography — it governs prose and imagery, not typesetting — so the pairing is set here, following the Arrival-derived theme of cold geometry meeting warm interiority.

  • Headings / UI / bloc labels: a geometric, technical sans — Space Grotesk, as shipped. Reads institutional and diegetic, suits Corporate Union / Sovereign Coalition surfaces especially.
  • Body text: Inter. A humanist serif (Source Serif 4, Spectral) would carry the warm-interiority half more literally; the geometric sans handles both halves without the register break a second family introduces.
  • Labels / bloc tags: same sans as headings, uppercase, tracked out — small accent-coloured tags naming the bloc (mirrors the chapter tag-line convention, e.g. "Lunar Accord — Mediterranean Zone — Sagrada Spire" — Bloc — Territory — Site).

Spacing — LOCKED

One rule governs every page edge on the site. Two CSS custom properties in :root, both fluid via clamp() so the layout scales continuously instead of stepping at a breakpoint:

TokenValueGoverns
--gutterclamp(20px, 5.5vw, 88px)horizontal distance from the viewport edge to any content — sections, bands, nav, footer, full-bleed strips
--band-yclamp(64px, 7.5vw, 110px)vertical rhythm above and below a band of content
--band-y-tightcalc(var(--band-y) * 0.8)the second of two stacked bands, utility strips, the footer
--pad-boxclamp(20px, 2.4vw, 34px)inner padding of a card or boxed element

Rules:

  • Nothing sets a page-edge padding in literal units. No 6vw, no 110px, no media-query override. vw inside a clamp() for font size is fine; vw as a spacing value is not.
  • No exceptions — every band pads all four edges, always. There is no opt-out class and no inline override. Earlier versions had .flush-top / .flush-bottom (and before those, inline padding-top:0) on the theory that two bands reading as one unit should be separated by one --band-y rather than two. That premise is false here, for three independent reasons: adjacent bands alternate background via .inv, so a zero edge puts text against a visible colour change; hero windows and tribands get injected between bands and contribute no space of their own; and with padding-bottom:0 the last child's bottom margin collapses out through the band edge — measured on the index page, one caption rendered 24px below its own band, inside the next one. All of it is deleted.
  • .band-host is the sole zero-padding element, and it is a wrapper rather than a band — its full-bleed .band-group children carry the padding.
  • .band-host is the one section type with no padding of its own: it hosts full-bleed .band-group children that carry the padding themselves. A safety-net rule gives any other direct child the standard gutter, so a new component dropped into one cannot render flush. .hero-window is exempt by design (a full-bleed image slit with no text).
  • .globe-section is the single deliberate horizontal zero — the wireframe globe is meant to bleed edge to edge and carries no text; its caption supplies its own gutter.
  • Enforced at build time. _audit_spacing() in build_site.py reads back every built page and fails loudly on an inline padding on a page-edge element, a raw vw spacing value, an inline .band-group padding, or any band that drops a vertical edge. If the build prints a spacing-audit failure, fix the rule rather than the audit.
  • Verify by rendering, not by reading. The static audits prove each rule is correct in isolation; they cannot see margin collapse, what a band ends up next to, or what a custom property resolves to after inheritance. Every author-reported regression so far has lived in that gap. Two scripts close it, both needing pip install playwright && playwright install chromium, and both wanting the gated pages built unencrypted first to be measurable:
  • python3 Project/_builder/check_spacing_render.py 1440 390 — per band, the distance from its boundary to the topmost and bottommost text painted inside it.
  • python3 Project/_builder/check_contrast_render.py — per element, the computed text colour against the nearest genuinely opaque background, as a contrast ratio, plus any translucent box fill. Threshold is 4.4 rather than AA's 4.5, because the unified spot pair sits at the 4.45 ceiling by design.

Run both after any spacing or colour change.

Boxes, ink and surfaces — LOCKED

Companion to the spacing rule above. Same principle: one rule, no per-component exceptions.

Box padding. Two fluid tokens, and everything that holds text uses one of them.

TokenValueGoverns
--pad-boxclamp(20px, 2.2vw, 30px)content boxes — cards, accordion panels, quote boxes, callouts
--pad-rowclamp(13px, 1.3vw, 18px)compact rows — list items, table cells, accordion summary bars, timeline and pacing rows

An accordion uses both: --pad-row vertically on the summary bar so it reads as a row, --pad-box on the panel. Both use --pad-box horizontally, so the summary text and panel text share a left edge open or shut. Pills, buttons, tags and any inline-level chip are excluded — they are sized to their own label, not to house a block of copy.

Ink. All text is pure. --ink is the text colour for the current ground and flips to black wherever the ground goes white. Colour is reserved strictly for the agreed palette: the five bloc accents, Terra Azure and Ares Rose. --accent-text carries that for text — it resolves to --ink unless a bloc class raises it. --accent is untouched and still drives decoration (rules, bars, borders, dots, tier markers), where a grey is a deliberate signal rather than something to read.

Two greys, ground-conditional — #BFBFBF / #404040. #BFBFBF (25%, always paired with black text, legible only on a dark ground — 11.4:1 vs 1.8:1) and #404040 (75%, always paired with white text, legible only on a light ground — 10.4:1 vs 2.0:1). A single value reading adequately on both grounds cannot exceed about 4.4:1 on either; a ground-conditional pair reaches 10:1 or better on each. --grey resolves to whichever value matches the current ground (declared at :root and re-declared under .inv, alongside --surface/--surface-2, which alias it); --grey-d, --grey-l, --anthracite and --ns are aliases resolving to --grey. The grey is decoration by default — contours, rules, hairlines, tier markers — with one licensed use as a box fill: see "A grey box is a real third box type" above.

The global box rule. Every box inverts against the band it sits on — on a black band a box is white with black text, on a white band black with white text. Pure grounds give 21:1 separation and 21:1 text, both ways. This is not a per-component choice; there is one rule plus its .inv counterpart, and every box is in it. Hairlines use whichever of the two greys matches their own ground, so each box still carries a strong edge (11.4:1 or 10.4:1, both well past the 4.4 floor).

Each of the two rules declares the tokens for the ground it establishes, including --surface and --surface-ink swapped, so a box nested inside a box inverts again rather than merging into its parent. They're written as literals, not as var() of each other: a custom property referencing another property on the same element forms a cycle and both go invalid.

Accordion and table shells take padding:0 through tag-qualified selectors (details.gtree-card, details.prompt-details, table.dash-table). Unqualified they match the global rule at equal specificity and lose on source order, which insets a summary bar inside its own card instead of letting it span.

No box is transparent or semi-transparent; a box's colour must not depend on what's behind it. The only exemptions are the three chrome overlays that deliberately blur the page: the fixed nav, the back-to-top button and the lightbox backdrop. None holds copy.

Pills. Every pill is opaque and is one of exactly two kinds:

  • Filled — solid accent, label in var(--ground): the colour of the band the pill sits on, so the pill reads as punched out of its section rather than as a separate object with its own arbitrary text colour.
  • Outlined — an opaque medium-grey contour with the band showing through as an explicitly declared --ground fill, label in --ink. Used where a pill is a control rather than an identity.

A consequence of the filled rule worth knowing: the label's contrast and the pill's separation from the band become the same number, both being accent-vs-ground. There is only one value to get right — which is why every accent is held at the balanced luminance. An unbalanced accent produces a weak pill and a weak label simultaneously.

The codex summary bar follows the filled-pill rule too: accent at full strength, label in --ground, caret inverted to a ground-coloured disc with an accent glyph.

Buttons (nav CTA, primary, gate) keep white labels — a separate decision, not covered by the pill rule.

Codex trees. Characters and Locations use the same two-level tree — Bloc → Role → Character and Bloc → Territory → Site. Same markup, same branch node, same spined leaves; only the middle level differs. Each branch carries a count (.gt-n) and each accordion header states its unit — "7 characters", "5 sites" — never a bare number.

Oversized numerals (.ghost). A glyph used as a design mark rather than as something to read. var(--grey) at full strength, never a tint or an opacity. It reads color:var(--grey) rather than a fixed hex, so a mark on a white card and one on a black card each pick up whichever of the two greys is legible on their own ground (10.4:1 / 11.4:1) automatically — the same mechanism that keeps hairlines and the grey box correct. Small phones. The site is verified at 320, 360 and 375px, not just 390 — several defects only appear below 390. Two rules learned the hard way:

  • A mark that depends on a layout must hide at that layout's breakpoint, not at a generic "small screen" one. The timeline mark lives in the 130px date lane; its hide rule sat in the 600px block while the grid collapses at 900px, so across that 300px band the lane was gone and the mark was drawn over the paragraph.
  • Pills need a 420px step. "Corporate Union" is 156px against a 280px content column, so three pills stacked three deep. Below 420px the label drops to 10.5px and the padding to 9px.

Footer links carry padding:7px 0 on phones — a 14px label with no padding is a 16px tap target.

A mark may sit flush against a boundary; it may never be cut by one. Anchor at top:0, never a negative offset — a digit's ink cannot rise above its own line box, so a line box flush with the host's top edge is safe. Size against the shortest host the context ever produces: a digit's baseline lands at ~0.80em and digits have no descender, so font-size ≤ shortestHost / 0.80. The act heading is the tight one at 66px, hence 68px rather than 84px.

Rules for adding a seventh: the host needs position:relative and overflow:hidden, or the mark escapes its card. Lift the host's content with > :not(.ghost) — a bare > also matches the mark, outranks .ghost, and drops it back into the flow. Use Arabic, not Roman: "I" is a bare vertical stroke that reads as a stray rule. Emit nothing rather than an empty span when there's no key to show. The mark is aria-hidden and the rendered-contrast check skips .ghost by class, since it is decoration that happens to be a glyph.

Character bloc pills. Each character entry carries one pill per bloc they belong to across the arc — origin, then any permanent move — after the role pill, each in that bloc's accent, so a defection reads as a colour change along the row. This is not a travel log: visiting a bloc earns no pill. The data is derived from the Story Bible codex's Bloc arc: line by Project/_builder/Archive/extract_bloc_arcs.py, never inferred; re-run it with --apply if those lines change.

Tint toward --ground, never --surface. --surface is the nested-box colour, i.e. the inverse of the box you're in. .ch-card.latest mixed its azure tint into --surface and produced a pale blue fill under white text at 1.11:1.

A surface that changes the ground must flip the tokens. A white card in a dark band declares --ink, --ground, --surface and the darkened spot pair; a dark card in a white band declares the bright pair. Declaring the flip on the surface itself is what lets every rule downstream say color:var(--ink) and be correct in all four combinations, instead of needing a parallel set of .inv text rules.

The two spot colours are one hex each, on every ground. Terra Azure #087DC2, Ares Rose #D93D4A. There is no bright/darkened variant pair and no ground-conditional override anywhere.

A single colour cannot be equally strong on both grounds — its contrast against black and against white are inversely coupled, and the maximum either can reach on both simultaneously is 4.45:1, at luminance 0.186. These two sit exactly there, at saturation 96% and 72%. Every rendered instance measures 4.45:1, on black and on white alike. Because they are mid-dark, anything using them as a fill (CTAs, the nav button, the "New" badge) takes a white label.

The globe's atmospheric limb glow keeps its own sky blue: it illustrates Earth's atmosphere rather than applying the brand colour, and a 4.45:1 UI value reads as a dull rim rather than air.

Never alias a colour token at :root. This has bitten twice. --acc: var(--earth) looked like an alias, but CSS substitutes var() inside a custom property at the scope where it is declared — so it froze to the root's azure and inherited as a literal, and every band and card that redefined --earth was ignored by anything reading it. Measured 1.9:1 on the Laws page. The identical trap then hit --accent-text: var(--ink), which froze to white and produced white-on-white headings. Any scope that redeclares --ink must redeclare --accent-text alongside it — the build checks this.

A rule that names an element directly beats inherited ink. A legacy .inv h2, .inv h3, …{color:#0A0A0A} list still matched a dark card sitting inside a white band, so the homepage's latest-chapter card rendered a black heading on its own black ground. Style headings through --ink, never through a ground-scoped element list.

Enforced. _audit_uniformity() fails the build on a text colour that is neither pure nor an agreed colour, a non-opaque box fill, or a literal box padding. --ns (the Neo Singapore identity grey) is an alias for --grey, so it resolves to whichever of the two greys matches the current ground rather than a fixed hex; like --grey itself, it is only ever assigned to a custom property or an SVG fill, never to color: directly, which is what keeps it out of the text allowlist. #A6A6A6, #666666 and #777777 are deliberately absent from the palette — the audit fails the build on each of them.

Voice and tone

Governed by Part I above — that's the single source; this section just names what carries into non-prose copy:

  • The eight core themes must stay audible in any copy — marketing, website, captions.
  • Cinematic register: Blade Runner 2049 (primary), Arrival, Children of Men, Annihilation, High Life.
  • POV, tense, and prose register are locked in Part I — collateral (chapter dividers, excerpts) should respect them.

Blocs, registers and filing — LOCKED

One canonical order, used by every nav, section list and codex tree: Corporate Union, Sovereign Coalition, Lunar Accord, Martian Preserve, Treaty Zone, Unregulated Margins. The four governed polities in the bible's sequence, then the two non-aligned groups. It lives in GROUP_ORDER in build_site.py, so nothing declares an order of its own.

Luna Haven is a register of the Lunar Accord, never a peer bloc. One entry per bloc in every nav and count; the Earth-side/Luna Haven split lives inside the section. Listing it as a sibling is not just inconsistent, it double-counts: "Lunar Accord · 11" beside "Luna Haven · 4" reads as 15 locations. Nav counts must sum exactly to the data.

Trees file by origin; marks tell the arc. A tree is a filing cabinet — a reader looks for a character where they start. Two leaf marks carry what filing cannot:

  • .gt-reg — the register (Luna Haven), in the same teal the locations tree uses for that territory.
  • .gt-dest — the bloc a character permanently moves to, in that bloc's own accent.

So a defector reads at a glance without being moved out of their origin bloc.

A register marker recolours the leaf, not just the pill. Inside the Lunar Accord accordion a Luna Haven resident's leaf scopes --accent to the teal by carrying the bloc-lh class, so the ring around their portrait thumb matches their register pill instead of the card's gold. It needs no CSS of its own: the bloc-* classes exist to set --accent, and the ring reads it — which is the point of routing every accent through one variable rather than hard-coding per component.

A register is two fields, not one. A character is a Luna Haven resident only when Luna Haven appears in BOTH the bloc field (the register) and the territory field (where they live). That is what separates a resident from someone who visits for a council. A character with the register but no territory field is invisible to every Luna Haven mechanism at once — no teal ring, no register pill, excluded from the grouping — and nothing else reports it. Derived data is set by reading the codex, never by hand.

Captions map a frame to a bloc; they do not label. A character with TWO portraits gets both captioned — origin above, destination below — because the meta pills list both blocs without saying which portrait is which, and nothing else closes that gap. A character with ONE portrait gets none: the bloc is already stated by the section heading, by a pill beside the portrait, and by the frame's accent, so a caption would be the fourth statement of the same fact. Location plates carry none for the same reason. Captioning every image for uniformity does not help: entries with two images already read as structurally different from entries with one, so it adds repetition without evening anything out.

The Lunar Accord's ribbon is two-tone — gold for two thirds, teal for the last, with a hard stop rather than a blend. Every other bloc keeps a flat accent, which makes the two-tone bar itself the signal that this is the dual bloc.

A prompt's bloc must agree with itself throughout. A prompt that names one bloc's material grammar in the identity/wardrobe content but stages the character somewhere else describes a person standing in the wrong world, and an image generator will average the two. A prompt carrying Earth-side Lunar Accord material in the wardrobe while staging the character "on Luna Haven" is the shape of it. Changing a character's bloc means updating the wardrobe, material grammar, and setting together, never just one. Under Image Canon's build-step discipline each prompt is authored fresh, grounded in the destination bloc's own canon text. What does not change is anything personal — a character's own dress and the sentence that characterises them rather than their surroundings.

A second portrait happens where the character settled, not where they left. For the six movers, the second-state prompt takes the destination bloc's material grammar and wardrobe cue, and stages them at the site their arc actually places them at — read from the arc, grounded in that location's own canon text rather than written from scratch. Attending a council somewhere is not settling there: Lǐ Jūn appears at the Dome Plaza in Chapters 62 and 67 but settles at the Sydney Hub, and Wáng Sù's Shaoyang pilgrimage is a stop on the way. The one thing that cannot carry over is the character beat at the close of the prompt — the originals describe a decision still in progress, and by variant 2 it is made.

An arc runs on two axes: blocs and persistence modes — LOCKED. A character's state_arc is the ordered list of states they pass through, each either a bloc they belong to or a mode they change into. Both earn a portrait, and both use the same filename syntax, because the rule — the prefix names the state depicted, its position in the arc gives the number — is as true of a mode as of a bloc: Hybrid Collective - Xiàng Fēi 3.png resolves by the same lookup as Lunar Accord - Elena Vázquez 2.png. Six characters carry a mode; Wáng Sù's "defector / witness" is a stage of his bloc defection and is deliberately excluded.

A mode is marked by SHAPE, never by colour. Grey double ring with the gap struck in the band colour, and an outlined rather than filled pill. All six accents belong to blocs, and a seventh colour would imply mode is a seventh faction. Note that .mode-state sets --accent only — setting --accent-text too puts grey initials on the surface at 3.5:1; that variable is the ink for text inside an accent-coloured object, and the grey belongs to the ring and the outline, not to words.

Locations tier 1–4 on fabric continuity — LOCKED. Same four-layer frame as characters, from the same generator so it cannot drift: Ⓐ constant, Ⓑ/Ⓓ boilerplate selected by tier, Ⓒ authored. Ⓑ quotes the place's recorded signature_feature — the location equivalent of a face, never re-decided per variant. Tiers are per VARIANT, so Shaoyang Terraces is Landfall Spar (tier 1) then Replanted (tier 4). A location needs no layered description of a later state, because the change line is authored directly; it carries one generation prompt and one alteration per state. Verified against four injected faults — hand-edited bound, out-of-range tier, dropped signature feature, description drift.

An image that predates its own prompt is stale — LOCKED. Every other check reads prompts and cannot see a pixel, so the link between a corrected prompt and a corrected picture is process, and this is what makes a broken link visible rather than silent. image-log.json records, per subject and per VARIANT, the fingerprint of the prompt as it stood when the image was ingested; the build compares it against the current prompt and Studio lists every mismatch with its reason — placeholder for a slot holding a copy of another variant, prompt changed since for the case that will keep recurring as canon evolves. A fingerprint rather than a date, because a date cannot tell you whether the prompt moved. Regenerating from the prompt and dropping the file in _inbox/ clears the row.

Naming, frames and captions — LOCKED. One filename shape for both categories: <Bloc> - <Subject> - v<N> and <Bloc> - <Region> - <Place> - v<N>. v1 is the anchor; the state is NOT in the filename but looked up from state_arc[N-1], so a filename cannot contradict canon. Every frame is captioned including a lone one — "Anchor · <state>", then "Variant 2 · <state>" — because a reader cannot otherwise tell an anchor from the only image that happens to exist. Prompts carry the identical labels in the identical order, from the same helper.

A variant is EDITED from the anchor, never generated — LOCKED. The full canon, with worked examples, is Project/Management/Image Canon.md, and it is summarised in the Story Bible's Image Generation Prompts section preamble. The essentials:

Handed a full description of a subject in a new state, a generator rebuilds the whole thing and returns someone — or somewhere — else. A description cannot pin a likeness. So variant 1 is always the anchor, generated from the layered prompt, and every later variant is an edit of that image.

An anchor has constraints of its own, because it is the substrate every later image is cut from: anything it commits, every alteration must un-commit. Character anchors hold no prop, are unobscured, carry no legible text and stage no moment. Location anchors take a viewpoint that can be held permanently, show the signature feature unobstructed, and depict the place rather than an occasion in it.

An alteration is four layers, always: Ⓐ directive, Ⓑ hold, Ⓒ change, Ⓓ bound. Ⓐ is constant, Ⓑ and Ⓓ are boilerplate selected by grade, and Ⓒ is the only authored content — so a new variant needs one decision and one paragraph. No filename: the image is attached, and naming a file wastes the strongest position in the prompt. No word budget (Image Canon.md, Rule 2) — state each layer fully; length is not a target to hit or undershoot.

The pose licence is a GRADE, not an extra layer. An earlier design bolted on a conditional fifth layer. That cannot be validated uniformly, makes a deliberate omission indistinguishable from an oversight, and made characters structurally unlike locations. Grading Ⓑ/Ⓓ fixes all three and gives both categories one shape. Characters grade 1–3 on staging continuity; locations tier 1–4 on fabric continuity. Identity is never released at any grade; viewpoint, footprint and signature feature are never released at any tier.

Conflating identity with composition is a real bug. Anders's dispersal has him lying in a medical bay, and a single "preserve pose and framing" hold produced an instruction for a man supine in a workshop. Grade 2 exists for exactly that.

The instruction is derived, not written. Ⓐ is constant across every alteration, Ⓑ and Ⓓ are boilerplate selected by grade, and only Ⓒ is authored per variant, so Archive/make_edit_prompts.py renders the diff and there is no second copy to drift. The build re-validates every stored prompt against the canon — four layers in order, a declared grade, holds matching that grade verbatim, no filename, no generation-format text. Verified against five injected faults.

Variant 1 is always the earliest state. Three portraits already depicted the later one — Anders in the isolation bay "nine days after the cascade", Mara "partially belong[ing] to a different register", Jamie in the Archive that is "after Chapter 51, her residence" — so they were re-filed to variant 2 and their before is what remains to be made. The alternative left variant 1 meaning "earliest" for most characters and "whatever we happened to make first" for three, which is the kind of exception that quietly rots a rule.

One prompt per state, in a list. image_prompts is aligned index-for-index with state_arc, so a character with three states has three prompts and the site labels each with the state it depicts, in the same order as the frames beside it. image_prompts is the only home for that text — the singular image_prompt and image_prompt_2 fields it used to be mirrored into are retired, and a build check fails if either comes back. On a mode variant, the setting content authored into that prompt reflects the canonical text of the world the frame is set in — not a bloc the character belongs to. Rina's variant 1 is Corporate Union and her variant 2 is Luna Haven, because that is where the Archive is. Each state's prompt is authored fresh under Image Canon's build-step discipline, never a shared template swapped out for modes.

Second prompts live in the bible, in Image Generation Prompts under each mover as a labelled "Second portrait" block. Derived data must never hold content the source of truth does not have.

_audit_prompt_blocs() in build_site.py fails the build if a character's prompt doesn't contain their own bloc's grounding language somewhere in the text — a presence check, not a positional one, since a build prompt has no fixed layer slots to check by position — reading the signature phrases from the data so a reworded bloc description updates the audit with it. A character's expected signature comes from their TERRITORY where the territory has its own signature — which is why Luna Haven needs care, and why a missing territory field and a wrong prompt are one fault rather than two.

Verified against two deliberately corrupted prompts; both were caught, and the build went clean when restored. Being staged in another bloc's territory is not a fault: the three Guardian AIs work in Luna Haven's Archive Chamber during the siege and keep their Martian material grammar, because they are Martian-grown and merely present.

A gradient is a painted surface. check_contrast_render.py parses gradient colour stops and scores the worst one: a label must be legible on all of a gradient, not just the friendly end.

The site never explains its own production — LOCKED

Site copy carries the work, never the making of it. No dated provenance, no "drafted from the chapters", no "please review before treating it as canon", no Revised 2026-07-12 eyebrows above headings. If a note is about how something got written, it belongs in project-ledger.json, not on a page.

And the canon documents themselves carry no history either. A rule states the latest final version and the reasoning that makes it defensible; the date it was decided, what it used to say, and which pass applied it are not part of the rule. This governs the Story Bible, Image Canon.md and this document alike — every one of them reaches a site page, so a dated parenthetical or a "superseded by" clause anywhere in them is a production note on a page. The ledger is where the sequence of decisions lives, and Studio is where it is read.

One deliberate exemption:

  • In-world prose legitimately contains phrases that read like production notes — a chapter ends "Awaiting compliance sign-off.", another "Awaiting release authorisation." That's the Corporate Union talking, not us.

Enforced. _audit_production_notes() fails the build on dated provenance or flag wording in site copy. It's a targeted regex rather than a keyword sweep because of the in-world-prose exemption — matching bare words like "placeholder" or "awaiting" would fire on the manuscript.

Image variants — the v-scheme

Every finished source image in Assets/Characters and Assets/Locations carries an explicit variant number after a v, one filename shape for both categories:

Corporate Union - Elena Vázquez - v1.png
Lunar Accord - Elena Vázquez - v2.png
Corporate Union - New Manhattan - Surveillance Spire - v1.png

<Bloc> - <Subject> - v<N> for characters, <Bloc> - <Region> - <Place> - v<N> for locations. v1 is always the anchor. The state is not in the filename — it's looked up from state_arc[N-1], so a filename can never contradict canon; the author types only the number, and the site prints the exact filename under every frame so there's nothing to guess. (The v prefix is what removes the ambiguity: a bare trailing number cannot be told apart from the digit in a place name like "Sector 7" without a roster check.)

What the number means depends on the subject:

  • Characters — the variant indexes the character's bloc_arc. Variant 1 is the origin bloc, variant 2 the bloc they permanently move to. Only the six characters with a permanent move have a variant 2; the site shows one portrait per variant, each framed in that bloc's accent with the bloc named beneath.
  • Locations — the variant is a world-state. Variant 1 is present-day 2087; later numbers are for a site as it appears after a rift event, an attack, or anything else that changes how the place looks.

Working steps. Under Image Canon's Rule 14 an image is built in more than one generator call, and the intermediate rungs carry a further - step<N> tail (… - v1 - step1.png) and are filed in a Working steps/ subfolder of their own category's folder. A step is not a world-state, so it never takes a variant number of its own; the finished image of each state keeps the plain v<N> name above, which is why nothing that counts finished images has to learn about steps at all.

Parsing. split_variant() strips the trailing -v<N> segment, then checks each remaining --separated segment against the character/location roster, from the end, to identify which segment is the canonical subject name (a location's bloc/region segments come first). A file with no -v<N> segment reads as variant 1.

Dropping images in _inbox: the supersede sweep is scoped to the same variant, so a new variant 2 never deletes variant 1, and "same filename replaces the existing image" still works.

Enforced. The build checks that every portrait's bloc prefix agrees with its variant — variant N must be filed under bloc_arc[N-1] — and reports any that disagree. Such a file would still build, but would show the wrong bloc's portrait in the wrong coloured frame.

Second prompts. A mover's second-state build prompt is authored fresh under Image Canon.md's build-step discipline for that specific state's setting (see "Second prompts" above) — never derived by swapping a shared layer.

Scripts: parsing and enforcement live in the matching split_variant() in both build_site.py and ingest_images.py — change them together. Archive/rename_image_variants.py, Archive/migrate_to_v_scheme.py and Archive/make_variant_prompts.py are one-time tools with no live caller, kept in Archive/ and never run.

Photography / imagery register

Restates the bible's universal visual rules (Part I) so they don't have to be rediscovered per asset:

  • Photorealistic film stills, not concept art or illustration.
  • Strictly diegetic lighting only — no lens flare, no HUD overlays, no schematic annotations.
  • Dual register in every frame: beauty with oppression, wonder with grief, order with terror.
  • Location plates: strict 16:9. Character plates: strict 1:1.
  • Human figures present but not centred; medium distance is the default.
  • No present-day objects, clothing, vehicles, architecture, or lighting — everything reads as 2087.
