04 — Drafting Guide

How the trilogy's prose is written. This guide is the only home for prose rules. The story is in 01 - Trilogy Bible.md, 02 - Cast.md, 03 - Book Outlines.md, 05 - World.md, 06 - Daily Life.md and 07 - Look Book.md.

Prose is drafted only when the author authorises it, one chapter or batch at a time.

04 — Drafting Guide · 1

Locked conventions

Point of view and tense. Rotating third-person limited, past tense, across all three books. Each scene commits fully to one viewpoint character's senses and thoughts. No head-hopping.

One viewpoint per chapter. Each chapter belongs to the viewpoint named in its outline. A second viewpoint thread, separated by a scene break, is allowed only where the outline marks it.

Chapter head. Each chapter opens with a short header: bloc and place, then viewpoint. Any chapter set on or talking to Mars, or on a ship between, adds the Earth–Mars signal delay:

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE SPIRE / Elena Vázquez — Mars 13 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Kaelin Drake — Earth 13 min

During the 2096 blackout the delay reads Earth: no signal on Mars and Mars: no signal on Earth. The header gives orientation only. The prose still renders how the place looks, sounds and feels. In Book 3 the falling number is the countdown, so never explain it in prose.

Register: "Elevated Commercial". About 70% commercial engine to 30% literary flavour. Clear sentences, fast pacing, dialogue forward, varied rhythm, invisible prose under action. Literary detail (specific metaphor, sensory precision, thematic echo) is seasoning, not a sustained mode. Slow down for the first sight of a new place or technology, and stay lean through action and dialogue. Inner states are shown through action, dialogue and physical detail, never summarised.

Chapter length. Between 1,800 and 4,000 words, enforced by the build; the target is 2,500–3,200 (section 9). Let pacing set the length; don't pad or trim to a number.

System voices. Machines may speak short, functional lines in their bloc's register: the Charter clipped and procedural, the Mandate formal, the Compact warm, Luna Haven conversational. The Gate and the Directorate's system speak in the Continuity's clipped register until the Hollow starts to show through. When it does, they become a little too kind.

04 — Drafting Guide · 2

Every chapter

Each chapter's card in the outlines gives what the viewpoint wants, what stands against it, the choice and its cost, what it carries forward, its pressures, its main suspense, one detail of the lived world and the weather. The draft delivers all of them.

  1. Open late. Start inside the scene, as close to the turn as the reader can follow.
  2. The goal is clearer than the lore. By the end of the first page the reader knows who wants what in the next hour and what happens if they fail. The history can wait.
  3. The pressures interfere. The chapter's pressures act on each other at once: the practical fix requires the betrayal. Never one after another.
  4. The choice is made, not suffered. The viewpoint decides something under pressure. A chapter where things only happen to them is a situation, not a chapter.
  5. Pay the cost on the page. A body, a relationship, a place or a future, and it is shown, not reported.
  6. End where a choice can't be undone. Leave early. The last line belongs to what the card carries forward, or something stronger, and never only to a new fact.
  7. One new idea at most. No more than one invented term or new piece of world per chapter. The world detail is shown working inside the action, never explained.
  8. Joy appears. Something ordinary, funny or loved happens in every chapter, ideally before the danger.

Scenes

Every scene turns. A character enters wanting something concrete, meets resistance, changes tactics (persuasion becomes threat, concealment becomes confession), gets a result they didn't expect, and chooses under a worse constraint. The cost of that choice carries into the next scene.

  • Quiet scenes do work. A low-action scene builds a bond, a ritual, a promise or an object that a later loss will test, or lets someone make a small compromise or win something that hides a debt. The rhythm is pressure, partial release, deeper pressure, never a reset.
  • Reveals come in layers. Give the immediate fact and what the viewpoint must do now. What it means for the wider conflict, and what it means morally, unfold through later choices. Never all four at once.
  • Cut between viewpoints with purpose. Cut when the next viewpoint answers, contradicts or worsens what the last one just set up, never only because the current one got exciting.
  • Vary the suspense. If every chapter threatens death, the reader goes numb. The cards rotate the main suspense; the prose honours the rotation.

04 — Drafting Guide · 4

Writing the strange things

Soria

  • Her voice: warm, teasing, interrupting. Her sentences stop where memory runs out. She hates being comforted.
  • The tell: the real Soria contradicts what the Hollow wants. When "Soria" urges Elena toward the bridge, the Gate or restoration, it's the mask, and the prose makes it slightly too smooth and too sure. When she teases, refuses, or tells Elena to go home, it's her.
  • Never resolve it early. Until Book 3 Ch 27, the reader must be able to read every Soria line both ways.
  • Bichito is rationed to about four uses per book, where the outline places them (in Book 1: Ch 1, Ch 15, Ch 22 and Ch 29). Each use has to land.

The Hollow

  • It is never described as a monster. On the page it is a chorus: warmth, many voices under one, relief at not being alone.
  • It never lies. Every warning in the prose must later prove true.
  • It wears faces it has taken. When it speaks as someone, it is that person almost exactly, with one small wrongness the reader feels before the character does.
  • In-world, humans call it the Hollow. The name is Kaelin's word for what Zhenyth calls the hungry one. Before Book 1 Act II-B, nobody has a name for it.

Drift

  • Write it from outside first: lost words, a voice that isn't quite theirs, stopping mid-sentence to listen. Then, in viewpoint chapters, from inside: other people's memories at the edge of sleep, and peace. Drift has to feel good, or it isn't frightening.
  • Sleepers are people, not symptoms. They are named, turned and fed, and spoken to by someone who loves them.

The link and the Preserve

  • The first time any viewpoint character links, slow down and give it full sensory weight. After that, go lean.
  • Inside the Preserve, perception is written as real: plains of slow light, distance, presences. The body is still in a cavern, and the prose remembers it: cold, aching knees, a medic's voice from far away.
  • After every session the body pays: fog, after-images, bleed, broken sleep. Show it in the next scene.

Light delay

  • Earth–Mars exchanges are messages, not conversations. Write them as letters that arrive late: a reply to a question the sender has already moved past.
  • Use the delay for suspense and for heartbreak: reassurance, warning and apology all arrive late.
  • Never give the delay as a number in prose, not even as a sensory phrase (minutes old, never thirteen minutes old), and never do the arithmetic. The header carries the number, and the build refuses a chapter that gives it.

04 — Drafting Guide · 5

Tone and theme

Two charges at once. Every scene carries two opposing feelings together: wonder and grief, safety and dread, love and possession. No scene is only one thing.

The themes underneath:

  1. Love that turns into possession.
  2. A power too useful to give up.
  3. Ordinary work as heroism: crews, medics, repair, care.
  4. Letting go as an act of love, not of defeat.

The prose never states a theme. It puts a person on each side of one and makes them choose.

The weather is never neutral. The Long Heat is in every chapter: in the air, a wall, a rationed thing, a habit learned in a heat year, a memory of the Evacuations. It is shown working, never explained. On the Moon and Mars it arrives as what Earth's weather costs them, and as Earth night. The build fails a drafted chapter where it never shows.

Humour arrives in the worst moments as well as the quiet ones, and never as relief inserted for the reader.

Other languages appear as single words or short lines, left untranslated where context carries them. Elena and Soria's Spanish is family language, so it appears at its most intimate.

Apparatus never becomes prose

04 — Drafting Guide · 7

Names

  • Two-word canonical names for people and places stay locked: given name + family name, place word + landform. Prose uses short forms freely.
  • Names created while drafting are provisional. If a draft needs to name an unnamed figure or place, add the name to the names table in Manuscripts/Continuity.md, list it and every invented detail for the author, and propagate nothing until it is confirmed. The build fails on a proper name that is in no Story Bible document and not in that table.
  • Every name gets its register fields. A name added to the names table gets its Kind (person, place, ship, object, group), its Within (for a place, the place it is in; for a person, where they live or work) and every alias the prose uses. A figure who recurs unnamed gets a row in lower case, renamed if the prose names them. Whenever the prose tells us something new about a person, place or thing, it goes into that thing's row the same turn. Manuscripts/Register.md is built from these rows; the build fails on a row without them.

04 — Drafting Guide · 8

The house voice

The trilogy reads as one hand from the first page to the last. This is that hand.

What it sounds like.

  • Close and restrained. The viewpoint's inner life is on the page, but people manage their feelings rather than announce them. A frightened character checks the instrument twice; the prose never says she is frightened.
  • Long against short. Long sentences that gather clauses as a mind works, cut by short flat ones that land like a decision. She had built the alarm herself, tuned it fine enough to hear one wrong signal in a city of right ones, and tonight it was ringing for her. She turned it off. Sentence length varies widely; that variation is the voice.
  • Hands and habits. Character shows in what people do with their hands and the habits they can't drop. One telling gesture beats a paragraph of description.
  • History in a clause. The past arrives in passing, inside a sentence about the present: the wall they had raised twice and would raise again. Never a paragraph of it.
  • Dry and exact. Irony is precise and unforced, and humour arrives when things are worst.
  • Images that return. A chapter keeps one or two images and lets them gather weight; it does not scatter twenty.
  • Quiet landings. Chapters end on a choice that can't be undone, said plainly, not on a flourish.

The scene tests, for every scene:

  1. Cold open. It starts inside something already happening.
  2. Could it be filmed? Every beat is something a camera could see or a microphone could hear. Thought is allowed; summary is not.
  3. Every line of dialogue does two jobs: it moves the scene and shows the person.
  4. Competence under pressure. People are good at their work, and we watch them do it.
  5. The physics is in the action. Delay, gravity, heat and air shape what people can do in the scene, not in an explanation.
  6. Cut on action or decision, never on a summary.

Never explain. The world is used, not explained. A reader learns what the lace is by watching someone switch it off. Definitions, histories and lectures are cut. A flashback is a played scene with a body in a present moment, never a summary; there is at most one per chapter, and it must change the choice the chapter turns on.

Banned tics. Machine-drafted prose leans on a handful of words and shapes. These are rationed, and the style check counts them: the way (as a simile), exactly, something (as a vague noun), particular, unhurried, patient, quiet and quietly, a beat, let out a breath she didn't know she was holding, the weight of, not X but Y, the kind of X that Y, and , which was … explanations.

Shape on the page. Paragraphs mostly under eighty words; dialogue in most scenes; white space in action.

Anchor chapters. The first approved chapter for each viewpoint is that voice's anchor: Book 1 Ch 1 for Elena, Book 1 Ch 2 for Kaelin, Book 1 Ch 4 for Rachel, Book 2 Ch 1 for Aur'k. Every later chapter is measured against its viewpoint's anchor and against that viewpoint's previous chapter. At the end of every act, the act's last chapter is read beside the anchor and the drift, if any, is named and corrected (section 10, step 9).

04 — Drafting Guide · 9

Keeping the trilogy one book

Length. The build refuses a chapter under 1,800 words or over 4,000. The target is 2,500–3,200. Action chapters may run shorter and climaxes longer, inside the limits. Pace decides; nothing is padded.

The brief. Before any chapter is drafted, chapter_brief.py assembles its brief from the documents, so no chapter depends on memory or on rereading the whole manuscript. The brief holds:

  • the chapter's card, heading, delay, weather and Bichito placement;
  • for every character on the page, their Cast entry, look, voice notes and current state;
  • for every place, its World, Daily Life and Look Book entries;
  • every storyline the chapter touches, with the closing paragraphs of the drafted chapter where that storyline was last left: its anchor point;
  • the end of the previous chapter and of this viewpoint's previous chapter;
  • the plants and payoffs due, and who knows what at this point;
  • the viewpoint's anchor chapter, and the voice targets.

The records. Manuscripts/Continuity.md keeps, besides names, the story so far and the plants:

  • Character state: where each principal is, what they want now, what they hide, their body, how their key bonds stand, and the phrases they have already used, updated after every chapter they appear in;
  • Storylines: each thread and the words that mark it, so the build can see when a chapter the outline gives a thread never touches it;
  • Who knows what: each fact a character learns on the page, and the chapter they learn it.

The build checks all three.

04 — Drafting Guide · 10

The per-chapter pipeline

Done in the same turn a chapter is drafted or revised, unasked.

  1. Brief. Run chapter_brief.py B C and read it whole. Read the anchor chapter for the viewpoint.
  2. Scene plan. List the scenes, each with its turn (who wants what, what changes tactics, what they choose), every new name, place or canon-adjacent detail the plan invents, and for each scene, its place and who is in it. Put it to the author as a questionnaire, with a recommendation on each call, and stop. No prose is written until the author approves it. Record the approval in the Scene plans table of Manuscripts/Continuity.md; the build refuses a chapter without its row.
  3. Draft to the conventions above.
  4. Check. Run, from Project/_builder/: check_prose_vocabulary.py, check_prose_concepts.py, check_prose_distances.py, check_prose_style.py (length, tics, explanation, paragraphs, dialogue) and check_prose_voice.py (against the viewpoint's anchor), and check.py register (scene lists against the prose, and names due for 02 Cast or 05 World). Fix what they flag. Then score the draft against its card, the chapter rules (section 2) and the scene tests (section 8).
  5. Cold read. A separate reviewer that has not seen the chapter being written reads only the brief, the draft and this guide, and reports breaks in continuity, logic, character, voice and the never-explain rule. Every finding is fixed or answered.
  6. Revise.
  7. Save to Project/Manuscripts/Book N - Title/Chapter NN - Title.md. Earlier versions go to the chapter's _drafts folder.
  8. Record continuity: the story-so-far row, new facts and names, the chapter's Scenes rows (place, present, by link, in the link), every character state the chapter changed, storyline beats and who-knows-what. Anything new about a person, place or thing goes into its own row. Show them to the author with the draft and the cold read's findings, with any promotion candidate the register now lists.
  9. Act read, when the chapter is its act's last. Once the author approves it, read it beside its viewpoint's anchor chapter and that viewpoint's previous chapter. Name any drift in voice, register or the house voice, correct it with the author, and record the read in the Act reads table of Manuscripts/Continuity.md. The build refuses to register the act's last chapter, or any later one, without that row.
  10. Register the chapter in MANUSCRIPT_CHAPTERS in build_site.py, keyed (book, chapter), once the author approves it.
  11. Rebuild: run build_site.py. Every audit must print clean.
  12. Log an applied entry in project-ledger.json.
  13. Surface any new name, canon question or outline change to the author. Never adopt one silently.
