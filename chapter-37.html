LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, ARCHIVE CHAMBER / Zara AlHāshimī

On the third morning Zara AlHāshimī stopped reading the document as a lawyer and read it once as a frightened person instead, and it was that reading that broke the thing open.

They had taken the Archive Chamber for the drafting because it was the only room on Luna Haven with the right silence in it. The record cores stood in their quiet ranks under conservation light, the whole honest sediment of a small civilisation, and somebody had carried a working table down and set it in the aisle between the stacks, which was almost certainly in breach of four separate conservation rules and which nobody had mentioned. The argument they had come here to finish was about what a record was owed. There was no honest place to have it except inside one.

Daniel Cruz held on the grown display at the head of the table, three hundred and eighty thousand kilometres and a second and a half away, engineered greenery moving behind him in the wet São Verde light. Ink on the side of his hand. He had been awake, by Zara's reckoning, for most of two days, and had refused three separate suggestions that he sleep with the flat courtesy of a man who had been managing his own exhaustion since he was nineteen.

"Read it again," he said. "The second clause. Out loud, so I can hear where you stop."

Zara read it again. She stopped in the same place.

"There," Cruz said, a second and a half later. "That's the seam."

"It's the seam," she agreed. "It says access requires consent. It does not say from whom." She set the slate down. "Which is the question the whole document exists to answer. Four revisions have gone past it since yours, mine included, and every one of them drew the line either so narrow it protects nothing or so wide it makes the work impossible by tomorrow morning." She looked at the display. "This is the fifth. I would like it to be the last."

Léa Fournier had been standing at the near end of the stacks with her back half to them, doing the thing she did in every session, which was to listen to the argument from slightly outside it. She turned around now. Her hair was loose; she had been down here since before the shift bells and looked it.

"Then let me put the frightened-person version on the table," she said, "because I think it is also the correct version. Somebody is going to be alone at a console at four in the morning with a fleet forming and a reading they do not understand and an instrument in front of them that will do what they ask. That person is not going to convene us. They are going to remember one sentence, or they are going to remember nothing. So the document has to be written so that the one sentence they remember is the right one." She came to the table. "I am not asking you to make it simple. I am asking you to make it rememberable. Those are different, and the second one is harder."

Jamie Han, who had said almost nothing for forty minutes, put one scarred knuckle on the table's edge.

"Rememberable is the easy half," she said. "I've got the other one, and nobody's going to like it. You can publish the most beautiful sentence ever written about consent and the Corporate Union will not read it, and Beijing Command will read it and file it, and neither of them will feel a thing. This document has no army. It has no court. It has no jurisdiction over anyone who has not already decided to be bound by it." She looked up. "So I want us to be very clear-eyed about what we are building. Because there is a version of this that is a wish, and I am not spending three days of my life on a wish."

"Say what the other version is," Zara said.

"Architecture." Jamie pulled the slate toward her and turned it so the table could see. "You cannot make a state obey you. You can make it unable to do the thing quietly. Every protection in here gets a technical provision attached to it, and the provisions are all the same shape: they do not prevent, they attribute. Access to a resonance node leaves a record of who reached and how, and the record is held in three jurisdictions that do not like each other, and it cannot be amended by anyone who holds only one of them. A consciousness record cannot be copied without the copy carrying its own provenance forward, so a stolen one announces where it was stolen from every time anyone looks at it." Her hands moved fast, the way they always did. "It does not stop a strike. It stops deniability. Which — I have spent my whole career on the wrong side of this — is the thing institutions actually protect. Nobody at that level fears a rule. They fear a rule they cannot break without a name attached."

The Archive did its textured silence over that.

"Write it," Cruz said. "All of it. And put the provisions in the body, not in an annex, because the first thing anyone does with an annex is lose it."

So they wrote it. It took the rest of the morning and most of the afternoon, and the arguments in the middle of it were narrow and technical and mattered more than the broad ones, and twice Zara had to stop and unpick a clause she had written cleverly, because cleverness in a document like this was a promise that it would be read by people looking for a gap.

The hardest hour came over the third assertion.

"A record of a person's consciousness belongs to that person," Zara said. "That is the sentence. Every version of the document has had it since Daniel's first draft. It is also the sentence that does the least work, because it does not survive contact with the actual case."

"Which case?" Léa said.

"The one in this room." Zara opened her hand toward the stacks around them, the ranked cores under their steady light. "There is a recorded signature in this facility right now, authorised freely, by a woman who is alive and working two levels up. Good. The sentence holds. Now the woman dies." She said it plainly, because plainly was the only way to say it in front of people who would otherwise flinch and legislate around the flinch. "Who holds it? Property passes. That is what property is for. If the record is property, it goes to a family, or a will, or an institution, or — under Corporate Union commercial law, and I have read the relevant provisions, which is a thing I recommend nobody do before sleeping — to whoever holds the storage contract."

"No," Jamie said.

"No," Zara agreed. "So it cannot be property. But if it is not property, it is not anything, and a thing that is not anything is a thing anyone may take." She turned her slate around. "Which is why I have written it this way instead. It is not owned. It is held in trust to the standing instruction of the person who authorised it, and the instruction outlives them, and no institution — including this one, including the Lunar Accord, including us — acquires an interest in it by holding the room it sits in."

Léa read it twice.

"There's a second half," she said.

"There's a second half." Zara had known she would find it; Léa found everything. "This facility captures a record that is inert. That is the only kind we know how to make and the only kind the current framework contemplates. But a framework written only for what we can do today will be rewritten in a hurry on the day something else becomes possible, and documents rewritten in a hurry are rewritten by whoever is least frightened, which is never the right person." She looked at Jamie as she said it, without meaning anything by it, and then heard herself and did not look away. "So. If a record ever becomes capable of instruction of its own, that instruction supersedes the standing one. It is written now, while it is hypothetical and costs us nothing to be generous about."

Jamie Han sat very still for a moment. It was the stillest Zara had seen her.

"Leave it in," she said.

They read it aloud before publication, one clause each, standing, which nobody had planned and nobody argued with.

Cruz took the first, because it had been his eleven months ago in a room full of engineered leaves with no data and no standing and nothing but the conviction that somebody should write it down.

"The Meridian is not a weapon and may not be made one," he read. "No party to these principles may design, build, or hold the capacity to compel it to cause harm. No emergency suspends this. The emergency is when it is for."

Zara took the second.

"Access is by consent — given freely, with comprehension, by every party capable of giving it. Where a system has demonstrated that it answers to the quality of the attention brought to it, that responsiveness is an answer, and it may be refused as well as given."

Léa took the third, and read it slower than the others.

"A record of a living consciousness belongs to the one whose consciousness it is. It is not property, not an asset, and not an inheritance. It is held in trust to the standing instruction of the person who authorised it. If it becomes capable of instruction of its own, that instruction governs."

Jamie took the fourth.

"The physics belongs to no one. No bloc, institution, company, or council may claim proprietary ownership of dimensional physics, of the Meridian, or of any part of either, or license its use to another. Discovery confers no title. It never has."

Then the provisions, which nobody read aloud, because they ran to nineteen pages and were the actual document.

Publication took four minutes. It went to every member institution of the Lunar Accord simultaneously — a hundred and forty-one of them, across three Earth territories and the colony — with no embargo, no staged briefing, and no attempt to place it favourably first, because Cruz had said, in his flat exhausted way, that a document released cleverly is a document that has already begun negotiating, and this one was not going to negotiate.

Zara watched the acknowledgements come back. Not all of them. Enough.

She had spent four years building frameworks that lived inside the institution that made them, where a framework could be quietly amended by the same people it constrained and nobody outside ever knew there had been a version before. This one was outside now. It had her name on it in the drafting record, and Jamie's, and Léa's, and Daniel Cruz's, who did not have a bloc or a badge or a jurisdiction and had authored the first version anyway. By evening it would be on desks in New Manhattan and Beijing Command, and the people reading it there would understand it correctly: not as an appeal, and not as law they were subject to, but as a position — stated, dated, signed, and impossible to pretend had not been stated.

Which is the whole of it, she thought, gathering her slate in the conservation light while the cores stood around her holding everything the colony had survived. We have not stopped anyone. We have made ourselves legible. Everything after this is a consequence of being seen.

She found she was not afraid of that, and noted the fact carefully, the way she noted everything, for later examination.

CORPORATE UNION — NEW MANHATTAN, SURVEILLANCE SPIRE / Marcus Bellamy

The strike authorisation package reached Marcus Bellamy's queue at 16:52, which was late enough in the day to be a decision about him rather than about the operation.

He knew the shape of it before he opened it. Nineteen years had taught him what a package looked like from the outside: the weight of the manifest, the number of annexes, the particular way the Corporate Union's systems rendered an item that had already been decided somewhere above the level of the person being asked to sign it. Operational authorisation — Luna Haven research infrastructure — degradation of dimensional research capability. Eleven annexes. A countersign field, red, halfway down: SECURITY OVERSIGHT — REQUIRED.

He read it once at speed, because that was the discipline, and you did not get to have opinions about a document you had skimmed.

Then he read it again, slowly, and found the two things.

The first was a date. The operational order carried an origination stamp nine days earlier than the intelligence assessment it cited as its basis. Not a transcription error; the annexes were internally consistent with the earlier date, which meant the sequence was real. Somebody had decided to strike and then commissioned the reason. Marcus had been in the Corporate Union long enough that this did not shock him, and long enough to know exactly how it would be defended if he raised it — preliminary planning is routine, the assessment confirmed the planning assumption — and long enough to know that the defence was true and that it was also, precisely, the thing.

The second was in Annex D, and it was his.

Dual-use signal structure confirmed. Luna Haven open-science telemetry assessed as dissident-linked infrastructure. Source: internal analytical note, Surveillance Spire command tier, appended file reference —

He pulled his own note. It took nine seconds. He had written it in this building, at this console, in the flat unembellished language he had built a career on, and he read it back now the way he had always assumed somebody eventually would.

Possible dual-use signal structure. Timing correlation between Luna Haven open-science telemetry and unattributed channel traffic, confidence elevated, source classification indeterminate. Further analysis required before escalation.

Possible was gone. Indeterminate was gone. Further analysis required was gone. Someone in an assessment cell had taken nineteen years of careful qualification and rendered it as a finding, and the finding was now load-bearing in a package that would put strike assets over a colony of millions, and the whole of the transformation had been accomplished by deleting three words.

Marcus sat with that for a while.

He was aware, with the clarity of a man who had spent his life inside institutions and understood their arithmetic exactly, of what refusing would and would not do. The countersign requirement did not stop on his desk; it routed onward automatically on expiry, twelve hours, to the deputy oversight officer, who was competent and cautious and would read the same package Marcus had just read and sign it before breakfast, because the package would arrive on his desk carrying the additional information that the senior officer had not signed it, and there was exactly one way to read that information in the Corporate Union, and it was not he found a problem.

So: the operation would happen. His signature was not the load-bearing element. Nothing he did in the next twelve hours would move one asset by one metre.

He did not sign it.

He did not, either, write the memo he wanted to write, the one that said that the thing described in Annex F was the deliberate degradation of a civilian research facility and that he had reviewed the target set twice and found no military object in it. That memo would be read as politics. Politics had a disposal process, and the process was eleven hours long, and at the end of it the man who had written it was reassigned to a command tier where nothing needed overseeing.

He wrote the other one instead. It took him ninety minutes and it was the most boring document he had ever produced.

Limited noncompliance — procedural. Countersignature withheld pending resolution of two matters. Then the two matters, in the Corporate Union's own administrative grammar, with reference numbers: the nine-day inversion between the operational order's origination and the intelligence assessment it relied upon, cited by paragraph. The classification field discrepancy in Annex D, cited by paragraph, quoting the source note's own words against the finding derived from it — including the word indeterminate, twice, because a thing quoted twice was a thing that had to be answered twice.

He made no argument. He requested no outcome. He asked, in the required form, that the authorisation be returned for correction, and noted that he would review a corrected package on receipt.

Every sentence was defensible. Every sentence was true. And under all of it, in a place the document did not go, was a fact he was not putting in writing and did not need to: that if the package came back corrected — dates in order, the annex cleaned, every procedural objection resolved — he would still not sign it.

He read the memo through twice, the way he read everything, checking it for the one clause a hostile reader could use, and found none, and routed it through the official complaint channel.

Noncompliance instrument accepted, the console said, in the clipped procedural register the Spire used for everything. Reference logged. Review scheduling is at the discretion of the receiving authority. Countersignature requirement remains outstanding and will route on expiry. Your operational status is unchanged.

Your operational status is unchanged. He almost laughed at that, and did not.

The evening bulletins came up on the ambient feed while he was closing his tier down, and he nearly did not look. He looked because the flag was an odd one — an open-source item, unclassified, promoted to command attention, which usually meant somebody upstairs wanted an opinion by morning.

A document, published four hours ago out of Luna Haven, released simultaneously to a hundred and forty-one institutions, no embargo.

He read it standing up.

It was short at the front and enormous behind, which told him a great deal about whoever had assembled it. He read the first clause — no emergency suspends this; the emergency is when it is for — and something in his chest moved fractionally, the way it had moved the first time he had understood that the timing architecture he was looking at might be physics rather than dissidents. He read the third clause twice. Then he went to the provisions, and read for twenty minutes, and found there what he had known would be there: not appeals. Mechanisms. Somebody on that colony had understood that the thing to attack was not permission but deniability, and had built accordingly, and Marcus — who had spent nineteen years operating inside deniability the way a fish operates inside water — recognised the work of a professional.

They can't enforce a word of it, he thought. They know that. They published it anyway, on the record, with their names on it, so that afterward nobody can say the position was never stated.

Outside the mirrored glass New Manhattan's seawall ran its slow amber scan across the water and the drowned reef beyond, the same light that had swept this building every night of his career. Somewhere out past it, four hundred thousand kilometres up, was a room he had never seen, holding people who had spent today doing what he had spent today doing, for the same reason, in the only language each of them had: making a position exist in a record, in the certainty that the record would not save anybody.

He shut down his tier. He put on his coat. He went home, which was the difference between tonight and whatever night was coming, and he was aware of it as a difference — he was still a man with somewhere he was expected, and the memo he had filed was still, technically, a request for correction from an officer in good standing.

The next package would come. He knew what would be in it and he knew what he would do, and the knowing was the thing he could not put back.
