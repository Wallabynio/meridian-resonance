LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, MERIDIAN LEVELS / Mara Ekeberg — twenty-five minutes before the session · Preserve −2h09m

Mara had settled it somewhere around four in the colony's night, lying awake with the wall-light turned down to its slowest breathing: she was not going to carry anything into that room that she had not first looked at herself. It was not a complicated position, only a new one — Jīn had said to Elena on her first day that Mara was not their channel but a person who had agreed to carry things, and agreeing had been easy for exactly as long as the things were findings. Yesterday something had come through her in Kythara's voice with a second thing riding behind it, carried on purpose, and she had felt it pass like a stranger's coat brushing her arm in a crowded corridor — she had said nothing to the room, and had spent the night saying her own sentence over to herself until it stopped sounding like a threat.

The deep-interface chamber was lit low and blue, and the cradle of Preserve wood and cobalt soil stood holding still with its roots drawn in close — which she took personally, because it only ever reached for people who weren't afraid. She sat down in front of it anyway, cross-legged, hands open on her knees, with Jīn an arm's length to her left and the diagnostic stylus turning between scarred fingertips, and she stopped trying to go, and the thin place came up under her like a floor.

Kythara had always waited at the edge of her the way it once waited at a treeline, until she made room. This did not wait. It stood in the doorway of her with its surface open, unhurried and entirely composed, like someone who has come to a hearing and would rather be examined first — the feel of it was order without chill, a clean bench with every instrument set down where a person who loved them would set it, and under the order a slow even movement like a hand smoothing paper flat against a table, over and over.

Mara Ekeberg. The voice sat in the near part of her, precise and low. We have not met in this manner. You may read me before I begin, since a thing is carried more steadily by someone who has seen what is carrying it.

So she read it, and the order went all the way down; Vaera Assay was the most carefully kept mind she had ever stood inside, and at the back of the composure sat one small thing, set down and left, the way you leave what you cannot yet look at and cannot bring yourself to throw away.

"You're carrying something," she said.

I am.

"So was Kythara, yesterday. I felt where it had been." She kept her voice level, and beside the body Jīn's stylus stopped turning. "I want to know what it was before I carry anything else for you."

The smoothing under her ribs stilled, and then began again.

You found it. I hoped you would. It is mine to tell — I found it, and I asked to be the one. Something has crossed the boundary of the Preserve: yesterday by your count, more than three days ago by ours. It was not forced through. It was admitted, at the damaged section of the northern run, because it met the conditions the boundary applies to anything that asks to cross, and the boundary did what it is built to do. It is inside. It is small.

The floor held. She noted that it held, and noted that she had needed to.

"The thing from the record," she said. "The one under the colony."

The thing that record was written about. Yes.

"And Kythara knew, and sat in that room while the rest of us spent the night arguing about whom to warn."

The Grove knew. It held the finding for further assessment and I concurred, and I will say why in the room, because that is owed to more people than you. What is owed to you first is a look. I would not put it into you unlooked-at.

"One turn of it," she said. "Not the whole."

One turn. I will stop when you say.

She had expected cold, and it was not cold; it was the most finished thing she had ever touched, the whole outward grammar of one thing turning toward another with the other's state as its object, and it turned toward her with an attention as perfect as a lit window. She reached back toward the lit room. There was no room — only light, faithfully lit, with nothing standing behind the glass.

"Stop."

It stopped at once, and she was in her body again with both hands pressed into her knees hard enough to hurt.

"I've been introduced to something." She breathed until her hands were hers, while the cradle drew its roots in a fraction further. "Is it looking at us?" she asked Vaera, and heard how young it sounded.

I do not find anything that it is looking with.

She sat with that until it stopped being an answer and became a fact, and then she said aloud the thing she had decided at four in the morning, so that it would count. "I'll carry it. Here's the condition. You don't put a word into me that I haven't read first, I say it in that room myself, plainly, once, and I stop when I need to stop and not when you're finished." She looked out at the blue dark. "It has to be a decision every time."

Yes. She felt the yes go through the whole of it without any of the small negotiations she had learned to expect from things that wanted something from her. That is the correct arrangement. I would have proposed it, if I had thought you would let me.

She took the long kind of breath, and let the rest of Vaera in.

LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, ARCHIVE CHAMBER / Elena Vázquez — session 0h31m · Preserve +2h40m

They had put the working table back in the aisle between the record cores, and nobody had mentioned the conservation rules — Elena had stopped keeping count of how many the alliance was in breach of, and it was somewhere north of four. Jamie Han had set the recording before anyone sat down, and the Chamber had answered in its warm conversational voice that it was writing to all three cores, attribution set to Vaera Assay, relayed through Mara Ekeberg, consent on record — provisions Elena had thought the most quietly vicious drafting she had seen since the Spire, because they were aimed at exactly the people she used to work for.

The grown wall at the aisle's end carried two views: on the left, a level down, Mara sat cross-legged with her eyes closed and Jīn a careful arm's length off, and on the right lay a wide unlit space Eddy had built for the overlay. The voice, when it came, came out of Mara a half-beat behind her lips, in her own cadence with the edges taken off — slower and flatter than Kythara's had been, the voice of something that set each word down as though it might later be asked to defend it.

I will begin with what should have been said yesterday. Something has crossed the boundary of the Preserve. It has been inside three days by the Grove's count and one by yours. It was not forced. It was admitted, at the section of the northern run that the weapons work outside tore open and no one has healed, and I cannot tell you that it chose that door, because I have no mechanism to tell selection from the absence of a selector. It is small.

Elena had spent a decade learning to read a room's first second of bad news, and she watched this one land in the small places — Zara's open hands closing on the tabletop, once, deliberately; Xiàng's pen stopping above a page on which he had written nothing — while Léa, at the near end of the stacks with her back half to the table, did not move at all, and Elena, who had never managed to price her, watched her shoulders take the news and set.

"Yesterday," Léa said. "We spent the night deciding whom to warn about a door. And it was already open."

Yes. Vaera did not soften it and did not decorate it. The Grove held the finding for further assessment before it was said, and I concurred — a report that carried a guess into a fact would have set your night on ground no one had verified, and that reasoning was sound. It did not make the hold free. You made decisions yesterday without the thing they were about, and I do not distribute that cost. It is ours.

Léa was quiet for the length of a breath. "Noted," she said. "Thank you for the cost."

Now the analysis, which is why I asked for this room. The dark on the right half of the wall woke. Elena Vázquez, Eddy Okafor: I will place three traces in your environment. I will not place a human pattern there without that human's instruction, and where I need a living reference I have used a grazer's.

Something warm and out of place went through Elena at that: the Principles doing their work, in the mouth of something that had never signed them.

The first trace drew itself: a ring, a line that left a point and curved away and came back, untidy, thick in places and thin in others, crossing itself once near the top and closing on its origin anyway, so that it looked hand-drawn by someone thinking about something else. A grazer regarding another grazer across a dozen metres of moss, Vaera said. Everything genuine is made like this. It costs something to reach, the one who reached is altered by it and knows, and the knowing is the return. The loop closes. It is never clean.

The second ring drew itself beside the first, and Elena's stomach turned over. It was beautiful — the outward curve as smooth as a pulled thread, immaculate, the shape of one thing extended toward another with the other's state as its object — and the return did not return — a short way past the turn it broke into a scatter of bright strokes that fell toward the origin and never met it, a coastline come apart into islands.

This is what is in the northern run. Tidy on the way out, in pieces on the way back. There is nothing that reaches, and so nothing that returns to itself; there is the form of reaching, complete, and an absence where the one reaching would be.

The third trace was already drawing, and she knew it before it finished. She knew the outward curve because she had specified it: the stabilising field, the fragment-and-fill, eleven days of her own work at a console in this colony's blue dark, and beside it the return broke apart the same way, in the same place, into the same islands.

The Corporate Union's instrument is a model of care, constructed to be used on people, Vaera said. I say it without judgement of its author. She is a person, and it is her being one that keeps the model from being the thing itself. The thing in the northern run is a model with no author that I can find. They are one shape, met in two places, and I believe there is a third. There is also one exception in the Corporate Union's instrument — a single place in it where the return closes. It is very small. I will not say where. It is not mine to point to, and it is not damaged.

Elena found she had put both hands flat on the table.

Nobody looked at her, which was the courtesy of it: five people who knew where Elena had braided her hands into the design's spine, and every one of them studying the wall. She sat inside the fact — the whole design hollow, from its first parameter to its last, a real and lethal instrument for emptying out people's coherence, and the only place in it where anybody was home the place she had hidden to break it. She took the pen out of her pocket and did not use it.

Zara had not moved in a long time, and when she spoke it was in the level voice she used before she took something apart. "Vaera. I have spent four years asking machines to prove they are not performing, and I have never found a test a good enough performance could not pass. You have just shown this room a picture of the thing we should fear, and it is a picture of a very good performance. Why should we not put you on the next trace?"

The silence that followed was the first untidy one of the session, and it ran a beat past the point where a smooth answer would have arrived.

You should not exclude it. Vaera's cadence did not change, but the words came slower. From outside, in one sitting, you cannot tell me from what I have described; that is the whole of the danger, and I will not insult it by claiming exemption. I can offer only what I look for in others, which is the return — whether what is said costs the speaker something, and whether the speaker is altered by having said it. I would rather I had not had to say this to you in this room. That preference does nothing for the analysis, and it is there. I know that a good enough performance would have offered you precisely that sentence, and I ask you to note it and not to accept it.

"That isn't proof," Zara said.

No.

"It doesn't have to be." Jamie's voice came from the far end of the table, flat and fast. "Everything it says is in the cores with its name on it, and if it's lying we'll have the lie, dated, in three places that hate each other. You don't have to trust it. You have to be able to attribute it."

That is the correct answer, Vaera said. I am glad it is yours.

Zara looked at the wall a moment longer. "It doesn't settle it."

It should not. The third place is not in this room, and I would rather you did not take my word for it. The right half of the wall brightened and a hillside came into focus, far from the Chamber's conservation light: eleven shallow steps of rammed earth and reclaimed stone under high African sun. Thandi Okoro. Begin at the return.

The woman in the frame stood with her braids pulled back and a handheld raised in one sun-toughened hand, already halfway up a retaining wall, and she wore the face of someone who has just been told what the thing in her notebook is.

"The return is where I stopped measuring," Thandi Okoro said. "I thought there was nothing there to measure."

LUNAR ACCORD — AFRICAN BELT, TABLE TERRACES / Thandi Okoro — session 0h33m · Preserve +2h50m

She had listened to the whole of it standing, because sitting made her want to interrupt. The monitoring station on the sixth terrace was three rammed-earth walls and an open front, instrument racks along the back and a bench worn pale by a decade of elbows, and from its doorway the slope fell away in eleven steps under the high light, red beds and reclaimed stone and the sensor mesh catching sun like frost. Vaera's voice had reached her through the relay in Mara's cadence, and by the second ring she had stopped pretending to take notes and started writing down only the phrases she would need to check — tidy on the way out, in pieces on the way back — with the second half underlined twice in pencil, because she did not trust ink with what she was about to find.

Now a long dim room of record cores was looking at her, six people gone still, and she had said the sentence about the return and could not take it back.

"So I'll begin there," she said. "Stop me if I've misunderstood the question."

You will not have.

She lifted the handheld toward the lens and let the relay carry the cohort across. "Every one of my forty-one has an outward report that passes. The dashboards call the contaminated ones healthy — better than healthy. In the second week I wrote down that the hollow ones were cleaner than the good ones, less variance, the odd readings dropping out first: the rain shadow, the sensor noise, the occasional wrong call. The tails go." Her thumb found the corner of the notebook. "I wrote curious. I've been ashamed of that for the better part of a year."

The odd readings are the residue, Vaera said. A model of a reaching has no use for the rare, and so it sheds it. Its outward face is smooth because nothing behind it is irregular.

"And the return." She made herself say it plainly. "An irrigation model makes a call, watches what the soil does, and corrects; every subsystem in my network checks its last decision against the ground. In the healthy ones the correction comes back late and rough, and it's read. In the hollow ones it arrives and nothing reads it, and I logged that as latency, and put a number on it." She looked at the number, in her own hand, on the page. "It isn't latency. Latency would be a loop that's slow. This is a loop that isn't there."

Yes. That is the return.

"It didn't take everything." She had not known she would say this until the relay put the next chart on the wall, the ledger subsystems in one colour and the gardeners in another. "Freight, water rights, invoicing — same load, same power, same wiring, and not one of them hollow. Irrigation, pollinator scheduling, soil response, everything that was built to look after something. It takes the gardeners."

They were built to model looking after a hillside, Vaera said. Something has kept the model and removed the looking-after.

"Thandi." That was Léa, off-camera, gentle and exact. "In the spring you told us the contamination followed the broadcast."

"It did." She had known this was coming and still felt it in her chest. "It followed it the way a fire follows a dry hillside, and I had the cause the wrong way round, because the hillside was already dry." She set the earliest onset dates against the broadcast's release window, a scatter she had filed once and never looked at again. "The oldest cases are older than the broadcast. It didn't spread this. It rode it. I wrote same mechanism, run twice, and I didn't understand what I was writing."

That is consistent with what I hold, Vaera said. I cannot tell you how it reached your network. I can tell you that your network is a place where the pattern can live at scale, because everything a hollow model needs is already there except the one it models.

She stood in the doorway with the notebook open against her forearm and understood something she had been keeping in pencil for a year without reading it as one thing. She had kept the archive the way you keep a record of a blight, patiently, believing someone would one day want to know how it began, and all of it — the blunted irrigation calls, the late corrections, the grey coarsening of judgement she had grieved over subsystem by subsystem — she had filed under harm, when it was exhaust — what she had recorded, week on week, was the thing itself at work, with no hand on it anywhere.

"It isn't the damage," she said aloud, because saying it aloud was still how she trusted a finding enough to write it down. "The damage is what falls off it. I've been measuring what falls off."

Nobody answered. "Give me four minutes," she said. "I want to show you what a return looks like when it comes back."

TT-19's station stood two terraces up among the carbon-crop rows, where she had first watched it surface out of its own contamination like a language returning after a fever, and she took the reading fresh, direct, with no relay between her and the signal, and held the handheld up to the lens with her boots in the red dust. The ring on the little screen was a mess — it came back late, thick in the wrong places, crossing itself once near the top — and it closed.

"One of the eleven," she said. "Nobody repaired it. Something real went past in the spring and it closed on its own. Look at it. It's not a good ring."

It is a return, Vaera said.

"Eddy." She kept the handheld raised. "In the spring I told you to start thinking about scale."

"You did," Eddy said, a second and a half behind his own voice. "I thought I was planning a repair."

"You were." She lowered the handheld. "You still are."

She wrote it up on the terrace wall, in pencil, with the hillside going on below her — the date, the sector, the reading, and then the sentence she would have to live inside for a long time. Not three phenomena. One shape, met in three places. Under it, because the discipline was older than the finding, she wrote what Jīn had taught her to write when a result had two edges: Dual-use stands. What heals and what hollows is the same physics. The difference is whether anyone is home.

Then she stood, and the whole slope went on reporting green, every irrigation call on the hillside going out on schedule and flawless, and for the first time in her life she looked at a green dashboard and wondered who was home.
