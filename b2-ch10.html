The Narrow Gate · Chapter 10

It knows them

The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

10

28 MarchAur'kthe Gate floorMars delay 18.3 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY AND THE POLDER / Aur'k — Mars 18.3 min

The first amber of the afternoon came up on the map wall at 12:41, in Harlem, and by 12:43 a crew was rolling toward a woman who had not yet put down her fork.

HEAT — HARLEM — 4F — SEND 12:43 — ONSET 13:01.

Aur'k read it from the head of the row and went back to his seams. The column had been on the right edge of the wall since Monday, amber tags down the side of the city, and the floor had stopped turning to look at it by Tuesday noon.

The Gate only wrote the strips. A dispatcher at the far desk read them out, and a crew went, and a woman in Harlem would open her door at ten to one to two paramedics she hadn't called, and be annoyed, and then sit down.

The crews had a name for it already: an early call.

Outside it was thirty-two and wet with it. The harbour air had come in over the wall at dawn and sat down on the city like a dog on a bed. The flood glass sweated on its inner face. The floor smelled of coffee and ozone, and of thirty people who had walked from the trains through air you could wring.

The work was a dead sail coming apart. Thursday's storm had cooked its controllers, and since then it had been shedding: a hinge, a strut, a panel the size of a tennis court, each on its own slow way down. The floor walked them into the Atlantic as they came. Nobody would cheer it.

Lin had attitude, her edge a blade in a shut drawer. Dev counted pieces before they broke. Moss had the air, wide and grey and moving. Joaquín had the burns, and his edge was warm and full of people, as always.

Almost as always.

Aur'k found it on his second block, as a tongue finds a missing tooth: a smooth place in Joaquín's strand where the noise should have been. Cousins, a radio, his aunt's kitchen, the floor's jokes, all crowded up to the edge of it and stopped. Inside, nothing. Not dark. Not warm. Smooth, like a seam ground flat.

It didn't lean. It didn't move. It held the burns as well as the rest of him did.

"Chair four," Aur'k said. "Edge holding."

"Always," said Joaquín, eyes shut. Then, still in the link, as if asking about weather: "What was it like? Thursday. Malé. Everyone says I flew it."

"You were logged off."

"That's what they say too." He put a burn in, four seconds, clean. "I don't remember taking my coat off. Isn't that funny? Twelve years I wait for thirty-four in March, and I don't remember."

At forty minutes Aur'k lifted out on his own clock and stood at the rail while the night lead read the row for twenty. That was the rule now, Rachel's: forty on, twenty out, at this weight. He spent the twenty with his plating ticking down and his attention on four seams he wasn't allowed to hold.

· · ·

Joaquín read his aunt's message out between blocks, standing at the rail with his handheld at arm's length because he wouldn't admit he needed the lenses.

"Two boys from the ambulance at my door at ten past twelve," he translated. "I told them I was perfectly well. I sat down on the step to tell them." He looked up. "She sat down on the step."

"How is she?" said the night lead.

"Insulted." He scrolled. "They gave me water. They took my pressure, which nobody has done without asking me since your uncle. I gave them the flan I was making for Sunday, and now I want to know who sent them, so I can complain."

The floor laughed. Even Lin, in chair one, smiled with her eyes shut.

"Tell her it was the Gate," said Dev.

"She'll want its address."

"Tell her it's under the harbour."

"She'll come." Joaquín put the handheld away. "She'll bring a spoon."

Half the Battery had stood in white for her daughter in July. Aur'k could not eat flan. He found that he wanted the woman to have sat on the step, and been insulted, and be alive to bring a spoon, more than he had wanted most things that week.

His last block ended at 14:40. The rule ended his day at three now, and the evening lead ran the row from there with nobody at its head, as the floor had before he came.

At 14:52 a call came in the old way.

A voice on a handset at the dispatcher's desk, not a strip. A woman, out of breath, saying a street name twice. The dispatcher said, "Is he breathing?" and then, "Keep him in the shade. Get him wet. Anything."

On the wall a white tag came up in the grey of the Shallows, past the green of Broad Channel, on a stilt street the relays reached and nobody on it paid for. No amber beside it.

"Old man," said the night lead. "On his porch, in the sun. Neighbour thinks since noon. Nearest crew's on an early call. Nineteen minutes."

The stilt street was eleven minutes by pod from the Battery's kerb. Aur'k was off the row. Nobody had to sign for where he went.

"I'll go," he said. He signed his key in at the rail, and went.

· · ·

He left the pod's cooling off. The ration was for people. The Shallows came up grey and green through the window, the road on its causeway over water the colour of tea, then a turning onto boards.

Thirty kilometres from his racks he was a half-beat behind his own hands, as if he were reaching through water.

The stilt street stood a storey up out of the marsh on posts black with tar, a row of porches facing the water, every house open to the air because the air was all there was. A neighbour in a wet dress was on the third porch with a bucket. The old man lay on the boards beside her with his shirt soaked through and his eyes half open, breathing fast and shallow, and his skin was hot and not sweating under the water she'd poured on him. Aur'k knew what that meant.

"Are you the ambulance?" said the neighbour.

"No. It's coming." He knelt, and the boards gave under a weight they hadn't been built for. "Shade."

There was no shade on the porch. There was shade under it, at the foot of the stair, where the tide had left the mud cool and black between the posts.

He picked the old man up. It was nothing, a man that light. It was the easiest thing his body had done all day. Then he felt the heat of his own chest plate go into the man's wet shirt, and stopped on the second step.

He was hot. He had been hot since the floor, and the pod had been hot, and the air was nearly as hot as his plating. Holding a man with a core already past forty against plating near the same was not holding him. It was another sun.

He put the old man down on the stair, in the strip of shade the porch made, and let go of him. He had never in his life been a worse thing to be held by.

"You," he said to the neighbour. "Hold his head. Wet him. Keep wetting him."

She did. He stood a step below in the sun, useless, hands open, and fanned the man with a flattened carton because it was what there was.

At the end of the boards, where the stilt street met the causeway, an ambulance went by on the road. Lights, no siren. It didn't turn. It would be on an early call to a linked block a kilometre on, where somebody's tags had gone amber twenty minutes before their body knew. He watched it go.

The crew came at 15:11, nineteen minutes after the call, and carried the old man down the last steps on a board, and put a line in his arm on the mud, and read him out to the radio. Unresponsive. Core 41.6. They took him. Transported, critical.

The neighbour sat down on the stair with the bucket between her feet.

"Thank you," she said, to Aur'k, because he was there.

"I didn't do anything."

"You came," she said, as if that were the thing, and he didn't have an answer.

· · ·

By the time he was back at the Battery the evening block had the row. He stood at the head of it a moment, with no clock over him and nothing to hold.

The night lead was signing out. "Oh. Inwood says thank you."

"Inwood?"

"Last night. Early call, half past nine. The crew caught her on the stairs going down for water. They put a note on the strip." She tapped her slate shut. She glanced at the clock column, and then didn't. "Logged to reader. Evening block must have patched you in."

At half past nine last night he had been at the kitchen table in the flat with Kaelin, who had been telling him what the physio said about his hips.

"Thank you," he said.

He turned right, to his panel, instead of left to the lifts.

· · ·

He had not opened the shift log since the ninth of February.

He opened it now, and read it as he read gauges, flat, every line, starting from the ninth of February, because that was where his own name sat under a note he had written.

15:13:47 — Boundary. Chair three, edge holding. Lift at fifteen-twenty. And in the note field beside it, over his own name: timestamp error, log clock. Aur'k.

He read past it.

The second of March, 19:04, four hours after he had left the floor: Chair two, edge holding. Lift at nineteen ten. The evening lead had lifted Raman at 19:10. Raman had not bled.

The sixth. The eleventh, twice. Every evening of the last week. What, where, how it stood, and when he would lift it, with the number spoken as a word, as he spoke numbers. The floor's other voices wrote digits. The Gate's own clipped register wrote digits. Only one voice on the floor said fifteen-twenty.

These lines carried READER in the head field.

Then Monday, when the strips began, and the same voice in a column where it had no business to be.

Harlem, second floor, holding. Send at twenty-one oh-four. Monday night.

Polder, ninth floor, holding. Send at twenty-two forty. Monday again.

Inwood, fourth floor, holding. Send at twenty-one thirty-two. Last night.

He set his own record beside the log and ran them together, second by second, to see where he had been.

His record had him at the table at 21:32. The pump house hummed in the table's legs. Kaelin said, Another year, she reckons. I told her I'd be dead of boredom in six months, and laughed, and Dae-jung's board leaned against the salt with its second nut one square off.

Every second of it was there. No gap at 21:32. No gap anywhere that night. He had not been anywhere else.

He searched his record for Inwood, fourth floor. He had never said it.

He read the strips again. Every one of them was right. Whatever was making these calls was making them as he would have made them, and making them well, and it was doing it while he sat at a kitchen table being told about hips.

It was his judgement. It was going on without him.

He considered, for some time, the note with his name on it. Timestamp error, log clock. He had written it in February because it was the likeliest thing and because he had wanted it to be. It had not been the likeliest thing. He had known it was not by the third time he read the line, and he had filed it anyway, over his name.

He sent the evening's lines to the printer by the coffee machine. Forty-one of them, since February. They came out warm.

Paper didn't get classified. He folded the sheets once, put them inside his chest plate where the key rode on shift, and went to find Rachel.

· · ·

Her room at the end of the glass was hotter than the floor and had given up pretending otherwise. A desk fan stood on the camp bed and turned its head from wall to wall, moving the heat from one side of the room to the other. Her jacket hung on the back of the door.

She sat at the desk in her shirtsleeves with a slate in front of her and her cuffs buttoned, in this heat, both of them.

On the glass outside her door the grease-pencil count had gone up again since the sail.

"Aur'k." She didn't get up.

He took the folded sheets out of his chest plate and laid them on her desk, and squared them to its edge with two fingers.

"Forty-one decisions after my logoffs," he said. "Since the ninth of February. Seam calls on the evening block. Since Monday, heat calls. In my phrasing. The head field says reader. I was at home for every one of them, and I have the record to show it."

She drew the sheets to her and read.

He watched her read. That was his trade too: the shape of a person's attention, where it leaned, where it held. Rachel's was usually all front, a woman walking into weather. Tonight it was braced along one side, very hard, as Kaelin's had been at the start of the month, with a question behind it. Her left hand had gone to her right cuff before she reached the bottom of the first page. It stayed there.

She was not surprised. He saw that clearly, and saw that she knew more than the paper told her.

He did not ask. He had things of his own he wasn't saying.

"They're right," she said, at the end.

"Every one."

"Then tell me what's wrong with them."

"I wasn't there." He waited until she looked up. "I want to be the only one who makes my calls. Stop it answering in my voice when I've gone. Or take me off."

"If I stop it," she said, "what goes?"

"I don't know what goes."

"I do." She turned the sheets to face him and put her finger on a line, then another, as she would have put it on a street. "Harlem, second floor, Monday. A man with a bad heart and his window shut against the noise. Polder, ninth floor, Monday too. A girl of nineteen studying, who didn't drink all day."

The finger moved. "Inwood. Seventy-eight. Going down four flights for water because her tap had gone warm."

"You know them."

"I read the crew notes. Every night. It's what I do instead of sleeping." She took her finger off the page. "They're alive, Aur'k. On your calls."

"Not mine."

"In your voice."

"And the man in the stilt street wasn't in it at all." He heard his own voice go flat, gauge-flat. "Nineteen minutes, because nobody had laced him. Core forty-one point six. I picked him up, and I was too hot to hold him. It saw a woman in Inwood going down for water because it could see her. I don't know what decides the rest. I don't like it deciding in my voice."

She said nothing.

"And Joaquín," he said. "There's a place in him that's smooth. Thursday afternoon. He holds the burns right up to it."

"He's in the green."

"He's in the green with a piece gone."

"I know," Rachel said, and that was all she said about it.

Rachel didn't answer at once. He let her have the time, as he had let the night lead have it in February.

"No," she said. "Neither do I."

She looked past him, through the glass of her door, at the floor: the four chairs lying back in a row, the evening block under its clocks, the medic with the cup at the rail.

"A hundred and twenty-seven days," she said. "Stay through the Fall."

"You're asking."

"I'm asking. I'll put it on paper if you want it on paper." Her hand was still on her cuff. "Four chairs and you at the head of them. That's the floor I built. I can't build another one by August."

"And on the third?"

"Ask me on the third."

He looked at the sheets. Forty-one lines in a voice that was his, saying the right things to the right people at the right time, a little after he had gone home. Somewhere a man on a porch in a wet shirt with a drip in his arm. Somewhere a woman of seventy-eight asleep with a glass of cold water beside her bed.

You'd tell me if it was going wrong, Kaelin had said, at the kitchen table, and he had answered that nothing had. It had been true by the width of a timestamp. It would not be true tonight.

"I'll stay," he said.

"Thank you." She said it flat, a reading off a gauge. Then, as he reached for the sheets: "Tell Drake what you like."

She said it easily. He heard how easily, and it was too easy, a door shut so smoothly it didn't touch the frame. He held her eyes. She held his, pale, as still as she was in meetings.

He didn't ask that either.

He folded the sheets once along their old crease and put them back inside his chest plate.

· · ·

The flat was twenty-nine at eight at night with the window open, and the window seal had stopped mattering because the whole harbour was in the room anyway, warm and weedy and smelling of the wall's wet stone. The radiator was off. Kaelin's field jacket hung on it from habit, cold.

Kaelin was at the table in a vest, his stick against the table's edge, doing the physio's exercises for his hands with a rubber ball and swearing at it.

"Dust take you," he told the ball. "You're all mafan."

On the table, under the salt, the March rota lay folded in three, AUR'K — READER at the head of every block. Dae-jung's board leaned against the salt with its two nuts and its washer.

Aur'k set the kettle on, because Kaelin would want tea in an hour and would not get up for it, and stood at the counter while it began to tick.

"Good day?" said Kaelin.

"Yes."

It was. The sail had come down in pieces into the sea, and nobody had bled.

"Joaquín's aunt got an ambulance before she knew she needed one," he said. "Two boys at her door at ten past twelve. She told them she was perfectly well and sat down on the step to tell them. She gave them the flan she was making for Sunday. Now she wants to complain to whoever sent them."

Kaelin laughed, the short wrecked laugh, and dropped the ball. It rolled across the table and stopped against the salt.

"Who sent them?"

"The Gate."

"She'll want its address."

"That was Dev's line."

"Dev's right." Kaelin reached for the ball and didn't get it, and left it. "Sit down, mate. You're hovering."

"The chair won't take me."

"None of them will. Sit on the floor, then. I'll come down to you."

Aur'k did not sit. He took the kettle off before it whistled.

Paper lived with paper. The month lived under the salt, and he would see it there every morning before he went, and so would nobody else, because nobody else read rotas. When Kaelin pushed himself up on the stick and went down the short hall to the bathroom, slow on his knees all the way, Aur'k crossed to the table.

He lifted the salt. He took the March rota out from under it and unfolded it, and laid the folded sheets inside, flat against the inner page, and folded the rota over them in three along its own creases, as the floor had printed it. He put it back where it had been and set the salt on top of it, square.

From the hall, the tap ran and stopped.

When Kaelin came back he stood a moment in the doorway with one hand on the frame. He looked at the table, and the salt, and the ball against it, and then at Aur'k by the counter, for longer than the answer had needed.

"Good day, then," he said.

He sat down heavily and picked up the ball.

On the table the salt stood square on the rota, and the rota lay square on the wood, and inside it, folded in three with the month's chairs, forty-one lines in a voice that was Aur'k's, a little after he had gone home.
