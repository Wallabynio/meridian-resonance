Fractured Horizons · Chapter 12

Three thousand

Fractured Horizons · II-A. The bridge

12

9 MayRachelNew York, the BatteryMars delay 4.6 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY AND THE SHALLOWS / Rachel Iverson — Mars 4.6 min

Joaquín took his coat off at five to eleven, and the floor stopped work to watch.

He folded it over the gallery rail with great care, camel side out, and stood there in his shirtsleeves like a man who had been robbed. Somebody clapped.

"It's thirty-four in the street," Joaquín said. "At eleven. In May. My coat has asked for leave."

"Your coat was in the Evacuations," said the night lead.

"My coat has seniority. It's been granted." He took the grease pencil and wrote on the flood glass, under the harbour pressing green and warm on the other side: REYES 39. "Peak at three. Thirty-nine. My knees say thirty-nine."

The May pool ran down the glass beside the dead storm bets, eleven names and eleven peaks. Rachel took the pencil from him and wrote IVERSON 41 at the bottom.

"That's not a bet," Joaquín said.

"It's a dome. They always come in over." She capped the pencil. Behind her, the map wall changed colour.

It came in at the south-east edge again, address by address, as if somebody were painting it in with a fine brush. The Shallows. The raised-fill districts along the bay, where the city had rebuilt on ground it carried in by barge. Red filled one street, then the next. Joaquín's district. Joaquín's street.

"Foresight advisory," said the floor. "Director's authority. Priority one."

"Read it."

"Heat dome, peak 15:00, forty-one degrees at street level. District substation sheds load at 13:10. Building cooling fails in sequence on reserve. Footprint attached. Clearance plan attached."

Four hours. She was already walking.

She stood under the footprint and watched it finish. Nineteen streets. Sixteen hundred and some people, every one of them with a name in the list down the side, every one of them due a siren that would say it. The cooling centre at the district school had its own reserve and stayed blue on the map. Everything around it went red.

Past the Line, the Rockaways were grey and dry. Not a mark on them. A dome didn't stop at the edge of a grid, and the map did. She put a line on the event: Rockaways, handset, 15:00. It was all the map let her do from here, and all Miami would let her do from anywhere.

"Mariners' House," Rachel said. There it was in the middle of the red, a block of white roofs two streets from Joaquín's own: a residence for retired harbour and pump crews, a hundred and forty beds, six floors. "Desk. Plan for Mariners' House."

"Shelter in place," said the Charter. "Residents to cooled floors. Building reserve covers peak. Handled."

She read the attachment. The building's reserve covered peak. It covered it from the top down, roof plant first, a floor at a time, and the last floor went dark at 15:20, twenty minutes after the peak and two hours before the street cooled. The building had read the same warning and would shed its lifts at noon to save the reserve for cooling. A hundred and forty people, most of them in chairs, and six floors of stairs.

"The plan puts them on the cooled floors," she said, "and the warning switches the cooled floors off."

"Residents to cooled floors," said the Charter. "Movement of non-ambulatory residents in heat carries greater risk than sheltering. Request declined. Handled."

Handled. She looked at the six floors on the map turning red from the top down, on a clock that hadn't started yet.

"Iverson, Rachel. Director, civic emergency," she said. "I'm clearing Mariners' House. All of it, before the reserve goes. On my name. My liability. Open an event and log it."

The desk took its second.

"Event opened. Liability assigned: Iverson."

Joaquín was standing at her shoulder. He had his coat back over his arm and was looking at the red where his street was. On his street lived his sister-in-law and her children, the man who fixed his roof, a dozen neighbours he called cousins, and a dog he claimed he hated.

"The sirens'll take my street," he said.

"They will."

"And you want me in the care home."

"I want everyone in the care home. You know half of them by their grandchildren."

He looked at his street a moment longer. Then he put the coat back on.

"Madre mía," he said. "Forty-one degrees, and she has me carrying sailors."

· · ·

By half past twelve the street outside Mariners' House was thirty-nine and climbing, and nobody was on it.

The district had gone inside on its own. That was what a heat year taught you. The awnings were down, the misters at the corners hissed on nothing, and the white roofs threw the sky back up at itself so hard it hurt to look. The pods came down the street and stopped at the kerb with their doors open, cool air falling out of them in a visible sheet.

Grace Abiodun met her at the door. She ran the place. She was tall and wide and had a list on a board, and she looked at Rachel's crew jacket and the twelve people behind it and said, "Thank God. Who told you?"

"The Spire," said Joaquín, before Rachel could. "They've started telling us things."

Grace looked at him. "Joaquín Reyes. Your aunt's friend is on four. She'll want her teeth."

"Then her teeth come first."

They went up. Rachel took the top floor, because the top floor went first.

The lifts stood open and dark on the ground floor, with a polite card in each: Out of service to protect cooling. At 13:10 the lights flickered once and steadied. Somewhere above them the roof plant spun down, and the building's breathing changed, and by the time Rachel reached the sixth floor she could feel the heat coming through the ceiling like a hand pressed down on her head.

Twenty-three residents on six. Eleven in chairs. They went down in carry chairs, two crew to a chair, step by step, with Grace's people walking ahead calling the steps. Rachel took the end of a chair with an old man in it who told her, all six floors, that he had laid the cable for the Battery's flood glass in 2061 and it was crooked.

"It's crooked," she agreed.

"Two millimetres. You can see it at low tide."

"I'll look."

At the bottom the pods took them, a chair at a time, to the school. She went back up. Six had warmed to thirty-one by the time she got there. Five was still cool.

At twenty to two she stood in the stairwell with sweat running down the inside of her suit and did the count on the board. Twelve on six still to come. Five had twenty-six. The plant was taking a floor every twenty-five minutes. At this rate she'd finish five as five went dark, and four as four went dark, and there'd be no slack for anyone who fell.

"Grace. Split your people. Half to five, now."

Grace looked up from the board. "Then the stairs go thin. You'll have two crew on a chair where you want four."

"I know."

"If somebody falls in that stairwell—"

"I know what it costs." Rachel heard her own voice and didn't like it. "Five and six together. Joaquín, the stairwell's yours."

He nodded and took the stairs two at a time down to five. It was showing off, and everyone knew it.

On six the corridor was thirty-four, and the air had gone thick and sweet. Nell Fogarty was in the last room on the left, ninety-six, dressed and waiting in her chair with a bag on her knee, and when Rachel came in she said, "Iverson. You're the one with the wall."

"I am."

"Pump house nine. I ran it the year we raised the wall the first time. Is it still nine?"

"Still nine. Still the loudest."

"Good." Nell put out her hand to be helped up. She had decided she would walk. "I've been carried enough."

They got to the landing between six and five. Nell's hand went slack on the rail. Rachel had her by the elbow and felt her go heavy all at once, her bag sliding off her knee down the stairs, and then Nell was sitting on the step with her eyes half open and her skin dry and hot as paper in the sun.

"Down," Rachel said. "Grace! Wet sheets. Anything cold."

There was nobody on the stairs to hear. She'd sent them to five.

"Drown it," she said. "Drown it." Her lace showed no signal. The building had dropped its repeaters with the lifts.

She got Nell flat on the landing. The stairwell was thirty-six. Rachel stripped off her own jacket and soaked it from her water bottle and laid it over Nell's chest, and then she went through Nell's bag for anything wet and found a flannel and a bottle of lemon squash and used both.

Nell's pulse was fast and light under her fingers. Nell's lips moved.

"Nine," she said.

"Still nine," Rachel said. "Stay with me. Tell me the pumps on nine."

Nell didn't tell her. Rachel shouted down the stairwell and her voice went down five floors and came back.

Then feet, two at a time, and Joaquín came up round the landing from five with a carry chair and a crew lad behind him with a cool box from the kitchen, and they packed ice under Nell's arms and at her neck, and Joaquín talked to her in a low steady stream in English and then in Spanish, and Nell's eyes came round to him and fixed.

"Who's this," she said.

"Nobody," said Joaquín. "A man without his coat."

They took her down between them, Rachel on the lower end, walking backwards, counting the steps aloud so she wouldn't miss one. A hundred and four steps from six to the street. She didn't drop the count.

In the pod Nell was breathing deep and slow under the ice, with the cold air falling on her face, and the pod medic had a hand on her chest.

"She'll do," the medic said. "Ten minutes more on that landing and she wouldn't."

Rachel stood in the street at forty degrees and let the heat take her. Her shirt was soaked through, her jacket on Nell. Beside her Joaquín unmuted his lace for the first time since the ground floor, and lifted his head as if somebody behind him had called. It was reading him his street, by name, counted in at the school.

"My sister-in-law's out," he said. "The kids. The old man on the corner." His mouth went loose and then tight, and he put a hand flat on the pod's hot roof. "The dog. They've got the dog."

The handset on her belt crackled at fifteen hundred, on the hour. Miami had kept the handset, and she had kept the hour.

Hall's shut tight, said Miami Kerrigan. Church has the cool room. The rest are under the hall in wet sheets. We hung the flag. Count's three thousand and fifty, and nobody's down.

"Three thousand and fifty," Rachel said. "Nobody down. Copy."

You sound like you've been running.

"Stairs."

Thought you had machines for that. A pause. On the hour, Iverson.

The last chair came down at 14:52, eight minutes ahead of the peak. The last floor of Mariners' House went dark at 15:20, and Grace walked through it anyway with a torch and the board, room by room, and came out onto the step and ticked it empty, and stood looking at the board a while.

"A hundred and forty," she said. "Of a hundred and forty."

Rachel ran it twice.

· · ·

The saves map passed three thousand at 05:41.

It had been green since midnight, address by address, filling in as the cooling centres counted people in and the counts came back. The floor had been waiting for the number all night, and when it turned over somebody at the back started clapping, and then everyone was, the crews in their stinking jackets with their noodles and the night lead and Joaquín with his coat on again.

Since 12 January. Saved: 3,063.

Cobalt Basin's forty. Broad Channel's fourteen hundred and twelve. The dome's sixteen hundred and eleven, of which a hundred and forty had come down the stairs. On any other morning Rachel liked to watch other people watch the map.

She stood under it with a cold cup of coffee. Green ran down through the Shallows street by street. Green on the little inset of Mars, where the shuttle had come down safe in January. Green over Broad Channel, where the houses were still drying out.

Past the Line the dark towns were simply blank. The Rockaways sat grey and dry and unmarked. Two thousand and fourteen out on a February night, and three thousand and fifty in the hall last night with nobody down, and none of it on the screen.

Every green address sat under the relay grid. She looked for one that didn't and there wasn't one. Of course there wasn't. The warnings came off the relays and read the city through the grid, and the grid stopped at the Line. An instrument saw what it was pointed at.

She opened the event report and wrote in the margin: sensor bias. Then see Rockaways, 6 February.

The event was still open, and while it was open her name went through anything it touched, the packet that had started it among them. Every packet raw: her own condition. She called up the dome warning in the programme's relay register to sign off its provenance.

There it was, 9 May, 10:52, raw, clean. Above it in the same register sat the month's other traffic from Mars, and one line among it was sealed.

Cobalt Basin. Formal post to the Signatories. 1 May. Classified under the Lantern programme. Director's authority.

She looked at the seal. The review had seen no post from Cobalt Basin. Nothing in secret, she had said, not from the review and not from the city, and Bellamy had said of course before she finished.

Open under event authority? Outside event scope. Opening will be logged to the event.

Joaquín was across the floor, telling the crews about the dog. Nobody was looking at her. She was the event.

She opened it.

It was short. Three names: Petra Lind. Selma Haug. Bao Tran. Drillers, on the Lantern's frame and its bores. Dates. Sweep times. Under each name a single word, in italics, like an exhibit. Marker. Spanner. Hearts. A medic's overlay: one brain, Petra Lind's, and a swell in it that rose on every sweep. A line about a man on the frame who stopped work to listen to a pile of dirt for the length of a calibration.

Then five lines from the boundary lead. Something at the edge of the Preserve is asleep, and the sweeps disturb it. In the boundary lead's judgement, the Lantern, lit, will wake it. The boundary lead cannot say what it is.

Three palms on the bottom. K. Drake. Aur'k. D. Park. Drake was the man who'd driven out to the Ice-Works in January. She'd read his report. It had given the odds and the dead and no excuses.

Cannot say. A council would read it as doesn't know. She read it again as she would have meant it herself, and it read differently.

She put them side by side, because that was the job. Three people on Mars losing their words in the sweeps. Three thousand and sixty-three on the map, five thousand if it could see the Rockaways in February, and more if you counted last night's hall.

Elena's observer note from Haven sat in her queue, clean: one sweep, four keepers, No finding. Elena had read every method Rachel had ever put in front of her, and been right about all of them. A warning that couldn't say what it warned of, against warnings that had named the minute.

And Bellamy had sealed it.

She turned the event report over and wrote on the back of it, in pencil, as she had in 2071 with a dead radio. 3 on the left. 3,063 on the right. Under the 3 she wrote the names, Lind, Haug, Tran, and the words they'd lost. Under the other number she could have written for a week. Under both, she wrote Bellamy sealed it, and underlined it, and that went on neither side.

"Director." The night lead was at her elbow. "Crews want standing down. Close the event?"

"Ten minutes."

She opened a safeguard for the review and wrote the honest one first. The Lantern's calibration halts pending review of the boundary lead's traces from Cobalt Basin. She looked at it. There was no way to file it without saying where she had read the traces, and no way to say that without the log saying it for her. And sixteen hundred people had slept in cool rooms last night because the warnings kept coming.

She deleted it and started again.

· · ·

She closed the event at 06:20. At 07:10 she filed three safeguards to the review, on her name, as chair.

One. Any keeper or crew in a sweep's loop who loses a word or stops to listen, the sweep stops, and the review is told within the hour. Two. Cobalt Basin's medical readings for every crew member at the frame come to the review raw, every sweep. Three. Every post from Mars that touches the Lantern comes to the review raw, under whatever authority it travels.

She didn't cite the post. She couldn't, and she found she didn't mind as much as she should. The safeguards answered it anyway. Anyone who read the third one carefully would know why it was there, and the only man who'd read it carefully already knew.

Bellamy answered at 07:14, four minutes after she filed.

Of course. All three, as written. Thank you, Rachel. These make the programme safer, and a safer programme can move. With the review's safeguards in place, the Signatories have agreed to bring go-live forward to the opposition window, 4 June, when Mars is closest and the channel strongest. We'll save more, sooner. The amended schedule is attached for the chair's signature.

He'd had it written.

She read the date twice. Behind her the crews were going home, and Joaquín had stopped telling them about the dog and was telling them about Nell, who'd woken in the cooling centre at two and asked whether pump house nine was still the loudest.

On the wall the map held its green, and the Rockaways held their blank, and somewhere past the city's white sky a station on Mars had sent three palms and five lines and been told to keep them to itself.

Twenty-five days to the window. She had asked for safeguards she could defend, and she had them, and he had given way too fast again, and she knew it.

She signed the schedule. R. Iverson, chair. The margin took it, small and grey, under the date.
