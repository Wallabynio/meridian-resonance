CORPORATE UNION — SÃO VERDE, VERDANT CITADEL / Daniel Cruz

The terraces smelled of fertiliser and warm soil, the way they always did an hour before the irrigation cycle, and underneath that, if you knew to look for it, the faint ozone tang of equipment that had no business running behind a monoculture facade. Daniel had grown up two blocks from what used to be Copacabana and had learned this smell before he'd learned most other things about the city that grew up over it — the particular chemistry of a resistance hub disguised as a vertical farm, engineered greenery stacked forty storeys into what had once been a hillside slum and was now one of São Verde's showcase agricultural developments, terrace after terrace of soy and cassava and manioc climbing toward a sky gone the flat pewter of late afternoon rain. Nobody who audited the Citadel's yield reports had ever found anything wrong with them. That was rather the point.

He was three cells deep into a routing check when the manifest node chimed — a small sound, almost apologetic, tucked into the ambient hum of the hydroponic pumps so that anyone without the ear for it would have missed it entirely.

"Incoming packet. Origin: masked. Priority: unflagged."

Unflagged meant someone had gone to real trouble to make it look like nothing. Daniel pulled it up on the terrace-side terminal, a battered thing built from three generations of scavenged Corporate Union hardware and running software none of that hardware had ever been licensed to run, and read the payload once, fast, the way he read everything the first time — for shape, not detail. Then he read it again, slower, the way his old handler had taught him before she'd been purged in a compliance sweep he still didn't have the full story on: for what it cost the person who sent it.

Entangled-channel telemetry. Three feeds, all drifting the same sliver of a millisecond, all drifting toward Luna Haven. A New Manhattan surveillance signature buried in the routing metadata that told him, without needing a name attached, exactly what kind of institution this data had walked out of.

His hands were already moving. Ink stayed under his nails these days from an old habit he'd never quite broken — a paper log he kept for nothing that mattered and everything that did, the physical half of a practice his handler had drilled into him until it stopped feeling like a choice: anything worth knowing is worth writing down somewhere a server sweep can't reach. He didn't know who'd sent this. He knew what it meant that someone inside a Surveillance Spire had chosen, at some cost he could only guess at, to send it to him rather than file it and forget it.

He compressed a summary — the raw drift figures, the direction, nothing that would identify a source that hadn't identified itself — and split it three ways before he'd let himself think too hard about whether he should. One packet to the Nairobi cell, folded into a supply manifest for greenhouse components that would cross four jurisdictions without anyone reading past the first line. One to the Neo Singapore relay, riding the same open-science traffic Eddy Okafor used, though Daniel didn't know Eddy's name yet and wouldn't for months. One to a dead-drop node in the Mediterranean Zone that existed for this and nothing else.

"Distribution complete," the terminal said, in the clipped procedural register every piece of Corporate Union-descended hardware defaulted to whether or not you'd asked it to sound that way. "Three point one seconds."

Three seconds, and no single point of failure. If one cell burned, the other two survived it, and neither of them could give up what they'd never been told. It was the only kind of safety Daniel had ever fully trusted — not concealment, which failed the moment someone looked hard enough, but redundancy, which failed only if everything failed at once, and even the Corporate Union's surveillance architecture hadn't managed that yet.

He archived the original the old way. Not the manifest node, not the terrace terminal, not anything that could be found by a network sweep no matter how deep it ran — a physical drive, unconnected, dropped into a lead-lined case behind a false panel in the irrigation control room, sealed the way you'd seal something you expected to need again in a context you couldn't yet picture. His handler had taught him that too, in the last month before the sweep took her: the people who built the eyes that watch everything also know exactly how to find everything that's plugged in. Don't be findable that way. Be findable the slow way, if you have to be findable at all.

He didn't know, sealing the case, what he was keeping. A leak from inside the Corporate Union's own surveillance apparatus, plainly — but leaks came and went, most of them nothing, filed and forgotten within a week. This one had a shape to it he couldn't name yet, the same shape the fertiliser smell and the ozone tang underneath it had: two things occupying the same space, one a cover for the other, and no way yet to say which was which.

Outside, the irrigation cycle began its slow hiss across the upper terraces, water running down through forty storeys of engineered soil toward roots that had never known a hillside slum, in a city that had built something genuinely good over something it had also, somewhere else, quietly refused to fix. Daniel closed the false panel, wiped the ink from the side of his hand, and went back to the routing check he'd been three cells deep into before the node chimed — because the work didn't stop for one good delivery, and he had learned a long time ago not to expect it to.

CORPORATE UNION — GULF CRESCENT, LOGISTICS COMMAND / Amita Rao

The launch gantries were still visible from the Logistics Command's upper catwalk if you knew where to look — pale verticals standing out past the container stacks at the far southern end of the arc, thirty kilometres down the lock line, where the Crescent gave up being a wall against the sea and became a place for leaving the planet instead. Most of the staff who worked this floor had never once watched a transfer go up; the spaceport was simply the last stop on a routing code, the same as any dock. Amita found she liked it better this way. A structure built to hold back a gulf and throw cargo at the sky, doing the unglamorous, essential work of making sure the right box arrived at the right dock on the right day — for a world that had stopped reaching quite so far, and still needed the boxes to arrive.

She'd been at the same clipboard-worn workstation for six months, cross-checking container manifests against customs declarations in the amber scan-light that Logistics Command kept running around the clock, because freight didn't sleep and neither, technically, did compliance review. It was tedious work by design — line after line of tonnage and routing codes and declared contents, built to be checked by someone with more patience than imagination. Amita had more of the first than anyone on her shift and had learned, over six careful months, exactly how much of the second it took to use that patience for something the manifest system was never built to allow.

Her handheld chimed with the same small, unremarkable sound it made a hundred times a shift.

"Manifest 44-Delta-19, Lunar Accord humanitarian logistics, quarterly medical resupply. Awaiting compliance sign-off."

She pulled the shipment record. Standard humanitarian tonnage — sealed pharmaceutical containers, cold-chain equipment, freight that crossed bloc lines under treaty protections nobody wanted to be seen violating, which made it, as it happened, close to the only freight nobody looked at twice. Amita had learned that fact the slow way, the way she'd learned most things useful: by watching what the compliance system flagged and what it didn't, and building, quietly, a working knowledge of the exact shape of its blind spot.

She opened the packet Daniel's Nairobi cell had routed to her three days ago — the greenhouse-component manifest that hid, inside its declared tonnage figures, a data fragment small enough to read as rounding error to anyone who wasn't looking for it. The entangled-channel telemetry. Drift figures, direction, the New Manhattan signature scrubbed clean by the time it reached her, because she'd never asked to know a source and nobody had ever offered one.

She embedded it in the cold-chain equipment's diagnostic subheader, a field the compliance system read exactly once and never again, formatted so precisely to the header's expected structure that it would pass a full audit without a flag — because Amita had built the format herself, over six months, checking it against every audit protocol Logistics Command ran until she was certain, the way she was certain about very little else in her life, that it would hold.

"Manifest 44-Delta-19 approved," the terminal said. "Cleared for scheduled orbital transfer."

She wrote the manifest number in her personal log — a habit that had nothing to do with resistance and everything to do with the compliance officer she'd trained to be, a woman who documented everything because documentation was the only protection a port logistics officer actually had. It sat now in the same notebook as forty-one other manifest numbers, six months of small, deniable acts recorded in the driest possible language, because if this ever needed proving — to a tribunal, to history, to no one — it would need to be proven the way she proved everything: with a paper trail too meticulous to argue with.

She did not know what the data was. She did not particularly want to know, not yet — knowing was a liability she couldn't afford on a compliance floor that ran biometric sweeps twice a shift, and not-knowing let her answer every question truthfully if it ever came to that. What she knew was simpler and, to her, sufficient: someone inside the apparatus she worked for had found something worth hiding, and hiding things worth finding was the one skill Amita Rao had spent her whole career perfecting, first for the Corporate Union, and now, in the same clipboard-worn hands, for whoever needed it more.

She closed the manifest, adjusted her glasses against the amber glare, and moved to the next item in the queue — forty tons of desalination parts bound for the Mediterranean Zone, ordinary, undramatic, exactly the freight that kept a world running while nobody was looking at it too closely.

CORPORATE UNION — NEW MANHATTAN, SURVEILLANCE SPIRE / Marcus Bellamy

Four levels above Elena Vázquez's tier, in a command room with the same mirrored glass and the same biometric amber wash across every surface, Marcus Bellamy was running a search that never showed up on anyone's performance metrics: hunting for the particular rhythm dissident networks used to hide inside legitimate traffic, a micro-jitter in entangled-channel timing that no automated filter had ever been tuned precisely enough to catch on its own. He'd built the search himself, over nineteen years, refining it case by case against networks that had eventually, mostly, been found. It was slow work. It was also, as far as Marcus was concerned, the only part of the job that still required him to think.

His hands moved over the console with the unhurried economy of a man who had done this ten thousand times — weapon-callused fingers, the calluses from a different decade of his career than the one he was in now, when the job had meant something more direct than pattern analysis in a mirrored room. Greying now at the temples in a way he'd stopped bothering to correct. He preferred the analysis work, if he was honest with himself, which he tried to be at least once a shift. It asked less of him, in the ways that had once cost the most.

The flag came up on a cross-correlation pass he ran maybe once a month, more out of habit than expectation.

"Timing correlation identified. Confidence: elevated."

He pulled it up expecting nothing — the search flagged coincidences more often than signal, that was the nature of casting this wide — and found instead a match too precise to file under coincidence without a fight. A quantum fluctuation dataset out of Luna Haven, sourced from an open scientific feed with no reason to be interesting to anyone in his line of work, sharing its exact timing architecture with the jitter signature of what looked, on every surface reading, like dissident channel traffic threading itself through the Spine's ordinary logistics noise.

"Run it again," he said.

"Confirmed. Timing architecture consistent across both sources to five decimal places."

Five decimal places wasn't noise. Marcus had spent nineteen years learning the difference between a pattern that meant something and a pattern that only looked like it did, and this sat, uncomfortably, in the narrow space between the two — a signature that could be camouflage, dissidents borrowing a real scientific anomaly's timing profile to mask their own traffic inside it, because that was exactly the kind of thing a competent network would do. Or it could be nothing to do with dissidents at all. Physics behaving strangely, and his search simply good enough, for once, to notice the strangeness before anyone whose job was physics did.

He sat with the ambiguity longer than the flag technically warranted. That was new, or new enough that he noticed himself doing it — nineteen years of finding things and escalating them cleanly, and here he was turning a result over twice before he let himself write anything down. He didn't examine why. He'd learned, the way anyone in his position eventually learned, which questions were safe to ask himself and which ones weren't, and this one sat close enough to the second category that he set it down without finishing it.

"Note appended to file," he told the console, and dictated it the way he dictated everything — precise, unembellished, the language of a man who had built his entire career on records that could survive being read back to him later. Possible dual-use signal structure. Timing correlation between Luna Haven open-science telemetry and unattributed channel traffic, confidence elevated, source classification indeterminate. Further analysis required before escalation.

He did not escalate it. Escalation meant a formal review, a review meant attention, and attention on a result this ambiguous meant a decision made by someone with less patience for ambiguity than he had — someone who would read dual-use and hear dissident and act on the second word without waiting for the first one to be confirmed. He wasn't protecting anyone by holding it back. He told himself that plainly, the same unembellished language he used for everything else, and mostly believed it. He was simply doing the job correctly: a result this uncertain didn't belong in front of people who'd treat uncertainty as an inconvenience rather than a finding.

Outside the mirrored glass, New Manhattan's seawall ran its slow amber scan across the water and the drowned reef beyond it, the same light sweeping past four floors below, where Elena Vázquez's own displays, though Marcus didn't know it and wouldn't for a long time yet, were running the same data through a different kind of silence. Two command levels in the same tower, two people looking at pieces of the same signal, and between them nothing but glass, distance, and the ordinary architecture of an institution built, with real success, to keep exactly this from ever finding itself.

Marcus closed the file. Unescalated. Flagged only for himself, in a note that existed nowhere the Corporate Union's own systems couldn't find it if they ever thought to look — which, filed under a search nobody else ran, they had no particular reason to.
