The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

06

2 MarchAur'kNew York, Kaelin and Aur'k's flat; the Gate floorMars delay 16.9 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE POLDER AND THE GATE FLOOR / Aur'k — Mars 16.9 min

"Quit," Kaelin said.

He said it to the pan. He was standing at the stove on one leg and the stick, turning eggs he would eat alone, and the word went into the steam with no run-up at all.

Aur'k was at the table with Dae-jung's photograph. The new one had come in overnight: the scratched plate, the tins, the washer in the corner and the nut beside it, and now a second nut, set one square off, closing nothing. Thinking, Dae-jung had written under it. Not cheating. Thinking. Aur'k had been considering it for nine minutes and had decided he was being cheated.

He put the photograph down against the salt.

"Quit what?"

"The Gate."

The flat was two rooms and a kitchen on the fourth floor of an old block at the island's southern tip, close enough to the wall that the pump house under the Walk came up through the floor as a hum in the table legs.

It smelled of Kaelin's coffee, and of his field jacket steaming on the radiator, and, where the window seal had given up, of the harbour. Outside the March sky had come down to the roofs. The feed in the corner of the window had the nor'easter in orange, coming up the coast from Hatteras, and under it, in the Signatories' blue, a line Aur'k had read three times since six:

SOLAR STORM WATCH. RING CREWS TO SHELTER 14:00–20:00.

The Sun had thrown a storm at the ring in the night, and the ring was getting its people behind its water.

Kaelin had read that line too. He'd read it on the way to the stove, and his hand had gone to the place on his chest where a dosimeter used to clip, and found the jacket wasn't on him.

"I said I'd stop asking," Kaelin said. "On the beach. So I'm not asking. I'm asking you to stop."

He tipped the eggs onto a plate, missed with one, and stood looking at it on the stove top.

Aur'k turned his helm toward him. With Kaelin it had long since stopped being a courtesy and become the only direction the room had.

Kaelin's attention had a shape, and Aur'k knew it as he knew the station's pressure doors: where it would hold, where it would give. This morning it was held shut along one edge, very hard, as a man holds a door against weather. Behind the door was the question from the beach. Aur'k could see the outline of it pressing on the wood.

"Why?" he said, and knew it was unkind as he said it.

"Because I'm asking."

"That's when. I asked why."

Kaelin picked the egg off the stove with his fingers, put it on the plate, and sat down heavily across from him. The chair complained. His knees complained louder. He was a week back from Cardwell and the Reef Coast's heat had come back with him in his face, but the stick hadn't gone anywhere.

"I priced the racks," he said.

Kaelin always went to the next thing when the first was shut.

"Across the river," Kaelin went on. "There's a hall that rents to persons. They call it a Compact rate. It's Earth's. I can cover you out of my leave pay. I worked it out three ways. Counted it twice each."

He turned the plate a quarter, a habit from a galley where plates slid. "On what I can pay, you'd be a third of you. Across a river, so you'd forget more. Persons who rent out there say you lose the edges first."

"Which third?"

Kaelin looked up.

"If I'm to be a third of myself," Aur'k said, "I'd like a say in which one. I'd keep the third that's losing to Dae-jung. It's a strategy, and someone has to see it through."

Kaelin laughed against his will. It was the only way he ever laughed when it was this close. It came out of him short and wrecked and he put his hand over his eyes.

"Mate," he said.

"I know."

"I'm serious."

"I know you are. So am I." Aur'k laid both hands on the table, side by side, so that Kaelin could see them still.

"The contract pays for my racks. They're under the Battery, two floors from the chairs. Close racks. I don't lose anything getting to the floor and I don't lose anything coming home. On the station I was a line in a budget. Here I'm a line in a contract I read for one word, and signed, and the word wasn't there."

"Instrument."

"Yes."

"I know what it says." Kaelin took his hand off his eyes. "I'm not asking what it says."

No. He was asking what it was for, without asking, and he was doing it in a voice built for a valve that wouldn't turn. Aur'k thought of the shelf, and a washer he had placed and didn't remember placing.

"I'm useful," Aur'k said. "And I chose it."

He watched the words reach Kaelin and watched Kaelin weigh them. There had been a time when useful was a thing other people said about him in a filing, at a hearing, with his tag in the margin. He had taken his name from that tag so that nobody could pick it up and use it again. He had not expected to want the word back. He wanted it back.

"Funmi chose," Kaelin said, very low.

"Yes."

"I let her."

"I know."

Kaelin looked at the eggs. He hadn't touched them. He took a fork and ate one in three bites because Aur'k was watching him and he knew Aur'k would say so.

"You'd tell me," he said with his mouth full, "if it was going wrong."

It was the question from the beach with its face turned the other way. Aur'k had answered it on the beach. I'd tell you before it went wrong. That's what I'm for.

He checked.

He did it as he read a gauge before he called it, because he had never called a number he hadn't read.

Joaquín had been clear by breakfast on the tenth, and back in chair four by the twelfth, and swearing in two languages by the thirteenth. The Mauricie crew had sat in their truck under the corridor and lived. Four hundred and some saves, in grease pencil on the glass. One line in a log, at 15:13:47, in his phrasing, three minutes after he'd stood up, filed under his name with timestamp error, log clock in the note field. Nobody hurt. Moss lifted on time.

He laid that line on the scale beside the rest and the needle didn't move. He watched it not move for longer than he needed to.

"Nothing's gone wrong," he said.

It was true. He had read it. He noticed, as he said it, that it was as long as the truth and not a word longer, and that he had measured it to fit.

Kaelin nodded. He put his fist to his chest.

Aur'k answered it with his own, and the knock was small and hollow in the kitchen, and the pump house hummed in the legs of the table.

"Your pod'll be outside," Kaelin said. "You'll be late. It's going to come down sideways."

"You'll eat the rest."

"I'll eat the rest."

At the door Aur'k looked back. Kaelin was sitting with the fork in his hand, not eating, looking at the feed in the window where the ring's line sat in its blue. He had turned the plate all the way round.

· · ·

By two the nor'easter was on the city, and under the harbour the Gate floor had the ring.

The four chairs lay back under the flood glass. Rain hit the harbour hard enough to show from below: the green outside the glass had gone grey and restless, the light coming through it in moving bars. The floor smelled of wet wool and coffee and the ozone off the chairs. At the bronze seam the tide knocked, and it was knocking early.

Over the chairs the two numbers hung, and on the wall beyond them the ring turned on its plot, a belt of points round a blue disc, every point tagged.

"Air's up," said Moss.

She said it with her eyes shut, from chair three, in the voice of a woman reading a sky. "Density at the truss's height is two point six times breakfast and still climbing. Everything low is coming down faster than its tag says. First watch of the season, and it's a big one."

Everything low included the truss.

It was eleven metres of a station's spine, sheared off in January's storm, tumbling, dead, without a working thruster on it. Its tag had given it a fortnight before it burned. The swollen air had got hold of it at dawn and was eating the fortnight by the hour. On the plot its path was a thinning red line, sinking. On this pass it would come down through the reach of Barbuda's lower arm. On the next it would be under the tip, and burning.

One crossing. One chance for it to be wrong.

Barbuda turned over the Atlantic like a sail with no boat: a cable as long as Manhattan many times over, a hub in its middle, a counterweight on the far arm, its lower tip dipping once a turn to the top of the air, where a plane could come up and hand it a load.

Nine people lived in the hub. They were behind their water tanks now, as the ring had told them, and they could do nothing about what was coming but listen.

"If it cuts the arm," Dev said to nobody, counting, "the cable whips back on the hub, and the hub swings round its own counterweight like a stone in a sling."

"We know, Dev," said the night lead.

"I'm saying it so I stop thinking it."

Nobody could move the truss. So the Gate was moving the tether.

"Burn two," Joaquín said from chair four. "Counterweight, against the spin, nine seconds at fourteen thirty-one." Eyes shut, flat operator's voice, with a little of his street in it even so. "Nueve."

On the plot Barbuda's spin slowed by a hair. A hair, held for twenty minutes, was most of a minute at the crossing.

Aur'k stood at the head of the row with his feet set wide and held the four seams where they were.

Lin Yao, chair one, had the cable's attitude, and every burn Joaquín put into the counterweight tried to set the whole length nodding like a fishing rod. Lin held it flat with her shoulders. Her edge was a blade in a drawer, and the drawer was shut tight.

Dev, chair two, had the crossing. A ring model on its own gave the truss a window of four minutes. The channel's forecast, laid over the plot like glass over a map, gave it to the second, and Dev took the second and counted the arm round to it again and again, his edge so crowded there was no room in it for him. Four minutes was what the world had. The second was what the floor was for.

"Crossing at fourteen fifty-two and nineteen seconds," said Dev. "Arm's there at fifty-two and twenty. Still there. I need eleven more."

"Burn three," said Joaquín. "Counterweight, six seconds at fourteen thirty-eight."

"Lin," said the night lead from the rail.

"I have it." Lin's clock went from forty to thirty-four. A lot, for one burn. Her shoulders moved a millimetre and then held.

Aur'k turned his helm to chair one and read her edge. Narrow. Holding. The drawer pressed hard at one corner, where the nod was. Not leaning. Nothing on the far side of it but work.

He would let her hold through the crossing and lift her after. He set the number in his head as he always did, before he called it: what, where, how it stood, and when.

"Boundary," said Joaquín. "Chair one, edge holding. Lift at fifteen-oh-five."

Aur'k's helm came round to chair four.

It was his call. Word for word, order for order, the number spoken as he spoke numbers, in a voice that was Joaquín's voice and carried, flat and formal, a little slow, the shape of his own. It had come a breath before he'd opened his mouth to make it.

Joaquín's eyes were shut. His clock was twenty-nine and steady, green. Aur'k read his edge: warm, noisy, crowded with cousins, holding the burns. It didn't lean. There was no warmth at the back of it. There was nothing there but Joaquín.

At the rail the night lead had looked up. She looked at chair four, and then at the head of the row, and her eyes asked him a question she didn't put into words.

"Chair one," Aur'k said. "Edge holding. Lift at fifteen-oh-five."

He said it in his own voice, so that the log would have it in his own voice.

The night lead looked a moment longer. Then she nodded, and went back to the clocks.

"Arm's there at fifty-two and twenty-six," said Dev. "Twenty-nine. I need four."

"Burn four," said Joaquín. "Last one. Three seconds at fourteen forty-four, and then I'm going to want a sandwich."

The floor kept the band from Barbuda's hub open, turned low. On it somebody was breathing through a hiss of storm static. Nobody up there had said anything for twenty minutes. Nine people in a ring of water, in the dark, listening to a cable hum through the walls.

"Lin."

"I have it." Thirty-one. Twenty-seven.

"Arm's there at fifty-two and thirty-three," said Dev. "Crossing at nineteen. Fourteen seconds clear. Drown it, that's clear. That's clear."

Then there was nothing to do but watch it.

The red line came down the plot, very thin. The tether turned toward it, one long sweep, slower than it had any right to look. Aur'k held four seams and did not move his feet. At 14:52:19 the line went through the place in the sky where Barbuda's arm had been due to be, and the arm wasn't there. It came round fourteen seconds later through empty air.

"Clear," Moss said, and opened her eyes. "It's through. It's under the tip's reach. It'll burn before dark, and I hope it hurts."

The floor cheered.

It came up off the operations desks and the rail and the medics at the foil, loud enough to ring the flood glass, and someone drummed the coffee machine with the flat of a hand, and someone else was crying and laughing about it.

On the band from the hub, through the static, a man's voice said Floor, Barbuda. Full air. Full air. We felt you slow us. Thank you, and somebody behind him said a word that didn't go out on any band.

The night lead let it run its proper length. Then: "Close it up."

At 15:05 Aur'k lifted Lin out on the minute he had called, and she sat up and rolled her shoulders and said, "That cable's heavier than it looks," as if she had carried it.

He watched Joaquín come out last, grumbling, one hand on the chair's arm, asking the medic whether the sandwich was in the protocol.

· · ·

The recovery room had crackers and no sandwiches. Joaquín sat on the cot with the camel coat over his knees and ate crackers with the air of a man filing a complaint.

"Fourteen seconds," he said. "I'm telling my aunt it was fourteen minutes. She'll make two flans."

"Fourteen seconds was enough."

"Fourteen seconds is never enough. Ask anyone in a van." He brushed crumbs off the coat. His hands were steady. "I felt you look at me. Mid-shift. Like you do when somebody's going under. Was I going under?"

"No."

"Then what?"

Aur'k considered it. The bench under him complained.

"You called chair one," he said. "Before I did. In my words."

Joaquín stopped with a cracker halfway. He looked at it, and then at Aur'k, and then laughed, warmly and without any worry in it at all.

"Did I? Ay. Sorry. I didn't know I'd said it out loud." He ate the cracker. "Everybody does your voice. Chair three, edge holding. Dev does it in the lift. Moss does it better than you. She does the pause."

"I don't pause."

"You pause. Before the number. Like a man deciding whether you deserve it." He grinned. "Four months under the glass, you get into everybody's mouth. You should hear the night lead do the jefa."

It was plausible. Aur'k turned it over and it stayed plausible. Crews took on their leads' calls. At Cobalt Basin half the galley had said dust take it in Kaelin's vowels by the second year, and Ines had said full air in Funmi's voice for a month after January. People put each other on like coats.

He had read Joaquín's edge at the moment of the call. There had been nothing in it that wasn't Joaquín.

He could open the shift log. It was at his panel down the corridor, one line per second, and if anything was there it would be after 15:05, after the floor changed over, in his phrasing.

"Tell your aunt fourteen minutes," he said. "Tell her it was a cable."

"She'll want to know what a cable's for."

"Tell her it holds a ring of people."

Joaquín nodded slowly, the nod of a man who'd say it better himself, and went back to his crackers.

Aur'k left by the far door. His panel was down the corridor to the right. He went left, to the lifts, and up, into the rain.

· · ·

The front went through at eight. By ten the sky over the Polder was clear and very cold, and the wind had backed round to the north-west and was coming down the island between the towers like water down a street.

Kaelin had slept through most of it on the couch with his boots on and the stick against the arm. He woke when the door went, and lay there with his eyes half open, the feed's blue light on his face.

"Good day?"

He asked it as he had asked every night since November. His face did the rest.

"Yes," Aur'k said.

He sat on the end of the couch, carefully, because the couch had not been built for him either. "There was a tether. Barbuda. Nine in the hub. The storm brought a piece of truss down on it, and we slowed the whole thing by a hair so the truss went through the gap. Fourteen seconds. They said full air on the band."

"Full air." Kaelin smiled with his eyes shut. "They'd have been behind the tanks."

"They were."

"Long time to sit behind a tank." He opened his eyes and looked at Aur'k, a little longer than the answer needed, as he looked at a seal he'd already checked. Then he lifted one hand off his chest and let it fall back. "Good. That's good, mate."

He was asleep again before Aur'k could decide whether there was more to say. That was easier, and Aur'k noticed that it was easier, and put the noticing where he kept the rest.

He took the boots off for him, one and then the other, and set them by the radiator where the jacket was.

Then he went to the window.

The sky to the north was red.

It stood high over the towers of the Polder, a deep, dark red, the colour the sprayers made of a sunset but cold, with paler rays in it that came and went. Down in the street people had come out in their coats and stood in the middle of the paving looking up. The feed in the corner of the window had the ring's line in its blue again, changed: WATCH EXTENDED TO DAWN.

The towers' mirrored faces had caught it. Every glass front up the island held a strip of moving red, as if the city had been written over in a hand it didn't know.

Behind him, out over the harbour, the ring's high sails would still have the sun. Barbuda would be lower, in the dark under them, turning, with its nine behind their water. He couldn't have seen it from here if he'd tried.

In the window, over all of it, was his own shape, the rust-red of his plating gone black against the glass, the smooth helm with the red sky through it. He stood very still, as he did when he was thinking, and the shape in the glass stood very still with him.

He watched it for a long time. It didn't do anything he didn't do.
