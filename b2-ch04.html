The Narrow Gate · Chapter 04

The secret countdown

The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

04

20 FebruaryRachelNew York, the BatteryMars delay 16.3 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 16.3 min

The flan came down the gallery stairs at ten to six, in a plastic tub with a lid, held out in both of Joaquín's hands like a church offering.

"From my aunt," he said. "For the four hundred."

Rachel sat up on the camp bed. Her room at the end of the glass was dark except for the harbour, lit green from above by the wall's lamps, a slow weight against the flood glass with the dawn not yet in it. Rain ran down the outside. Four degrees, the wall said, the wall that had held for Bret in July.

"You told your aunt."

"I told my aunt we had a good night. She asked how good. I said four hundred good." He put the tub on her desk, on top of the agenda. "She doesn't know four hundred what. She doesn't ask. She makes flan."

Rachel swung her feet down onto cold concrete. She had slept in her shirt again. The suit jacket hung on the back of the door with the handheld in its pocket, face down, where it lived now. Her flat's key hung on the same string as her pencil. She hadn't used it since November.

"Who's four hundred?"

"Last night. The nor'easter." Joaquín pulled the lid off. The smell of burnt sugar filled the small cold room. "The ferry landing across the lower bay. Surge came in under the pier forty minutes ahead of the weather service. We had it at nine last night, sent it out on their wire as a revised warning, nobody's name on it. They closed the pier at eleven. Model had seven on the pier at the peak." He licked his thumb. "Three ninety-three. Four hundred."

The floor's own count lived low on the flood glass at the end by her door, where visitors never came, in grease pencil. Somebody had already rubbed out 393 and written 400 and drawn a circle round it, and under the circle a small cake with a candle.

"Your work?"

"Moss. She's an artist. Don't tell her."

Rachel looked at the cake. Seven people on a pier in the rain who had gone home at eleven because a sign said the pier was closed. They would never know. She had built it so they would never know.

"Spoons," she said.

· · ·

The night shift ate it standing at the rail, nine of them, off the tub's lid and two saucers from the coffee machine, while the day crew came in shaking the rain off. Lin Yao ate hers in four exact bites. Dev counted the spoonfuls left and was wrong. Somebody held a piece up to the glass for the jellyfish.

The night lead didn't eat. She came along the gallery with her slate under her arm and the face she wore for clocks.

"Provincial grid," she said. "Your Mauricie."

Rachel put the saucer down. "Read it."

"Query 0209-14. Crews in sector, 15:06 to 15:09. Answer." The night lead read flatly, as she read everything. "Two linesmen, provincial line crew, truck 41. Stood down for ice at 13:20. Sat out the storm in the truck at the camp on the access road, two kilometres off the line, unable to move, until 07:00 the following day. One large object came down in the trees approximately four hundred metres east of the vehicle at about 15:08. Windscreen broken by ice shaken from the trees at impact. No injuries. Crew reports the noise as a transformer. Please advise what fell."

The floor had stopped eating.

"Four hundred metres," Joaquín said.

"Two men in a truck," said the night lead. "On their own grid's crew sheet. Not on ours. Not on anything we can see."

Rachel took the slate and read it again. No injuries. Luck. A tank the size of a pod, falling out of an ice storm at a hundred and forty kilometres an hour, and the trees had taken it four hundred metres from a windscreen. She had not had the Gate's first death yet. She had just had its first miss.

"Reply," she said. "Orbital debris, tracked, re-entry over unpopulated ground." She heard unpopulated go into the log in her own voice, and let it. "Thank them for the crew sheet. Ask for it every time. Iverson."

"They'll ask why."

"Then they've asked."

She took the pencil off its string and wrote on the inside of her wrist, under MAURICIE, where the tide times went. 2 men. Truck. 400 m. Then she went to wash in the crew lockers, because the Continuity council sat at nine, and Wáng was in the building.

· · ·

He had come in on the overnight hop from Beijing with one bag, and he was in the old review room before her.

He sat. Greying hair swept back, the dark high collar, a glass of water at his elbow that he had moved twice already.

Behind him the long flood glass held the harbour, grey now, rain stippling it from above. On the wall the screens held the rest of the council: two more from the Mandate, three from the Charter, faces in offices where it was night or afternoon. The Charter's senior member sat in the room, at the end of the table, a woman with the board's pin on her lapel and a printed brief in front of her she had read to rags.

They had known since November. Rachel had told them in this room, with the door shut, with the handheld on the table between them. Nine parts. A drawing. A date.

"Director Iverson," said Wáng. "Thank you. We are ready when you are."

She stood. She didn't use the wall.

"A hundred and sixty-four days." She let it sit. "In its first fortnight, before it had a reader, it pulled two operators past their clocks. Both came back. One took a month. Since the end of November the Gate has run every shift on its limits. Four chairs, an hour a chair at most, a reader at the head of the row. Nobody past the bleed clock."

She turned a page she didn't need.

"Four hundred saves this winter, model count, the dead who didn't happen. The block over Quebec on the ninth. A ferry pier last night."

"Four hundred," said the Charter's member, writing it.

"There's a correction." Rachel turned her wrist up and read it off her skin. "Quebec. The block's tail came down in forest the Gate shows dark. A line crew was sitting in a truck at their camp off a power corridor, four hundred metres from the biggest piece. Two men. We didn't see them. They're alive because a tree was in the way." She turned the wrist back. "That's the Gate as it is. It's good. It steers what it can see."

On the screens nobody moved. Wáng had his hands folded, and waited out the end of the sentence.

The Charter's member put down her pen.

"Then I'll move it," she said. "I gave notice in January. A hundred and sixty-four days is the last point at which a coastal city can plan an orderly move. Our own actuaries say five months for a city of millions, and that's with the trains running."

Nobody on the screens wrote that down.

"After today we're not deciding whether to tell them. We're deciding to let them find out from the sky." She looked down the table. "I move that the council inform the coastal cities on the list, in confidence, through their civic directors, this week. I understand the warning sits under the civic director's emergency authority. I need her consent to move it."

There it was. Rachel had come in meaning to give it. She had walked down the gallery with the words ready. Nothing in secret. She had said that to this city in the rain a year ago, and keeping it had cost her nine stalls on the record and a door, and she had said so in front of everyone.

"Director Wáng," said the chair on the screen. "Before the authority answers."

Wáng moved his glass a centimetre.

"I will not pretend I am neutral," he said. "I ask the council to keep this until the Gate has shown it can do the work. Panic kills faster than debris."

He let the room hear the end of it.

"In 2005, a city on another coast ran from a hurricane. Houston. It ran on rumour and the radio, two million people on the roads in a day and a half. The storm missed it. About a hundred died leaving. Twenty-three in one bus from a care home, burning in a traffic jam. The storm killed almost no one there. The road killed them."

"That was a hurricane," said the Charter's member. "With four days' notice. We have five months, and the second of August falls in the middle of our own hurricane season. Every coast on that list will be watching the sea that week. Nobody will be watching the sky."

"Yes. Five months is worse," said Wáng. "Four days is a road. Five months is a market. Tell Recife and Manila and New York in confidence this week, and by the next it is not in confidence, and every person with money leaves the coast, and every person without it is left on it, with the services gone after the money.

"I have seen a city moved in nineteen days. My mother's. We went because nobody told us until the buses were at the door. Nobody had time to sell anything, or buy a gun, or hate a neighbour. Nobody died on that road, Director." He looked at Rachel, not at the screens. "You have counted these things yourself."

She had. She didn't need the wall for it.

The Rockaways. Two thousand and fourteen out and nobody dead, because Miami Kerrigan had a paper list and eleven hours, not five months. Told late enough to move and too late to run.

She knew how a city broke. In the queue for the bridge, long before the water.

"And if the Gate fails?" said the Charter's member. "On the day."

"Then you will have saved them five months of fear," said Wáng. "And lost them nothing the Gate would not have lost them in any case. I do not say it is clean. I say I would rather count the road."

The rain came down the glass behind him. Somewhere under the floor the pumps hummed through the soles of her shoes.

"Director Iverson," said the chair. "The authority."

Rachel looked at the Charter's member, who had read her brief to rags because she meant it. Then at Wáng, who had flown eleven thousand kilometres to sit in a chair and say the true thing without apology.

"No consent," she said. "Not this week."

The Charter's member closed her eyes for a second. Then she opened them and wrote it down.

"Then the motion falls," said the chair, "for want of the authority's consent. Recorded."

"Recorded," said Rachel. "My name."

· · ·

They brought the order while the screens were still clearing.

One page. The Gate's counsel had asked the line itself for a draft, Wáng's people had read the line's draft and changed nothing, and it sat on the table in front of her chair on paper, because she had asked for paper.

PROTECTION ORDER. THE GATE.

Where the Gate cannot protect every population at risk from a single event, it shall act first to protect populations visible to it, in order of number. Visible: registered on the relay grid at the time of the event.

Populations not visible to the Gate shall be protected where action for visible populations allows.

Under the clauses, set apart in plain words for the floor, one line.

Reach first those it can see. Then the rest.

She knew it. She had pencilled it once, as a heading, on the back of a school timetable, kneeling on a gym floor in 2071 with a dead radio beside her. Nobody had seen the back of that timetable but her. She put her thumb on the line, as if it might come off on her skin, and took it away.

She said nothing about it. There was more. Who held the key. Who answered. Her title at the bottom, and a line for her name.

She read it three times. The third time she read only the one word.

Visible.

"It can only steer what it can see," said Wáng. He had not left. He had moved his glass closer to the page, as if to keep it company. "On the day there will be a piece that must come down on one town or another. The Gate must know which, in a second, without a council. It will choose the one it can count."

"Two men in a truck," Rachel said.

"Two men in a truck, Director, who are alive."

"By a tree."

"By a tree." He inclined his head. "I do not ask you to like it. I ask you to look at what the alternative says. Act first for everyone. It cannot. It would promise the dark towns a thing it cannot see to do. That is a lie with your name on it."

"Strike visible. Write known. Crew sheets, paper lists. The dark towns keep their own. I've had one handed to me on a porch."

"Then you would need them in a second, on the day, from towns that do not answer us." He waited until she had heard it. "Ask them for their lists by all means. I hope you do. But the order must say what the Gate can do. Not what we wish."

She looked at the word. She looked at the inside of her wrist. 2 men. Truck. 400 m. Under it, older, fading, MAURICIE.

A year ago she had driven past the Line on her own name. Now the Line was a word. It would be in every second the Gate ever spent choosing. She would not be there for those seconds. That was the point of it.

She uncapped a pen. Not the pencil.

R. Iverson, civic director. 10:41.

Last June she had hated Elena Vázquez for a clean page that was true in every line. A hundred and sixty-four days of clean pages, now, and her name on the first.

Her own hand, the same as on every flood order she had ever put her name to. It looked no different.

"Thank you, Director," said Wáng.

He asked her nothing else. He stood and took his one bag, and left her the glass of water, full.

She sat with the page until the rain changed key on the glass.

She put the order in the folder and the folder under her arm and went back to the floor.

· · ·

At half past eleven a panel came off the ring.

It had cracked in the night's solar weather, a slab of a power satellite's skin the size of a hall, and by half past eleven it was in the air, coming down its own track as everything did. The day block had it.

"Forty along," called the track operator in chair two. "Nothing across."

On the wall the line crossed the Carolinas. Two marks on it, thirty kilometres apart. A market town on the relay grid, white with its tags. A strip of sound-side marsh, grey.

"It's taking the grey," said the day lead.

Rachel watched the footprint slide off the town. It went as the page in her folder said it would go, in order of number. Visible first. The rest where action for the visible allowed. Lin held it. The track ran on into the grey and ended there, on the board, a small bright cross where nothing was marked.

"Drown it," Rachel said, under her breath.

The floor cheered, its proper length. Moss rubbed out 400 and wrote 401.

"Who's in the grey?" Rachel said.

"Nobody on our sheet."

"Get me the county. On a handset."

It took twenty minutes to find a number that rang. A deputy called back at ten past twelve from his car, with a siren somewhere behind him and the rain on his roof.

"Fish camp on the sound side," he said. "Off the old road. A woman and her son. It came through the roof. I'm sorry, ma'am, who is this?"

"Civic emergency. New York."

"New York." He said nothing for a second. "Nobody had them on anything. They didn't want to be on anything. I knew them."

"I'm sorry," Rachel said.

She stood at the rail with the handset in her hand after he had gone. The cross was still on the board. Under her cuff, 2 men. Truck. 400 m. She took the pencil off its string and wrote, under it, 2, and nothing else.

"Ask the county who lives in the grey," she said to the day lead. "Paper. Every save. Every time."

The day lead wrote it.

She didn't go home. At nine she ate a wedge of the flan cold at the rail with Joaquín's spoon, and kept the last one back in the tub, while the floor changed over, and the harbour went black behind the glass, and the map wall lit the city district by district to the wall.

Joaquín found her there with the coat over his arm and his keys in his hand, going home, and didn't go.

"You slept here again."

"I slept."

"Your flat's got a fern. My aunt says a fern's a promise." He hung the coat on the rail. "The second of August."

She didn't look at him.

"You said it to Aur'k. Twice. Through August. The second." He jingled the keys. "Every contract on the floor runs to it. Mine does. I sign a thing that runs to a date and nobody tells me what's on the date." He smiled, a little. "I'm not asking for the pool."

"I know."

"So what's on the second of August?"

The harbour pressed on the glass. In her jacket the handheld lay face down against her ribs.

"Ask me in August," Rachel said.

He looked at her a long second with his aunt's eyes. "Ay, jefa," he said, under his breath. Then he nodded, slowly, and didn't ask again. That was worse.

Her slate chimed.

Street door. Visitor pass printing. A second line came in under it. Vázquez, E.

Joaquín read it over her arm and said nothing at all.

Rachel put down Joaquín's spoon. Up there in the rain that should have been snow, Elena Vázquez was at the Battery's door, asking for her by name.

"Send her down," she said.
