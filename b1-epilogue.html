The ring

Fractured Horizons · Epilogue
12 July 2095a rigger on the orbital ringThe orbital ringMars delay 5.0 min

THE SIGNATORIES — EARTH ORBIT, THE RING / Galveston Price — Mars 5.0 min

"Price. Watch just went amber."

"I heard it." Galveston clipped her second tether to the rail, tested it, and only then let go of the first. "Amber's a watch. A watch is a rumour with a number on it."

"The number's going up."

"Then talk to me when it's red."

The spine of the power satellite ran away from her in both directions, a white truss two kilometres long, and on either side of it the panels lay out flat and black under the Sun like a field nobody would ever harvest by hand. The truss threw the light back hard enough to darken her visor. Her boots were on the rail. Below her feet, the Earth went by.

She had stopped looking at it in her first year, as everybody did, and started again in her tenth, as some people did. This pass it was the Gulf. Bret had gone ashore somewhere up the coast and was a long grey smear over New England now, rain for somebody, and from here you couldn't see what it had cost. Down in the warm water a new one was turning. Small, tight, white. Its eye wasn't open yet.

She knew that water. She had been named for a city at the edge of it, which the sea had taken the year before she was born. Her mother had a photograph of a seawall, and that was all.

"Price."

"Still amber?"

"Still amber. You've got four items left, your shuttle down goes at eighteen hundred, and I've got a crew of nine who'd like to be behind the tanks before it isn't amber."

"Three items."

"Four. I'm looking at your list."

"I'm looking at it too, and I'm doing two at once." She pulled the cover on the coupling box at bay forty, read the meter, closed it, and ticked it with her thumb on the cuff screen. "Three."

She went hand over hand along the rail toward the old end of the spine. The truss there was the first build, twenty years up, darker metal with the paint scoured off it by grit. Somebody had drawn a face on a junction plate in grease pencil, long ago, and somebody else had given it a moustache.

Tomorrow Eleni would be nine. If the eighteen-hundred went, Galveston would be there for the cake.

The present had gone down on Saturday's shuttle anyway, in a padded box: a chip of debris shield the size of her palm, polished on one face, with a pit in it from a fleck of paint that had hit it at eight kilometres a second. Hold it up to the light, she had written on the card. That's what the sky throws at your mum. Your mum is fine. It had seemed funny on Saturday.

Item three was a seal. Item four was a cabinet.

It sat at the very end of the old truss, a grey box the size of a fridge, bolted to the spine with eight bolts and a lot of hope. A label on its door, the print gone pale: NAV 14. Under it, smaller: Nav/timing. Cal. set CB SURVEY 79. Under that, newer and red, a removal tag with this month's date on it.

She didn't know what CB was. Nobody on this crew had been up long enough to know.

"Here's your four," said the shift boss in her ear. "It's on the decommission list. Pull it, cap the feeds, tag the bolts and leave it for the cargo run."

"Pull it how? It's live."

"It's obsolete."

"It's obsolete and it's live." She opened the door. Inside, a film of fine dust clung to everything by static, and under it the lights were green, every one. She laid her glove on the casing, and through the glove and the suit she felt it. A hum. Not much of one. A machine doing its job.

She called up its counters on her cuff. Fourteen years of numbers, mostly flat. A heartbeat of timing pulses, every few seconds, out to whoever still listened. And one line, nine days old, that wasn't the heartbeat.

TX 9 frames · 03 JUL 22:12Z–22:50Z · Dest: coastal re-entry, Atlantic.

Nine frames. On the third it had talked to somebody, and done it fine.

"Boss. What does it feed?"

A pause while he looked. "Timing to the old nav net. And the Atlantic re-entry advisories go out through it. Backup path."

"Backup to what?"

"To whatever replaced it." Another pause. "Which I'm not finding."

"So if I pull it, the coast loses its debris warnings until somebody signs a new path."

"Somebody will."

"Who? A removal that touches a coastal feed. That's a safety case and a sign-off from every civic desk on the Atlantic."

"A week," he said. "If they're fast."

"It's hurricane season. They're not fast."

Her cuff chimed, a hard double note she felt in her teeth.

"That's red," the boss said. "Proton warning. First arrivals inside forty minutes. Shuttles are grounded. Everybody in. Price, now."

Grounded. She looked at NAV 14 a moment longer. It hummed under her glove. It worked. It had talked to a coast on the third and nobody had complained, and there was a storm in the Gulf, and the one thing she knew for certain about this box was that it did its job and nothing would do it if she pulled it.

"Price."

She thumbed the cuff. NAV 14: inspected. Operational. Feeds live coastal advisories, no replacement path. Removal deferred pending sign-off. She signed it with her thumb and shut the door on the green lights.

"Logged it working," she said. "Coming in."

"Pole on the rail."

"I'm always pole."

"You are never pole."

Two kilometres. She went hand over hand, tether, rail, tether, the clips snapping shut under her thumb, never both off at once. The rule book said one metre a second on the rail. She did two. The black panels slid by on either side, and on her wrist the dosimeter sat at its morning number and stayed there, and she watched it anyway. At bay forty the cuff said thirty-one minutes. At the airlock it said twenty-two, and the boss was in the hatch with his hand out, and she let him pull her in by the shoulder harness like a parcel.

· · ·

Behind the water it was dark and warm and close, and it smelled of nine people who had all been in suits.

Nobody minded knees. The shift boss was wedged against the far tank with his eyes shut. The twins from the cargo crew were playing cards on a helmet. Somebody's dosimeter ticked over and they all looked at it and then at each other, the old joke, who's dosed out first, and somebody said it, and nobody laughed very hard.

Four hours in, the eighteen-hundred went off the board.

Galveston held her handheld up to her face and started the recording.

"Happy birthday, Eleni. Nine. That's very old. I'm in the cupboard with the water, because the Sun's having a tantrum, and I'm squashed between my boss and a man who smells of feet." The man who smelled of feet made a rude sign, and she turned the handheld so it caught him. "Say happy birthday."

"Happy birthday," said the shift boss, without opening his eyes.

"My shuttle's stuck, baby. I'm on the first one after. Hold the present up to the light, like I said, and eat a cake for me. Not the whole cake. Most of it." She stopped. The water pressed close on every side, black and dead still, and she could hear her own breathing in it. "Full air. Your mum is fine."

She sent it down. From up here it would be there before she had put the handheld away.

By the hatch the outside camera ran on a palm-sized screen, and the cargo twins had given up the cards and were kneeling in front of it, shoulder to shoulder.

"Gal," said one of them. "Look."

She got up on her knees beside them.

They were coming round the edge of the night. Below, Earth was dark, a few cities scattered on it like spilled embers, and somewhere under the cloud a storm showed only as lightning, flickering without a sound.

Then the Sun came over the curve of the world, and lit their own station white.

The twin on the left swung the camera aft. Behind them along the ring the stations were still dark. Then the next one back caught the light, and went gold. Then the one behind it. Then the next. Power satellite, tether, port, one after another after another, each one lit in its turn as it came round into the morning, until the whole thread of them was burning gold against the dark, as far back as the camera could see.

Galveston thought of the grey box at the end of the old truss, out there in the light now, its green lights on, humming.

The ring turned into the morning, station by station, all the way round.
