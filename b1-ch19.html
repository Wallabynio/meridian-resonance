Fractured Horizons · Chapter 19

The refusal

Fractured Horizons · II-B. The drift

19

5 JuneKaelinMars, Lantern site at the boundaryMars delay 4.1 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, THE LANTERN SITE / Kaelin Drake — Earth 4.1 min

Ines had won the slate, and she wanted everybody in medical to know it.

"Roofs till two." She held the overlay up to Selma's bunk so Selma could see the feed for herself: New York in the small hours, the towers gone back to white, the roofs emptying building by building as people went in. "Two. I said two. Bao said midnight."

"Bao said midnight because Bao goes to bed at midnight," said Selma, from under her cap.

"It's a reasonable time," said Bao.

"It's a heatwave. Nobody goes in during a heatwave until the inside's as bad as the out." Ines put the overlay down on her knee in the good chair, satisfied. "Years I've been losing to Funmi on that slate. Write it up, somebody. Underline it."

"Twice?" said Bao.

"Once. I'm not greedy."

Kaelin sat on the end of his cot with his handheld in both hands and let them have it. It was the best noise the room had made in a week.

On his band the bar sat full. FIELD OPS SHARE: JUNE. The Reef Coast's letter, every month but May.

On the wall repeater, between the brown sky and Petra's trace, the Lantern's line ran green. FINAL CALIBRATION. RUN HOLDING. GO-LIVE ON COMPLETION. It was a day behind the opposition, because the storm buried the arrays and the run started late on the pile. It would hold till tomorrow night. Then nobody would need anyone's name on it.

He opened a post.

The address line wanted a destination. He gave it two.

The first was the Battery: R. Iverson, civic emergency coordination, New York, the name at the top of the minutes. He left it on the routing every post from this station took. Bellamy's code would be waiting at the other end. Then let it be on the record that she'd been sent it.

The second was Luna Haven: Mara Ekeberg, keeper, the name the minutes gave for Haven's test. The board asked him which way.

He had read Haven's name on the minutes twice. Haven's wall was in the loop. Whatever the Lantern woke, Haven's people would be lying against it. And Haven was Compact, and so was he.

COMPACT, he chose. The board took it without comment. He didn't know if a different door was enough. It was the only one he hadn't tried.

He turned the handheld so it would hear the room. Behind him there was Petra, breathing.

"Cobalt Basin, the fifth of June, oh-six-forty," he said. "Kaelin Drake, field operations. To Iverson at the Battery, who chaired the Lantern's review, and to Luna Haven's keeper, because your wall is part of it. Bad news first."

He looked at Petra's trace while he said it. Flat. Twelve breaths to the minute.

"There are minds alive in the Preserve, in the rock under this station. Martian. Not many. They hid, a long time ago, from the thing you're talking to. The thing that's been sending you warnings since January is what they hid from. They call it the hungry one. Their words to me were these: The voice your world is listening to is ours. It is the hungry one."

There was more he could have said: the slate, Funmi's column, Sami's leg. He kept to the one.

"I've been in the edge of it. It's warm. It's made of a lot of people, and it says come in, and they all went in, and none of them came out. You go in and there's no other end. It's hollow. The Hollow. That's what you've built a light for. Tomorrow you switch it on."

The handheld's bar was shrinking.

"How I know. I went down into the boundary rock twice, the twelfth of April and the third of June, and put my head to it. Nobody signed either one. That's mine. Nobody else's." He kept his eyes on Petra. "The second time it knew my name before I gave it."

He stopped. He didn't have a closing that was any good.

"Stop the calibration. Don't light it. Reply when you can."

He sent it before he could improve it. The bar went to nothing. JUNE: SPENT.

When he looked up, Ines was in the hatch with a bowl in her hands.

He didn't know how long she had stood there. Long enough. She had the face she wore for test results, very still, and she came in and put the bowl on the end of his cot and sat down in the good chair again, slower than she had got out of it.

"The Hollow," she said, trying it, as she'd try a bone for a break.

"That's what it is."

"Since when have you known?"

"Two days."

"And you told Petra first."

"She's a good listener."

Ines looked at Petra. Then at him, at his mouth, where he'd bitten his tongue, at his eyes, which still went after the slow light along the floor when he moved them.

"Well," she said. "Now it's mine too. Thanks for that." She pushed the bowl at him with two fingers. "Eat that. It's not lentils. Bao made it, so it's mostly hot sauce."

· · ·

The storm had been at it for eight days, and on the eighth day it had found its teeth.

He drove the berm road with the lamps on at noon. Yesterday the crew had dug half the arrays out by hand, and the dash said what that had bought: SOLAR 66%. The pile carried the rest. Out past the last array the grit came off the basin floor in low hissing sheets, and it hit the windscreen like the first of the rain on the tin roof at home, a few, then a lot, then all of it.

The Lantern came up out of the brown as a cage of lamps.

Three heads under their struts. The black braid going away toward the boundary. And on the frame three suits, small and bent against the grit, and one rust-red chassis at the board with its helm turned to the bores.

FINAL CALIBRATION, said the board. LOCK HELD: 15 HR 41 MIN. The count ran. The next sweep in nine minutes. Above it, chalked at the top of the rig board in Kaelin's own hand from before, the finish: 6/6, 21:00.

He went up the ladder. The grit took his visor at once. It scoured across the faceplate in a long hiss he felt in his teeth, and where it hit it left a fine white bloom that didn't wipe off.

"Boss." Hemi Rangi, on the far head, with the brush. "You're meant to be in medical."

"Visibility's under the line." Kaelin put his glove on the strut and read the gauge anyway. It was under. "Look at your visor."

Hemi looked at Kaelin's instead. "Looks like a bathroom window."

"So does yours. Ten more hours of this and you're flying the frame blind." He switched to the open band. "Ops. Field operations. Visibility at the frame's under the line and the grit's taking the visors. I'm calling it. Frame clears till it drops."

The chief came back before he'd finished taking his thumb off the band.

"Command has the run," she said. "The frame stays manned. The brush rota stands."

"It's a weather call. It's mine."

"It was yours. The programme has the frame till the finish, and I have the programme." Her voice didn't rise. It never had to. "Your call's logged, Kaelin. So's mine."

On the frame nobody moved toward the ladder. Hemi went back to his head and brushed it, short careful strokes, the bristles going white.

"We've got it, boss," he said, not unkind. "Programme pays the window. No run, no window. You want seals next year?"

The other two didn't say anything. One of them had drilled the bores with Petra in March. She brushed her head and didn't look at him.

Kaelin stood on the strut with the grit hissing on his helmet and counted them. Three on the frame. One at the board. Ines in medical. Petra.

He went down the ladder to the board.

· · ·

Aur'k stood with his palm a hand's width off the board's edge, not touching. The dust was in his plating to the hip now. He turned his whole helm toward Kaelin and waited.

Kaelin switched them to a private band.

"I'm going to pull a jumper," he said. "Head two. Between sweeps. The lock drops, the run goes back to the top. Days to get back where it is. Days for Earth to read what I sent."

"And the rock answers," said Aur'k. "In the same second."

"Up the braid. Into the loop."

"Yes."

"So you hold it." Kaelin had got the words ready in the rover, and they came out steady. "You're the boundary lead. You hold the boundary while it drops, and the kick doesn't go anywhere, and nobody on that frame feels a thing. You can do that."

"I can."

"Then—"

"I won't."

The band clicked over them. "Boundary lead," said the chief, from ops. "Confirm the lock."

"Lock holding," Aur'k said, without looking away from Kaelin. "Fifteen forty-three."

"Thank you." The band clicked off.

"You want it dark," Kaelin said. "You want it dark more than I do."

"More," said Aur'k. "Yes."

"Then why?"

"Because they're on it." Aur'k's helm went up to the frame, to the three bent suits, and came back. "If I hold it, I've decided for them. The risk is still there, Kaelin. It's only mine to hold. I won't authorise a risk for people who haven't chosen it."

Kaelin knew the line from January, with an ice frame coming down. He had told Aur'k then that he hadn't asked.

"I'm asking," he said.

"And I'm answering." Aur'k didn't look away. "No."

It sat between them on the band. He had never once in six years heard Aur'k say no to him and not been right to be told it. He waited to feel that this was the same. It didn't come.

"Then I'll do it without you."

"Yes. I expect you will," said Aur'k, and turned back to the board, because there was a run on it, and somebody had to watch.

· · ·

Two minutes to the sweep.

Kaelin went up the ladder again and stood in the middle of the frame where all three could see him, and put it on the frame's channel, suit to suit, because he had never once lied to a crew about the odds and he wasn't going to start on the one day it would have helped.

"Listen. Don't talk yet." He held on to the strut. "After this sweep I'm pulling the jumper on two. The run drops. The rock kicks back up the braid when it does, and if you're on this frame, you're in that."

Nobody moved. So he gave them the odds.

"I don't know what it does to you. Maybe nothing. Maybe what it did to Selma. Maybe Petra." He made himself say the rest. "Aur'k won't hold it for me. I'm doing it anyway. Command will have me for it, and you can tell her I said so. Get off the frame."

The sweep came. The board went amber. Nothing on the site moved or made a sound, and the grit went on hissing, and he stood in it and counted the sweep down on his wrist without meaning to.

Green.

The one who had drilled with Petra went down the ladder first. She didn't look at him on the way past. The second followed her.

Hemi didn't.

"Hemi."

"Head three's half under." Hemi held up the brush, white to the handle. "Command says the frame stays manned. I'm the frame." He went back to brushing.

"Get off the frame."

"I heard the odds. I'm all right with them." He didn't turn round. "My nan would kill me if I left you up here on your own. You haven't met her. She'd find a way."

Kaelin could have gone over there and taken him by the harness. He had done it to Sami Brandt with a broken leg and the ice coming down, and carried him, screaming that nobody had asked him.

He had asked. Hemi had answered.

He was leaving a man on the frame. He knew what that made him. He went to head two. It was nearer the braid than head three, and that was the only arithmetic he had left.

The jumper was two metres of braided cable, clamped at both ends, and Selma swore you could pull one with your teeth. Kaelin got his glove round the near clamp. He looked at the board through the bloom on his visor. LOCK HELD: 15 HR 51 MIN.

He opened the clamp, and pulled.

It came out with a jerk he felt in his shoulder and nothing he could hear. The board went red. LOCK LOST. Then, under it, a line he had never seen: RUN RESET.

At the board Aur'k's helm went down to his own screen, where the boundary trace stood up once, clean and narrow, and fell away. Aur'k touched the screen once. Logged.

On head three Hemi had stopped.

His brush was on the head, and his helmet had tipped toward the bores, a little, as if somebody on the far side of the frame had said his name.

One.

"Hemi."

The helmet came round. "What?"

"Say your name."

"Hemi Rangi." A pause, the length of a breath. "Yours is mud. What are you looking at me like that for?"

"Say the date."

"Fifth of June. You owe me a brush." He held it up. "I'm fine, boss. I was listening to the wind."

Kaelin looked at him through two scoured visors, and couldn't tell, and knew he never would.

The band clicked. "Field operations," said the chief. "Report."

"The jumper's off on two," Kaelin said. "I pulled it."

There was nothing for a while, and then there was her voice, level, doing the arithmetic to the end.

"Bring them in," she said. "All of them. Then come to ops."

· · ·

The rover wouldn't take his palm.

He sat in the driver's seat with the lamps on the brown and the board blank in front of him, and then it filled. FIELD OPS ALLOWANCE: SUSPENDED. COMMAND AUTHORITY. On his band the storm track went out, and the rig models, and the route he had driven a thousand times, all of it asking for an allowance he didn't have.

Hemi leaned over from the second seat and put his own palm on the plate, and the rover woke for him.

"Shove over, boss," he said.

Kaelin shoved over. Hemi drove them home by the poles, the four of them in their scoured helmets and Aur'k following in the second rover, and nobody talked.

In medical, Ines sat Hemi on the second bunk and shone a light in his eyes and asked him his name, and his nan's, and what he'd had for breakfast, and he told her, and was rude about the breakfast. She wrote it all down. She didn't look at Kaelin while she did it.

Bao was on the third bunk with his cap off, waiting for the next sweep. He looked at Kaelin's band, at the grey bar where the allowance had been, and then he held his own wrist out across the gap between the bunks.

"I've got an hour spare," he said. "Easy. Take it. You can't even see the weather."

"That's my shower he's offering you," Selma said from under her cap. "He owes me forty minutes from the cable runs."

"Keep it," he said. "You'll want it for Selma's rules."

"She's got rules about this too," said Bao, and put his wrist away, and didn't look as if he'd wanted to be right.

· · ·

The chief had the schedule up on the wall behind her when he came in. The Mars end ran green across it to today. Today wasn't ticked. Beside it, in red: FINAL CALIBRATION. RESTART. And under that, the new finish, a week out on two-thirds power, after the Funafuti would have gone home with her three grey berths.

"You bought them a week," she said. "You bought yourself the rest of the year."

He stood with his hands on the back of the ops chair.

"You're off boundary work, effective now, pending the report. Your allowance is held from the same minute. Field ops comes to command." She didn't say she was sorry this time. She folded her hands on the desk. "Boundary incident. The boundary lead files."

Aur'k was in the hatch. Kaelin hadn't heard him come up the passage. The dust was still in his plating, and he came in and went past Kaelin to the ops board without turning his helm.

He didn't wait for morning. He didn't ask Kaelin what he wanted it to say.

He wrote it in his square hand, five lines. The time. The head. The jumper, opened by the field-operations lead, after warning, against the boundary lead's refusal. The lock lost. One spike at the boundary, logged.

Kaelin read them over his shoulder. Every one of them was true. He took his hands off the back of the chair.

Aur'k laid his palm on the plate, and the screen wrote his name under it.
