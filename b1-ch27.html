Nine minutes

Fractured Horizons · III. The cut

27

14 JuneRachelNew York, the BatteryMars delay 4.2 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 4.2 min

At 00:44 the night lead's handheld chimed with a photograph, and she laughed and turned it round for the floor.

Pump house nine's impeller bearing, in its box, six days early. Somebody on the crew had tied a bow round it out of yellow cable tape.

"They want overtime for the bow," the night lead said.

"Give it them," said Rachel.

At 00:46 a handheld at the back of the floor lit with a slip of paper photographed on a table somewhere, and then the one beside it lit, and then Joaquín's, at the rail below her. They came in pieces, off the cooperative roofs, a page in one hand and the next page in another, and people leaned across their desks to put them in order. Nobody had sent them to anybody. They were simply everywhere.

Joaquín looked up at the gallery. "Rachel. You need to see this."

"Later."

"It's the line. The 2089 one. It's—" He read, and his mouth moved, and he stopped. "Number one's a patient. Theodore Bellamy."

She didn't go down. She had seen his hand on the blanket at ten to ten, and the green line over it. She hadn't known it had a number. Number one, before the volunteers. And a nurse kept on every machine for six hours after she died, Joaquín read out, as somebody's key, and under it a hold with the architect's own name on it.

Rachel put the pencil down on the pad, very straight.

She wiped her palms on her suit, one and then the other, and they were wet again before she had finished. The gallery cooling coughed. Out past the flood glass the harbour hung green and warm and lit from inside by the Battery's own windows, and a fish turned in it, slow, and went.

On the wall, beside the green clock, Beijing was past noon. Wáng had a stack of pages in front of him now, whole, not photographs, and he was reading them in order, one finger at the edge of each before he turned it. Bellamy stood with his back to the screens where he had stood all night. He was reading too. His own name. He read it without moving his face at all.

Thirty-nine names on the pad under her hand, and Petra Lind's above them. The tags still rose on the wall, small and white. A ferry's port engine. A pump in Harlem. The tide, just off the top and falling.

At 00:51 the Spire's engineer at the end of the front row stood up so fast his chair went over.

He was the one who had kept the clock all night, a young man in Spire black with a case at his feet he hadn't opened. He went to Bellamy. Bellamy looked at what he was shown and then, for the first time since seven, raised his eyes to the gallery.

"Director Wáng," Bellamy said, to the wall. His voice was as low as ever and carried to every desk.

"The Spire's night desk has read a post on the hard stop. It was sealed to me, and somebody posted on it anyway, tonight, through a door we didn't know was still open. Drake. 01:00 New York, 14 June. Both ends. I won't move it." He let the floor hear it. "And the Earth end is set to go out of service at one. Maintenance authority, E. Vázquez."

Nine minutes.

Rachel stood up.

"Clear it," Bellamy said to the engineer.

The engineer already had his slate out. "I've suspended her rights," he said. "It doesn't touch a pending action. It's set." He tried the clear. "Refused." "Remote clear, author only. Any other authority: at the equipment, in person, with the site authority present." He swallowed. "The equipment's here. The terminal's under us."

Compartment three. Below the waterline, at the bottom of the east stair. The site authority was her.

Wáng had put his pages down, square. "Director Bellamy. I have read Ms Vázquez's papers. All of them." "I have counted thirty tonight, and nine, and now a woman in 2089 who was counted by nobody. I will answer for the Mandate's part at the council, and so will you." He moved his glass. "In July a storm comes to your coast, and this channel has not yet seen where. Lieutenant."

The two Mandate officers had come onto the floor at midnight, with Beijing's request for the night's names, and stood by the east doors since in dark high collars, hands behind their backs, the stillest people in the room. The older of them, a woman with grey at her temples, touched her collar.

"Director."

"Escort the Spire's engineer to the Earth terminal and secure it, pending the council. Nobody is to touch it but him." Wáng looked up at the gallery, at Rachel. "Director Iverson. The Mandate asks the civic director to open the lower level."

The whole floor looked at her. A hundred people who had watched her keep every rule in the building, in public, for twenty years.

She had not said yes to Elena. She had not said no. She had sat with the phone against her ear and heard Elena out, and she had written thirty-nine names, and that was all she had done. Looking away was a thing you could do with your hands in your lap.

This was not looking away.

"Desk," Rachel said. "Open an event."

"Event type?"

"Flood." She came down the gallery stairs, fast. "Compartment three, lower level. We came off high water an hour ago at two eighty-one. The seam's knocking."

It was. It had knocked once a minute all night, as it knocked every night, the tide against the bronze. It had never meant anything.

"Event opened," said the Battery. "Flood, compartment three. Civic authority: Iverson. Entry below the waterline: civic director's review."

The lieutenant had reached the foot of the stairs with the engineer and the younger officer behind her. She stopped. She didn't argue. She touched her collar.

"Logged," she said. "Event opened at 00:52, compartment three. Iverson."

"Iverson," Rachel agreed. "With an I."

"Director. The Mandate holds the Continuity's security."

"Above the waterline. Below it, in an open flood event, it's the city's." Rachel kept her voice where it was on the floor every night. "It always has been. Ask Beijing."

The lieutenant looked at her for a second, then went to the wall and spoke to Beijing in the Mandate's language, low, and the answer came back in the same. Rachel didn't have it. She didn't need it. At 00:53 her slate chimed with an order in the Mandate's script and the Charter's letters under it. Access, lower level, compartment three. For the Mandate: Wáng Sù. Addressed: civic director, host city.

She looked at it. Below-waterline orders in an open flood event went to the civic director. She was the civic director. She sent it on to the one queue in the building nobody would open before dawn.

"Routed," said her slate. "Council, petitions. Earliest quorum: 06:00."

"Director." The lieutenant had seen it go on her own screen. "The order was addressed to you."

"Then the desk's sent it to the wrong place." Rachel kept walking, toward the east stair. "I'll have it looked at."

"Logged. 00:53. The Mandate's order, routed to the council by Iverson." No change in the voice. "Director, I will need a pass for the lower level for the engineer. He's Spire. The stair's flood door reads him as a visitor."

The passes came off Joaquín's desk. Joaquín was already at the printer. He held his thumb on it. It whirred. He took the badge off the tray and read it and frowned and held it up to the light.

"Ah," he said. "It's printed Mariners' House."

"Print another."

"It's thinking. Vamos, viejo." He gave the printer a look of deep family disappointment. "It does this in the heat. It's an old machine. It was in the Evacuations."

The lieutenant looked at Joaquín. Then she looked at Rachel, and didn't speak yet. She was going to log a name, and she was choosing which.

Joaquín Reyes, who had a cousin on a corridor floor on the Moon, and a district with a plaque on it, and a camel coat on the back of a chair.

"Desk," Rachel said, before the lieutenant could. "The pass is mine. I told him to hold it. On my name. My liability. Log it."

The Battery took a second it didn't need, as it had in February.

"Logged. Liability assigned: Iverson."

Joaquín put the badge down. He didn't look at her. He looked at the printer, and his mouth was pressed shut, and she could see he would have liked very much to say That's not a bet.

"00:55," said the lieutenant. "Pass held on the civic director's instruction. Iverson."

The second badge came out of the printer clean. SPIRE. Lower level. LINKED. The engineer took it. Rachel was through the east stair door ahead of all of them.

· · ·

Halfway down the east stair the heat of the floor fell off her like a coat. Sixty steps in the old granite, the Battery's first wall, under the new, and by the bottom it was cold.

It was the only cold room in the building. The terminal had wanted it. Her suit, wet down the spine since nine, went cold on her all at once, and she shivered and was glad of it.

The passage smelled of salt and condensate and new cable lacquer, and under that of iron and old weed, the harbour's own smell, coming through the stone. Water stood in the grooves of the floor. The pumps were loud here, right under her feet, and on the other side of the wall the whole harbour leaned, higher than the ceiling, and the seam in the corner knocked.

The flood door filled the end of the passage. Steel, a wheel at its centre, a panel beside it at shoulder height in the Battery's amber. COMPARTMENT 3. EARTH TERMINAL. Flood event: open. Entry: civic director's review.

Behind it, through the steel, she could hear the terminal's fans.

She stood in front of it and turned round.

They came down behind her, the lieutenant, the younger officer, the engineer with his case. The engineer was out of breath. The lieutenant wasn't. The younger officer looked up at the ceiling, at the beads of water standing on the old stone, and she watched him work out where the sea was and decide not to say it.

"It's held since before the Evacuations," Rachel told him. "Mostly."

He didn't smile. He nearly did.

"Director." Formal, complete, not unkind. "I am instructed to secure the Earth terminal. I ask you to open the door."

"It's under review."

"What does the review require?"

"A mandatory jurisdiction review." She took her slate out and read it to them, slowly, as she had read safety cases to rooms full of people who wanted to go home.

"That the compartment's flood state is confirmed by the civic director. That the authority for entry below the waterline is confirmed by the civic director. That the order of entry and the persons entering are confirmed by the civic director, in person, at the door." She looked up. "It's a good rule. I wrote it."

The seam knocked.

"And how long does the review take, Director?"

That wasn't the lieutenant. Bellamy had come down the stair after them, alone, his coat buttoned to the collar in a building that had been twenty-four degrees an hour ago. He stood on the bottom step. He had his handheld in his hand.

"As long as it takes," Rachel said.

He nodded, as if that were an answer he had expected and was grateful for. He didn't come any closer. He didn't look at the lieutenant. If he asked her to force it, she would have to decide whether to, and Rachel watched him not ask.

"I've read what she sent," he said. "So has Beijing. So, by breakfast, has everybody in this city who can read. There won't be a question anyone asks me about tonight that I can answer well."

He looked down at the handheld. "I spent fourteen years learning not to look at a line, Rachel, and four hours learning to look at it again, and I find I'm not able to go back. He's still moving."

She took the pad out of her jacket. Thirty-nine names in pencil down the left, and how each of them was. She held it up so he could see it, from where he stood.

"So are mine," she said.

He looked at the page a long time. He didn't read it. He was too far away to read it.

00:58. The fans behind the door. The pumps under the floor. The lieutenant, waiting.

"Director Iverson," the lieutenant said. "For the record, I ask once more."

"For the record, it's under review."

"Logged."

Rachel looked at the panel. 00:59. Both ends. She had read Drake's name on a hard stop and a time, and nothing about what a cut did to the people on the other side of it. Elena would have known. Elena hadn't told her.

She took out her handset and opened Haven's line without taking her eyes off the officers.

"Mara."

A pause the Moon's length. Then Mara Ekeberg, very close to the handset, the corridor's breathing behind her. "Iverson."

"Get everyone's hands off the wall. Yours. Rosa's. Everybody's. Now."

The pause again, and in it Rachel could hear Mara decide whether to ask why.

"One o'clock," Rachel said. "Drown it, Mara, now."

She heard Mara turn away and shout it down the corridor, hands off, everybody, hands off the wall, and the shout went down a long hard floor and came back.

The panel changed.

There was no sound. The fans didn't stop. Somewhere above them, sixty steps up, a hundred people made one noise together, a breath going out, and she knew without seeing it that on the map wall the tags had stopped mid-word, a pump house half named, a date with no hour.

EARTH END. OUT OF SERVICE. Maintenance: E. Vázquez.

Bellamy was looking at his handheld. She watched his face, because she couldn't watch the line from here, and she saw it on his face when the hills on Theo's trace went down, one after the other, into a level road.

He put the handheld in his coat. He kept his hand on it in there.

In her ear, behind Mara, a monitor started a long flat tone.

Then another one, further down the corridor, under it.

"Iverson," Mara said, and nothing else.

Rachel stood at the door with the handset at her ear and the sea above her head. Mars would do what it was going to do, or it wouldn't, and she would hear about it as everybody heard about Mars. Late.

The lieutenant touched her collar and read it back, standing straight, with her hands behind her.

"For the record, Director. A flood event on compartment three. The Mandate's order, routed to the council. A pass held. This door, held for review. Each of them logged to Beijing Command, under one name." She looked up. "Is that correct?"

On Haven's line a third tone joined the other two.

"All mine," Rachel said.
