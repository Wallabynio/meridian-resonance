Fractured Horizons · Chapter 22

Minute forty-one

Fractured Horizons · II-B. The drift

22

10 JuneElenaNew York, the SpireMars delay 4.2 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE SPIRE / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.2 min

The vault door wanted a reason.

SPIRE LINE. SESSIONS BY BOOKING ONLY. STATE PURPOSE.

It was a grey panel at the bottom of a stair the lifts didn't reach, three floors under the anomaly room, and the cold down here raised the hair on her arms after the street. Thirty-one degrees up there at a quarter to ten, and the air so wet the misting heads at the corners had given up and dripped.

She had booked this door every night of 2089. A slot, a name, a purpose, like a surgeon booking a theatre, and a door that logged who came out as well as who went in.

She typed. Calibration reference for Luna Haven restart. It was nearly true. Her overlay still held the closed work order from the Moon to back it: Luna Haven coupling: re-zeroed. Ready for restart. E. Vázquez. She had done the job after the manifest, twenty minutes on her knees with Mara in the doorway watching, and signed it.

Her finger stayed over the field.

She deleted the line and wrote the true one. Architect of record. Review of retained session records, 2089. Her name under it.

The panel thought about it.

Sealed line. Booking notified to the sealing authority.

Her lace chimed. Daniel, from Vila Verde.

A photo first. The cooperative hall from the yard, in hard white light, the new roof on it entire, panels laid edge to edge with a ribbon of sealant still shining wet along one seam. His mother at the foot of the ladder with her arms folded and her chin up, inspecting it like a cake somebody else had baked. His thumb over one corner of the lens.

All of it. Mãe says it's crooked. Mãe also says she has never been so happy with a crooked thing, so.

A second photo. The cistern room, dim and blue, a row of children asleep on mats along the cool wall in their underwear, one of them with her feet up on another's stomach.

38 again. They've declared a republic down there. I've been made minister of fans.

Elena looked at the children for a while. She saved the photos. She didn't answer.

Booked, said the panel. 21:52 to 23:52. E. Vázquez.

The door opened.

· · ·

The vault smelled of paper and cold: old stock, clean, faintly sweet, and under it the chalk of desiccant packs, like a library nobody visited. The city ate paper. Down here it kept it.

The drawers ran the length of both walls in steel cabinets, floor to head height, each with its own small readout. RH 38%. 17°C. At the far end stood the line itself in its long cabinet, behind dark glass. She didn't look at it.

She went to the drawers marked 2089.

"Altered records," she said. "Session thirty-one."

Drawer thirty-one, said the vault, in the Spire's clipped voice. Sealed: M. Bellamy, personal authority. Originals retained on paper. Handle with gloves.

"Open it."

Opening is logged.

"I know."

There were gloves in a box by the door. She put them on.

Paper had been her rule, argued for at a table in 2087 while somebody laughed. Nobody altered a session log on her line without the original going somewhere first that couldn't be altered back. For a year the drawers had stayed empty, and she had been a little sorry about that.

The drawer for session thirty-one was full.

The lock gave. The seal let go with a soft breath of dry air, and the readout flickered to note it. Inside lay a stack of slips the width of her hand, in order, each with the same header in square capitals.

ORIGINAL BEFORE ALTERATION. SESSION 31. OPENED 00:00. ALTERED BY: M. BELLAMY, PERSONAL AUTHORITY.

She put the first one on the reading shelf.

"Session report," she said. "Thirty-one. As filed."

Session report, 31, said the vault, and laid it on the glass beside the slip: the one she had been given in 2089, the one she knew by heart.

Session 31. VÁZQUEZ, S. Volunteer. 00:41: coupling cascade, loss of cardiac output. Resuscitation unsuccessful. Session terminated 00:41. Prepared from the session logs of the line team.

And under it, the line team. Six names. Hers first.

For six years she had carried one explanation like a stone in her pocket. A coupling tolerance she set in 2088 had been a little too generous, and at minute forty-one it had found her sister's heart. A risk she had missed. An accident. She had come down here to make it true.

She read the first slip.

00:41:06. Cardiac output lost. 00:41:12. HOLD ENGAGED.

The hold. She knew it as she knew her own handwriting. Her throughput spec had carried it from the first winter: if a volunteer's body faltered mid-session, the line would hold the coupling at full strength through the dropout instead of tearing them out. Nobody would ever be ripped out of a session on her line.

00:42. Medic: call to release hold. 00:42:30. Release declined. M. Bellamy.

00:44. Resuscitation begun.

00:58. Output restored on support.

She read down the orders. Ventilation. Circulatory support. Cooling, to slow the brain's dying. Pressors. A line into the neck. Every machine the floor upstairs could send down a stair on wheels, brought in for a hospice nurse who had once sat in a hot room in Veracruz and opened a window for their mother because their mother asked her to.

Elena's hands had found each other on the shelf. She took them apart.

01:10. Pattern 31: signal clarity above all prior sessions. Stream to calibration band, Cobalt Basin. Key quality: reference.

01:15. Patient 1: session open. Calibration reference: 31.

She stood still and let the tree build itself.

Branch one. Minute forty-one was her margin. Maybe. The slips said only that a heart had stopped. She could keep that branch. It didn't matter. The report said her sister had died at 00:41, and the paper said her sister was alive on a machine at one in the morning, and at three.

Branch two. The hold had engaged on its own, as designed, and nobody had chosen anything. But the medic had asked to release it a minute later, and someone had said no, and his name was on the slip.

Branch three. Soria had consented. Her signature was on the manifest, number thirty-one, VOLUNTEER. Elena had designed that column. It meant an hour on a booked slot with a medic in the room. It did not mean this. Nothing anybody had ever signed for this line meant this.

At 01:20 a man from the director's office had found Elena on the stair outside this door and told her, kindly, that her sister was gone. She had booked that slot herself and given it to the night operator, because she could not sit through it, and she had sat on the third step until it was light. The slip in front of her said that at 01:20 her sister's pattern was going out on the line to Mars, the clearest thing it had ever heard, so that a calibration could find a fainter one.

06:47. Support withdrawn on director's order. Session ended.

Six hours. Six hours and six minutes, and Elena on the stair for all of it, ten metres from the door.

The last branch was the hold. She found the spec at the front of the drawer, where the vault filed whatever a session had run on. Two pages. Her diagram. Her numbers, still right. And at the foot of the second page, in her own small grey signature, Approved: E. Vázquez, architect.

Without the hold, the line would have let Soria go at minute forty-one. It would have torn her out, and she would have died of it, an accident in a margin Elena had set. That was what Elena had come down here to prove, and her own design had taken it away.

· · ·

There was a second drawer with the same seal on it, at the end of the row, out of order. 2095. JANUARY.

"That one."

Opening is logged.

In January she had watched packet one's raw capture go out of her clearance and into Bellamy's, one line of amber going grey. He had filed it here, under the line's seal, with her sister.

The top slip was her own.

ORIGINAL BEFORE ALTERATION. FORESIGHT DECODE LOG, 12 JANUARY. ALTERED 05:48 BY: E. VÁZQUEZ.

The vault had taken in her log with its grey mark in the margin and done what the rule told it: kept the original on paper. Halfway down, in the residue, the seven letters she had cut out of the record and out of her report, in Spanish, spelled right. Her name at the top as the one who had cut them.

She set it face down on the shelf.

Under it lay the raw capture's header, and clipped behind that, orbital traffic's after-report on the ice tug Tarawa, from the Charter's own investigation, with the tug's recovered telemetry.

Thruster fault, port quad: onset 9 January, 08:31:44 New York.

In January she had read the packet's epoch field, the moment its data had been taken, and seen a date three days stale. She had put it down to a clock error.

She looked from the header to the report and back.

The epoch said 9 January, 08:31:44. To the second.

So the clock had been the best in the room. The sender had seen the fault the second it appeared and written down the moment, and then it had kept what it saw for three days.

She took a pencil from her pocket and turned the after-report over on the shelf.

Three days out, the survey shuttle was still on its long coast in. A trim burn then, a few centimetres a second, and it would have arrived an orbit later, in empty sky.

Or the Tarawa's crew, warned, could have fixed their own thruster before they ever needed it. Or the tug could have moved its parking orbit at leisure, with a full load aboard. She wrote them down the side of the page, one under another. Four answers. Nobody under any ice in any of them.

Nine hours out, when the warning reached her threshold, there was one. Move the tug's burn. The tug had to drop its load to make it, and the load came down across the Ice-Works, where two people were standing where the second layer had said they would stand.

The second layer had carried the same epoch. She had seen that in January, on Daniel's spare handheld at three in the morning, and written it on the back of her hand and left it. On the ninth of January the sender had known the names.

It had waited until only one answer was left.

She looked at the column of answers in her own hand, the four it had let go by. Forty people on the shuttle, sent to measure the rock for a relay nobody had yet been allowed to build: kept. The relay stood on that rock now. Funmi Oyelaran and Tuvalu Mensah, under the ice.

The voice your world is listening to is ours. A tired man on a cot, with someone breathing behind him. She had taken him apart in a lock on the Moon, clause by clause, in the voice for rooms.

She laid the slips side by side on the shelf, the session and the epoch, and stood over them with the pencil still in her hand.

· · ·

"You booked it in your own name."

Bellamy stood in the doorway. He hadn't come in. He had his coat on, the high collar closed to the throat, though it was nearly eleven, and his hands were empty. Behind him the stair went up into the dark.

He had come down. She had sent him a notice with her name on it.

"It's my line," she said.

"It always was." He looked past her at the drawers standing open, the readouts on both showing the room's damp coming in, thirty-nine percent, forty. He didn't step over the threshold. "You'll want to close those. The paper doesn't like us."

She didn't move.

"You told me she died at minute forty-one."

"The report told you." His voice was low and level, the voice he used for boards. "It was prepared from your logs, and they were good logs, and we needed one thing in them to be true for a while, and it hurt nobody who wasn't already hurt. For six years it was true. Nobody has ever been sorrier for anything than I am for the stair."

"You could have burned them. You had six years."

"I could have done a great many things in six years, and most of them would have been easier on all of us than a drawer I couldn't open without the door telling you. Destroying a medical record is a crime. Sealing one is a signature." He almost smiled. "And they were your rule. I've always kept your rules, Elena."

"You declined the release."

"Yes."

"The medic asked you. A minute after."

"She did. She was right to. I was right to say no. We don't often get both in one room."

There was no one on the stair behind him. No guard, no lace chime. He had come alone.

"Who's patient one?"

He said nothing.

"The first row. Before any of us signed. Who did you open a session for at quarter past one, on my sister's pattern?"

For a long time he stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides. The readout on the nearest drawer ticked to forty-one.

"My son," he said. "Theo."

Her hands found each other in front of her, one closed over the other.

"He was a boy," Bellamy said. "I've sat in his room and talked to him every evening for fourteen years, and in fourteen years he's never once been in it."

"Then where is he?"

"Somewhere. The line could hear that he was somewhere. It could never hear where. It needed a clear voice to tune against, and we never had one, and then for six hours we had the clearest thing anyone has ever heard." He stopped. "Your sister was very loud, Elena. In there. I think you know that."

"Did he hear you?"

"No." A muscle moved in his jaw and was put away. "Not yet."

Not yet. She heard it, and the Lantern in it, four nights off. The epoch lay on the shelf under her hand. She didn't turn it over for him.

"She was a hospice nurse," Elena said. "She opened a window for our mother."

"I know what she was. I read her file the night she volunteered. She wrote that she wanted to be useful." He looked at the open drawer, at the slips on the shelf. "She was."

Her lace chimed. She knew whose it was without looking. Daniel, in Vila Verde, near midnight there. He would be lying on a mat in the cistern room with the children, typing with one thumb.

Bellamy waited for her to read it. She didn't.

"Mr Rocha's cooperative lost a contract in May," he said, "over a door into our maintenance layer. Compliance sent me the referral in the spring. I read where the route went, and I let it go on to the Continuity without a name." He said it gently. He said everything gently. "We all pay for our sisters. Some of us make other people pay."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a kindness. I'm not very good at them anymore." He looked at the shelf. "On the thirty-first of May you lay down against a wall on the Moon to find her. A keeper with her hand on a lever, ten minutes in grease pencil over your head, and when the ten were up you broke your own exit to stay. The Ward's report came into the review's register, and the register came to me with everything else on the fourth. Then you stood up in front of the review and put your name on the coupling. Then you booked my line. We're the same, you and I. We just got here in a different order."

"I'm not you."

"No. You'd have done it for her." He stepped back, out of the doorway, onto the bottom stair, and stood aside. "You are doing it."

She looked at him for as long as it took the readout behind her to tick over.

Then she gathered the slips off the shelf. The session first, in order. The two pages of the hold, with her signature at the foot. The header and the after-report with her four answers down its back. Last, her own decode log, face down, her name on it as the one who had cut the word, and the team report with her name first. She folded them once and put them inside her coat, and he watched her do it.

She walked past him.

On the panel by the door, as she went through, a line wrote itself in the Spire's grey. Exit logged. E. Vázquez. 22:58.

"Go home, Elena," he said behind her, low. "Sleep."

She climbed the stair. He didn't follow.

· · ·

The heat met her at the street doors like a hand.

She stood on the Spire's steps in it. The plaza was empty except for a cleaning machine going round the fountain that hadn't run since the drought before last. The towers stood mirrored to their crowns, warm light below, and at the end of every eastward street the wall. No wind off the harbour. Thirty-one degrees at eleven at night, and the air thick enough to drink.

Under her coat the paper had already begun to give. She could feel it through her shirt, the slips going soft at their edges, taking the city's damp into themselves. She pressed her arm across it.

It was raining at the back of her head. The sky over the plaza was clear.

The warmth came up behind her eyes where it always came, and leaned, and she let it. It was the only warm thing on the steps that wasn't the air.

Don't be angry, Bichito, her sister said. Come home.
