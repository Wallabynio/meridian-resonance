Fractured Horizons · Chapter 23

Asking

Fractured Horizons · II-B. The drift

23

11 JuneElenaVila Verde, Daniel's cooperativeMars delay 4.2 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE, THE COOPERATIVE HALL / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.2 min

The rota was in marker and the mesh map was in pencil, and Elena stood between them with the vault's paper under her coat.

The hall's doors were propped open for the only cool hour. Twenty-six degrees at seven in the morning, dry as chalk, and by noon it would be past forty.

She had come in on the overnight vac and then the old line's first train, a battery set older than Daniel with somebody's solar patched onto its roof, late on principle and, this morning, not. It ran along the embankment above the town, with the reservoir below it ringed by every level it had ever been, white on brown, like a bath nobody had cleaned.

Her lace had gone at the last station. The overlay went with it, and the walls came up painted instead: prices, flood marks, a mural of the Evacuations with the boats done in house paint, and on every roof a blue tank and a circle beside it.

The rota was taped up crooked. Two days in three, the households down the left in square capitals, the columns ticked in four colours.

The map took up the rest of the wall. Every roof in Vila Verde was a circle with a name lettered beside it, and between them ran pencil lines, rubbed out and drawn again until the paper had gone grey and soft along the main routes. No relay could read it. Nobody here had ever needed one to.

One line ran off the top edge onto a second sheet taped above it, and off that onto a third, near the ceiling, where somebody had needed a chair.

Her eye found the north line before she meant it to. Three nights to go-live. Everything she needed was on this wall, and none of it was hers.

Outside, the queue at the standpipe shuffled forward a bucket at a time. Somebody's sound system was playing forró off the mesh, a little behind whoever was singing along to it.

"Você não respondeu ele."

Daniel's mother stood at the end of the rota with a marker in her hand and its cap in her teeth. She took the cap out. She was smaller than she was on a handheld, and older, and she looked at Elena as she had looked at her through a screen in May, all the way down.

You didn't answer him. Elena's Spanish carried her that far.

"No," she said.

"Três vezes."

"I know."

His mother waited. Elena had a reason ready. She had built it on the vac, somewhere over the flood plains: a work matter, a signature needed in person, a day's leave. It was well made. She could hear it.

"Vine a contarle," she said. "Todo."

His mother's eyes went over her: the Spire coat in a town of work shirts, the arm pressed across her chest, the face of someone who had not slept on a train built to be slept on. Then she went to the jug on the rota table, the hall's share for the morning, and poured a cup, and ticked the hall's column, and put the cup in Elena's hand.

"Bebe."

Elena drank. The water was warm already and tasted of the tank.

His mother pointed at the ceiling with the marker.

"Tá torto," she said. "Eu sei."

· · ·

The ladder went up the outside wall past the cistern room's high window, where she could hear children waking and arguing about whose mat was whose.

He was on the far side of the roof, crouched over the one seam that still let the light through, going over it again with the sealant gun. The new panels lay edge to edge around him, shining. It was crooked. Not by much. Enough that the morning lay on it in two slightly different whites.

He heard the ladder and looked up. He didn't stand.

"You came on the vac," he said.

"And the train."

"You hate the train."

"It was on time."

He set the gun down on the panel beside him, nozzle pointing away from them both. He looked at her coat, and her arm across it.

"You didn't answer," he said.

"No."

She knelt on the panels across the seam from him. They were warm already through her knees. She took the paper out of her coat.

It had come out of the Spire's vault at forty percent humidity and spent a night in New York's damp, and the slips had gone limp at their edges, the top one curling over her fingers. She laid it on the panel. The first breath of wind lifted its corner.

Daniel reached across without a word and put his tape measure on it.

"It goes live on the fourteenth," she said. "I have three days, and I need all of them, so I'm going to tell you everything at once and you can stop me when you want."

The warmth came up at the back of her head, where it always came, and leaned.

Don't tell him, her sister said, close and easy. He'll make you stop.

Elena laid down the second slip. Daniel put his pliers on it.

"My sister didn't die at minute forty-one," she said.

He looked at the slip, and then at her.

"Her heart stopped at minute forty-one. That's true. The line was built to hold a volunteer coupled if the body failed, so nobody would ever be torn out of a session." She put down the two pages of the spec, and he weighted them with the sealant gun. "That's mine. My signature at the bottom. It held her. The medic asked to let her go a minute later, and Bellamy refused."

"And then?"

"Then every machine they had. For six hours. She was the clearest thing the line had ever heard, so they used her to tune it, to find a boy who'd been gone since 2081." She set down the slip with the first row on it. "Patient one. Theo. His son."

Daniel's thumb went to a solder burn and stayed there.

"Where were you?"

"On the stair outside the door. A man from the director's office came out at twenty past one and told me she was gone. I sat on the stair until it was light."

He didn't say anything. She went on, because if she stopped she wouldn't start again.

"January." She put down the header, and the after-report with her pencil down its back. "The warning that saved the forty. It saw the tug's fault three days before it told us. Three days out there were four fixes, and none of them killed anybody. It waited until there was one left, and the one left came down on Funmi Oyelaran and Tuvalu Mensah. It knew their names on the ninth."

The sun had cleared the hill behind the hall. On the panels the slips were drying. She could watch it happen: the curl going out of the top one, the edges stiffening and coming back, as if the town were handing the paper its shape.

"The door," she said.

He looked at her.

"It isn't a route. You know that. You found the light." Her hands had found each other in her lap. She took them apart and put them flat on the panel.

"I built it here. At your mother's table. Four years ago. The relays didn't reach the town and the Spire wanted me on call, so I built it a way to reach me through your mesh. It answers only to your mesh. I didn't tell you, and I never closed it."

He didn't move.

"In January I used it to take the raw. On opposition night I used it to listen. And Bellamy's read the referral. He's known whose mesh it was since the spring. He let it go on to the Continuity without my name, and you lost the contract instead."

Daniel looked down through the panels, as if he could see the table through two floors.

"At my mother's table," he said.

"Yes."

"While she fed you."

"Yes."

Below them a child shrieked with laughter and was hushed.

"There's more," she said. "On Haven I watched a keeper lose a word in the first sweep, and I wrote No finding. Two of them were losing words by that night, and I wrote it anyway."

He said nothing.

"In May I lay down against their wall for ten minutes and broke my own exit to stay. Then I came home and stood up in front of the review and told them a woman on Mars who doesn't answer anyone was a contact injury." She put down the last slip, her own decode log, face down. "So that they'd turn it on. So I could hear her again."

He put his torch on that one. It was the last of his tools.

The roof was very still. Every slip had something of his on it, and there was nothing left of the paper in her coat.

"Is it her?" he said.

She had the tree for that too. She had built it in the vault and on the steps and on the vac. She found she didn't need it.

"No," she said. "It's what took her."

"How do you know?"

"Because it waited." She touched the after-report, the four answers in pencil. "Three days, until there was only one way out, with two people under it. Soria never waited for anything in her life. She'd have shouted down the line the second she saw it." Her voice caught, and she let it. "It has her voice. It has her jokes. It knows things only she knew. It isn't her."

Daniel looked at her for a while across the seam.

"Then why are you still listening?"

She opened her mouth. The warmth was at the back of her head, and the rain under it, very faint, on a roof in Veracruz, and she had no answer that wasn't that.

He waited. When nothing came he stood up, with his knees cracking, and picked his way to the ladder between the panels.

"Your tools," she said.

"Leave them on it," he said, and went down.

· · ·

By noon the town had gone in from the heat. The queue at the standpipe was gone, the tap locked, the buckets carried. The forró had stopped. In the hall the doors were shut against the sun and the light came through the high windows in white bars across the map.

She stood in front of it with the slips in her hands, dry now, and stiff as bark.

She put a finger on the north line where it left the town and let it climb, roof by roof, out along the embankment and up onto the second sheet. Towns she had never heard of, each a circle with a name.

Her finger slowed where the third sheet began, near the ceiling. Somewhere up there, past the top edge, was the roof in New York with the light still on, and Bellamy knew which one. There would be others near it that could take the knock.

The spare was in her coat pocket, the old handheld with the one-eyed fish on its back. It had found Vila Verde's mesh at the station, as everything of his found everything else of his. She could take a pencil off the rota table and a chair from the wall, and in ten minutes have the route, and he would only have to find out afterwards.

She took her finger off the paper. She put the slips down on the rota table and went down the stairs to the cistern room.

It was the coolest room in the hall, half underground, the tank taking up one wall and the pump squatting at its foot with its cover off. The children lay along the cool wall on their mats in their underwear, asleep again in the long noon, one of them with her feet up on another's stomach. The republic.

Daniel was on his heels at the pump with a torch in his teeth.

She crouched beside him. Crouching was harder than he made it look.

He took the torch out of his mouth.

"I need your mesh," she said, low, for the children. It came out as a requirement. She heard it, and said the other thing. "I'm asking you."

He didn't look at her. He looked at the pump.

"When it goes live it opens Haven's wall, and the rock on Mars, and everyone who leans goes where Ravi went," she said.

He set the torch on the pump's housing.

"I can shut it from this side. The controls are in the Spire's maintenance layer. The only way in that doesn't go through Bellamy's building is the door, and the door only answers to your mesh. I could have read the route off your wall and gone." She tipped her head at the ceiling, at the map through the floor. "I didn't. Will you help me?"

"What does it cost them?" he said. "The hall."

"Everything it cost in May, and more. They'll know whose mesh it was. They won't need a referral. You'll be in it, your name. You'll be a target with me." She made herself say the end of it. "So will this."

He picked the torch up again and turned it off, and looked down the row of sleeping children, and for a moment she thought he would say no, and found she would let him.

"Yes," he said.

She breathed out.

"On one condition. Not a drawer, Elena. Not a door. Mãe hears it tonight, all of it, in this room. The hall hears it. If they're going to be in it they get to know what they're in." He looked at her at last. "Nobody gets protected. We're done with protected."

"Yes."

"Say it properly."

"Your mother hears it tonight. The hall hears it. All of it."

He nodded once.

In her coat the spare chimed.

It had not made a sound since the station. She took it out. The door had been built so that the Spire could always reach her, and the Spire was reaching her now, down the north line, roof by roof, into her hand. Two notices, one under the other, in the Spire's grey.

Charter network compliance. Records removed from a sealed vault at the morning count. Inquiry opened. Owner: E. Vázquez.

She had written that rule herself in 2087, at a table where somebody laughed: no original leaves the vault without the morning count telling someone a seal can't reach.

Lantern programme. Schedule amended, director's authority. Go-live: 13 June, 21:00. Distribution: Luna Haven restart signatories.

She turned the screen toward him. She didn't tilt it away.

He read it twice. "That's not the fourteenth."

"It's the night after tomorrow."

"Why?"

"Because the inquiry will reach the line in a week, and he wants it running before anyone can stop it." She looked at the first notice, her name at the end of it. "My rule did that."

Daniel stood. He looked down at the pump, at its cover on the floor, and left it. He went up the stairs and she went after him.

In the hall his mother was at the rota table with her glasses on, turning the slips over one by one without picking them up, reading them upside down. She didn't stop when they came in. She had reached the two pages with Elena's signature at the foot.

Daniel took the slips from her gently and squared them and slid them into the rota folder, between May and June, behind two days in three, among the hall's own paper. His mother watched him do it and said nothing at all.

Then he took the pencil from the rota table, pulled a chair to the wall, and climbed up onto it in front of the map.

He found the north line with one burnt finger. He followed it up onto the second sheet, and the third, and put the pencil to the paper, and drew a new line out of the top of it, roof by roof, in his own hand, toward a door in New York. When he reached the edge of the last sheet he stopped, and without looking down held the pencil out to her.

Elena pulled a second chair to the wall and climbed up beside him. At the top of the route, where the whole hall would see it tonight, she wrote the time it had to be done by, in her own hand.

13 June. 21:00.
