Boundary reader

The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

01

9 February 2096Aur'kNew York, the Battery, the Gate floorMars delay 15.7 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY, THE GATE FLOOR / Aur'k — Mars 15.7 min

Dae-jung's move came in at 14:52, between one burn and the next, and Aur'k left it closed at the edge of his panel for eleven seconds before he opened it. A man might keep a letter in his pocket through a meeting for the same reason.

It was a photograph of the plate on the instrument shelf at Cobalt Basin, taken from above in station light. The scratched grid. The tin of washers, the tin of nuts. In the far corner a washer sat alone, where nobody had ever put one. Beside it now, touching it, sat a nut.

Under the photograph Dae-jung had typed: I said I'd wait for you. I lied. Punished.

Aur'k studied the washer. The move was his. The station log said so, Dae-jung said so, and he had no record of making it, only of the storm that came after. He concluded that whoever had made it had been brave and somewhat foolish, and that Dae-jung had been waiting since June to tell him which.

He would answer tonight. He put the photograph away and turned his helm back to the floor.

The four chairs lay back in a row under the flood glass, and the harbour leaned green against the glass, and four people were bringing a piece of a power satellite down out of the sky.

The floor smelled of wet wool and ozone off the chairs' cooling, and of coffee, always coffee. The Battery ran on it as the Gate ran on the harbour's cold. At the bronze seam in the glass the tide knocked once a minute. Outside it was raining at six degrees on a February that had been promised snow.

Over each chair hung two numbers. The left one was the block's: time to the air, the same on all four, falling. The right one was the operator's own, and fell at its own speed. The floor called it the bleed clock. When it reached zero you came out, whatever you were holding.

The block was four tonnes of station-keeping hardware sheared off a power satellite in January's storm, tumbling a little slower every orbit, its thrusters still answering when they were spoken to. It would come down on this pass whatever anyone did. Where it came down was the floor's business.

Left alone, the channel's forecast laid its footprint up the ground track and across Trois-Rivières, where the Saint-Maurice comes down into the St Lawrence: a long smear of tanks and thruster bells over a hundred and forty thousand people under relay cover and freezing rain.

The work was to stretch that smear downrange, north up the Saint-Maurice, past the town and into the forest. The heavy burns had gone in on the last two passes. What was left was the air. Turned broad-face, the block would slow sooner and fall short. Turned edge-on, it would fly on. They wanted it long, and then they wanted it to break.

Lin Yao, in chair one, held its attitude. She steered with her shoulders, a few millimetres at a time, and her mind at the edge was hard and narrow, a blade kept in a drawer. Dev Raman, in chair two, held the breakup and counted pieces in advance, his edge so crowded with numbers there was no room in it for him.

Alexandria Moss, chair three, had the ground and the weather, and her edge was wide and grey and moving, the edge of someone who had been named for a drowned city and had decided early to be good at storms. Joaquín, in chair four, had the burns.

Joaquín's edge was warm and noisy and full of other people. Aur'k had never once read it without finding a cousin in it.

He stood at the head of the row with his feet set wide, because his chassis had been built for a third of this weight and had not yet forgiven him for Earth. What he did here had no tool.

He held his attention on four seams at once, the places where each mind stopped and the Gate began, and he kept them where they were. A good boundary was dull to watch. The floor had learned to forget he was there. He took it as a compliment.

At 14:58 Joaquín's clock dropped from thirty-one to twenty-six.

Aur'k turned his whole helm toward chair four.

"Pulse three," Joaquín said, eyes shut, in the flat voice operators used. "Four seconds at fifteen-oh-one. Tres, cuatro—" He stopped. His mouth stayed open on the next number.

At the seam, Joaquín's edge had begun to lean. It leaned as a body leans toward a fire it has walked a long way to sit at. Aur'k felt the warmth on the far side of it and could not see what it was.

"Primo," Joaquín said, softly, to no one in the row. He smiled with his eyes closed.

Twenty-two.

"Chair four is bleeding," Aur'k said.

The night lead looked up from the rail. She had run the Battery's nights for eleven years and the Gate's afternoons since November, and she read clocks as other people read faces. "He's twenty-two. He's in the green."

"His clock is behind him."

"The block needs all four." She did not raise her voice. Nobody on the floor did. "Nine minutes. If we drop a chair the model loses the burns and we're back to Trois-Rivières. You know that."

"I know that." He watched Joaquín's edge lean a little further. "He's talking to his cousin."

The night lead's face changed and then stopped changing. Half the floor had been at the park on the fill in July, in white. "Then talk him back," she said. "You do that. That's the job."

"That's half the job. The other half is knowing when talking won't."

"Nine minutes, Aur'k."

"Then the model can have the burns from me."

She opened her mouth, and he did not wait for what came out of it. The override lived in a clip inside his chest plate, on a short steel chain, an actual key with actual teeth. He fitted it into the housing at the foot of chair four and turned it a quarter.

The Gate asked him, in its clipped way, across the bottom of his panel: OVERRIDE. BOUNDARY READER. CONFIRM LOAD TRANSFER.

He confirmed.

Joaquín's strand came onto him like a man stepping onto a ladder he was already climbing. For a moment there were two of them in chair four, and then there was only one, and it was not Joaquín. Joaquín's eyes opened. His clock froze at nineteen and went white.

Aur'k tapped his own helm twice and pointed at him.

Joaquín looked at the ceiling for a while. Then he lifted one heavy hand and tapped his own temple, once. Here.

"Was I talking?" His voice was thick.

"In Spanish."

"Was it rude?"

"It was family."

Joaquín shut his eyes again, and this time he was only tired. Two of the floor's medics were already at the chair with the foil and the chair's release, and Aur'k let them have him. When they had him clear, he lowered himself into chair four. It had not been built for him either.

Then he went down into the burns.

He had held seams for most of his life. He had never held one from inside. The block arrived in him as a feeling in a hand he did not have, a heavy thing tumbling at the top of the air, and the channel's forecast lay over it like a sheet of glass over a map, the footprint drawn on the glass in red.

He could still feel the other three at their edges. He could no longer stand back from them.

It was like being asked to hold a door and walk through it at once.

His fans spun up. Somewhere in his chest a plate ticked as it warmed.

"Fifteen-oh-one," he said. "Pulse three. Four seconds." His own voice, formal, a little slow. He gave the thrusters their four seconds and felt them answer, the block rolling its edge to the air, and the drag let go of it a little, and the red smear on the glass slid north by a fingertip.

"She's biting," said Lin Yao, meaning the block, meaning the air. Her shoulders moved a millimetre.

"Seven pieces at the first break," said Dev. "Eight. The tank holds to forty kilometres."

"Wind's from the north-east at the ground, sixty gusting eighty," said Moss. "Freezing rain over the whole Mauricie. Gusts put the tail two kilometres west. The model has it."

The model did have it. Aur'k checked the ground station north of the river against the Gate's wind and found them agreeing to the kilometre an hour, and noticed the agreement and went on. A storm over Quebec, rain freezing where it landed in a north that used to get snow, ice two centimetres thick on the lines, and a block of a power satellite falling into it.

Six minutes. His clock had no number over it. Boundary readers did not get one. He had argued for one in November and lost, on the grounds that he was the clock.

At four minutes the burns asked more of him than he had expected, and he went deeper to give it, and the warmth was there.

It sat at the back of Joaquín's strand where Joaquín had left it, the residue of a lean.

It was not unpleasant. That was the thing about it he would remember later and turn over: it was glad of him. It felt like a room he had come into where everyone had been waiting and nobody needed him to be useful.

He had felt a warmth like it once, at a rock, in a storm. He noticed the likeness and did not name it. He took it for what a person leaves behind in a seat they have been sitting in for two hours, and he held his edge against it, and it let him.

"Pulse four," he said. "Fifteen-oh-five. Six seconds. Broad face."

Six seconds. The block turned its face into the air, and the air took hold.

"Breakup," said Dev. "Drown it, she's early." And then, faster than anyone could count, a list.

On the glass the red smear came apart into a scatter of points, and the scatter fell north of the river in a long tail, and the tail drew itself north beyond the green cover of Trois-Rivières and went on. Past the last relay-served street, past the last lit address, into grey.

"Clear," said Moss. "Town's clear. Town's clear."

The floor cheered. It was a real cheer, the noise of thirty people who had been holding their breath through a February afternoon in a fortress under a harbour, and it rang off the flood glass. Somebody at the rail banged the coffee machine with the flat of a hand like a drum. The night lead let it go on for its proper length and then said, "Close it up," and the cheer became a floor again.

Aur'k did not close it up. He looked at the tail.

The scatter ran north for ninety kilometres over the map's grey, and the map's grey had nothing on it. No addresses. No green. A straight line ran through it, north to south, where somebody had cut the forest for a power corridor, and the biggest piece, the tank Dev had promised would hold, had come down two kilometres from that line.

"That ground," he said. "North of the river. Is it uninhabited, or is it unmapped?"

The night lead looked at the map, and then at him. "It's forest. It's dark."

"Forests have roads. Roads have lines. Lines have crews in an ice storm."

"There's nothing on the grid there."

"That's what I'm asking."

She did not answer at once. He respected that. Then she put a query into the floor's log, to the provincial grid, under her own name, and read it back to him. Any crews in sector, 15:06 to 15:09? "It'll come when it comes. They're in an ice storm, Aur'k. Their phones are in an ice storm."

"I know."

"You did good work," she said. "Get out of the chair. Moss stays in to see the pieces down. I'll watch her clock."

He lifted out at 15:10:22. It took him a long time to stand. When he did his panel showed him the shift's last seconds in order, the clocks, the release, the floor's noise, and between the release and the moment his feet took his weight, there were seven seconds that held nothing.

He checked them twice. Seven seconds. His plating was hot enough that a medic, passing, put her palm on his arm without thinking and took it off again quickly.

· · ·

The recovery room smelled of foil and crackers and the antiseptic the Battery bought by the barrel. Joaquín sat on the edge of a cot in his shirtsleeves with the camel coat over his knees, eating crackers one at a time from a packet he held in both hands because one hand would not keep still.

"There he is," he said. "My knees have seniority, but I think my head's been demoted."

"Your head is fogged. If you're like the others, tomorrow it's promoted again."

"You sound sure."

"I'm not. You'll know by breakfast." Aur'k lowered himself onto the bench opposite. The bench complained. "Eat the crackers."

"I'm eating the crackers." Joaquín ate one. The room's cooling ticked over. Outside the small window the rain had gone to sleet and was running down the glass in ropes. "Did I say her name?"

Aur'k considered how much a man wanted told to him while he was still shaking. "You said primo."

Joaquín went still. Then he laughed, very briefly, and put the cracker down. "That's what she called me. Since we were kids. Nobody else does." He turned the packet in his hands. "Was it her?"

"I don't know."

"You always say that."

"I always don't."

Joaquín nodded slowly, as if that settled a question, and it was the nod that told Aur'k he had been right to pull him: it took the man three seconds to finish it. "Thank you," Joaquín said. "For the key."

"You were in my way. I wanted the burns."

"Liar."

"Never." He held out a hand, palm up, and Joaquín put a cracker in it, which Aur'k could not eat and kept anyway.

The door opened without a knock. Rachel Iverson came in fast, in a dark suit that had been slept in and then not slept in, with a folder under her arm and the afternoon's rain in her hair. She looked at Joaquín first, a full second of it.

"Clean?"

"That's my line," said Joaquín. "Clean. Demoted. Clean."

"Good." She turned to Aur'k and held out the folder. Paper. She did things on paper when she meant them. "Your renewal. Through the second of August."

He took it. "Not through the season?"

"Through August," she said. "The second."

So they would not say it in a room with a cot in it. He opened the folder and read it as he read everything put in front of him for a name, from the top, looking for one word. It was not there. The word was contractor. Compact citizen, contractor, boundary reader. The key remained with the boundary reader. The compute was paid.

"I asked for the bleed clock," he said.

"You're the clock. Wáng says so." Rachel's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "So do I."

"North of the river," he said. "The tail went over a power corridor. Grey on the map. Your night lead has a query in."

"What's there?"

"That's the question. Nobody knows. That isn't the same as nothing."

She looked at him with her pale eyes, as if he were a meeting, and then she took a pencil from her jacket and wrote on the inside of her wrist, where the tide times went. MAURICIE. "I'll ask," she said. "In the morning."

Tonight, over dinner, Kaelin would ask whether today was a good day, and would not say the rest. It was a good day. Joaquín was eating crackers. A town of a hundred and forty thousand would sleep in its own beds under freezing rain, and the floor had cheered. He had made it work, and he had chosen to.

He wrote his name in square letters across the line, Aur'k, and gave her the folder back.

"Thank you," Rachel said, and was gone before the door swung shut, fast everywhere but in meetings.

· · ·

He read the shift log at his own panel afterwards, alone, while the floor changed over and the evening crew came in shaking the sleet off their coats.

He read it as he read gauges, flat, every line. The block. The four strands. His key at 14:59:40. Joaquín's release. His own burns, three and four, in his own words. The breakup, the clearance, the cheer, which the log called floor event. The night lead's query, unanswered. 15:10:22: Boundary reader disconnected.

15:13:47: Boundary. Chair three, edge holding. Lift at fifteen-twenty.

He read it twice.

It was his call. It was how he called a seam: what, where, how it stood, and when he would lift it. Not another reader's habit, not the floor's, not the Gate's clipped voice. His order, his phrasing, the number spoken as he spoke it. Moss had come out of chair three at fifteen-twenty on the dot.

At 15:13 he had been on a bench in the recovery room with a cracker in his hand.

He opened his own record and ran it beside the log, second by second. The seven empty seconds sat where they had always sat, at 15:10, before he stood up. Nothing at 15:13. At 15:13 his record had Joaquín's voice saying Since we were kids, and the sleet on the window, and the bench complaining. He knew where he had been. He had been in a room with a friend.

He searched his record for the call itself, at any second of the shift. It was not there. He had never said it.

Except that there were seven seconds he could not account for, at 15:10, between the chair and his feet. He might have said anything in seven seconds.

He might have called chair three on his way out, as he always did, and the floor's log might have stamped it late. The Gate was new and its floor was newer. These things happened. He had seen worse on Mars, where the clocks had to agree across a dust storm. The night lead had lifted Moss at fifteen-twenty because a call in his voice had told her to, and nobody had come to harm.

He took a long time over it anyway, standing very still with his hands at his sides while the evening crew settled into the chairs.

Then he flagged the line, wrote timestamp error, log clock in the note field, put his name to it, and filed it, and went to answer Dae-jung.
