Cobalt Basin

The Narrow Gate · II-B. The old story

19

18 JuneElenaMars, Cobalt Basin StationMars delay 21.5 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Elena Vázquez — Earth 21.5 min

The dust came off her suit in a fine red smoke that hung in the stall's light and would not fall, and Elena, who had read about perchlorate in nine weeks of passenger digests, still put her hand out to it.

"Don't," said the woman at the dust-off station, and slapped the glove down. "It's salt that bites. You'll taste it for a week anyway. Turn."

Elena turned. Mars came with her. After nine weeks on the spin ring it was heavier than she had planned for, a third of her weight that went on being there, and her knees had argued with every step down the lander's ramp and up the rover's. Somewhere under this deck the Drop went down to the rock. She had come to go down it, this week, before anything else could stop her. Aur'k stood in the next stall with his arms out while two crew went over his seams with brushes and a hand vacuum, the grit lifting off his plating and settling straight back.

"Pole, big man," the brush woman told him. "You've brought half the plain in."

"I've brought you a passenger."

"I can smell her." She sniffed, not unkindly. "Earth. Rain and somebody's soap. It wears off."

The inner lock cycled. Warm air came through it, and the smell of the station: people, ozone, hot electronics, and under them a green smell, wet leaves, the greenhouse breathing through the walls. Then Ines Varga, in station blues with a medic's pockets, her hair gone greyer than her file and tied back with what looked like suture.

"There he is," Ines said. "You could have written."

"I did write."

"You could have written a thing I wanted to read." She put her hand flat on his chest plate, as she did with patients, and left it there for a count Elena could see her taking. Then she took it away and slapped the plate twice. "Full air. My knees are worse. Thanks for asking."

"Your knees were always worse."

"They're competitive." She turned, and looked at Elena the length of a breath, and nodded once, as you nod to someone whose name you have read too often. "Vázquez."

"Ines."

Dae-jung was behind her, pale sage green, his smooth skull tipped to one side. He did not say hello. He took a washer from his pocket and held it out to Aur'k on his open palm.

"Your move," he said. "It's been your move since May."

Aur'k looked at it for a long time. "What did you do to the corner?"

"I developed a strategy. You weren't here to see it." Dae-jung closed his hand on the washer and put it away. "I'll show you the board. You'll want to sit down."

Elena found she was smiling and didn't stop. Then she saw the fourth person in the lock passage and stopped.

Mara Ekeberg stood with one palm on the wall, her braid over her shoulder, a keeper's suit under a station jacket that didn't fit her. She took the palm away and looked at it.

"Your walls are dead," she said to Ines.

"They're walls."

"I know. I keep touching them." Mara looked at Elena. "You got thin again."

"You're on Mars."

"Haven's courier. A week after the vote, nine of us and the mail. We came in on the seventh." The Haven pause, while she chose how much. "I've got a Ward full of people who breathe and don't come up. I came to ask what's holding them."

"Who did you ask?"

"Nobody." Mara looked at Aur'k. "I was waiting for him."

· · ·

The chief met them outside ops with a slate already open. She stood as Continuity officers stand, as if the deck might be inspected, and read Elena's entry while Elena watched her read it.

"I'll need the boundary module," Elena said. "Tomorrow. Early."

"Vázquez, E. Civic clearance, New York. Interface licence suspended under inquiry. Lifetime dose reached, return passage only." She looked up. "Your link is suspended. On this station that means you touch no lattice. Ours or anybody's."

"I wasn't going to touch yours."

"The boundary is the boundary lead's. The lattice is nobody's, on your licence." The chief heard Elena's silence out, and didn't fill it. That was the answer. She turned the slate.

"Water," she said. "You're on the station's newcomer allowance. Four minutes, every third day, at your slot. It goes in the log."

Elena read the figure. "The ship came in today."

"The ship came in light." The chief brought the manifest up beside it without being asked. "Your window moved two days. Two days later the burn was longer, and Haven's fuel was the thinnest margin they sell, so the hold went down to margin. Water's heavy. It came off first." She closed it. "We've rationed since the Lantern. We go on rationing."

Elena did the sum before she could stop herself. Two days, a longer burn, a hold cut to make mass. Rachel had bought those two days, to keep Aur'k for the wave. And Elena had put Aur'k on the manifest herself.

"Take mine for the ward," she said. "The allowance. I don't need it."

"Water isn't a gift here. It's a line in a log." The chief nearly smiled, and didn't. "You'll initial at your slot like everybody. What you do with your minutes after that is between you and the people behind you in the queue."

· · ·

Medical had five bunks. The ward had taken the stores module next door, and stores had gone wherever stores went: a long low room under the berm with cots from the Alexandria in two rows, the stencils still on the frames, and the monitors over them showing ten traces in a column, Petra's from next door among them, all moving, all slow.

It smelled of skin cream and the feed bags and of bodies kept clean on very little water.

Petra was in the fifth bunk in medical, the first one Elena saw through the hatch, her dark hair brushed out on the pillow, twelve breaths to the minute. Somebody had put a lighthouse card in her hand and folded her fingers over it. Selma and Bao lay on the first two cots in the ward, turned toward each other. The galley man, his hand open now. Five more Elena only knew from the files.

She had sent a time to Mars. She had kept the count herself. This was what the count had been.

In medical's second bunk Hemi Rangi was breathing wrong. She heard it from the hatch: a wet catch at the top of each breath, a sound a pipe makes with water in it. Ines was already at him with a suction line, and she didn't look round.

"Basin," Ines said. "Under the cart. Hold it there. Both hands."

Elena held it. She knelt on a deck that pressed her knees with a third of her weight and held a steel basin under a man's chin while Ines cleared his throat, and he didn't wake, and his eyes moved under the lids as if he were watching weather. When it was done Ines took the basin and emptied it and didn't give it back.

"He's had that a week," Ines said. "A year flat. Lungs don't like a year flat."

"Is he—"

"I don't lie to anyone in my beds. He's in one." Ines wiped her hands. "Ask me tomorrow."

· · ·

The boundary module smelled of cold cable and old dust, and the monitors along its wall showed the rock's hiss in a slow grey band. Aur'k had his plating open at one side and a line running from it into the wall. He was getting himself back. She could see it in how fast his helm turned when she came in.

"Three times what the ship gave me," he said. "I'd forgotten what it's like to finish a thought in the room it started in."

"I want to go down," Elena said. "This week."

He didn't answer at once. She knew his lag now, and this was longer, and it was his.

"On the ship," he said, "you said you wouldn't ask."

"You said I would." She stood where she could see the hatch and the monitors both. "I'm asking. A descent. Your log, your clock, you at the top. I go down there and I ask them what the Gate is, and I bring back a thing Rachel can't call noise." And whether it was her. She didn't say that. She didn't need to. He had been in the shelter.

"It costs."

"I know it costs."

"You know a sentence." He closed the panel on his side, carefully. "Kaelin went down twice. The second time Zhenyth put him out, and a piece of the Glow went dark where his head was. The width of a hand. It's still dark. It doesn't grow back. There isn't more of them being made, Elena. There's what hid. Every time someone goes down, there's less."

"And every hour the Gate runs there are more people on it who might not come up." She heard her own review-room voice and couldn't stop it. "Lin. Raman. Moss. Joaquín. You've seen Joaquín. Weigh it."

"I don't weigh it. It isn't mine to weigh." He turned his helm to the wall, to the grey band. "That's the rule. I don't ask the rock for anything. I didn't ask it for Kaelin. I won't ask it for you."

"Then I'll ask it."

"You can try." He let the silence run, and with his whole mind back it was a choice. "Dawn. I'll take you to the edge. Not down. The edge. You can put your hand on it."

"And if it answers?"

"Then it answers you," Aur'k said. "Not me."

· · ·

Earth night ran in the station's evening. The galley repeater showed the Gulf Crescent at three in the morning, minutes old: Doha's misting arcades running at full in the dark, the street under them crowded, children on scooters, men playing cards on a cooler, a cooling-centre sign in blue Arabic. At night the Gulf came out. By day it hid.

The forecast in the corner said wet-bulb thirty-four by two in the afternoon.

The slate was where it had always been, a cargo panel beside the galley hatch, chalked in columns. Dae-jung had the chalk.

"Ines. Arcades fail in Basra before three."

"They will. They always do."

"Bao says the arcades hold and the grid goes." Dae-jung wrote it under Bao's name in Bao's square letters, as close as he could make them. "He'd have said it. He was always wrong about grids."

"You'd have said it for him anyway," said Ines, from the good chair, the one with arms. She had taken it before anyone else came in.

Dae-jung moved to the next column. PETRA, at the top, in Petra's own hand from a year ago. Under it a column of bets in other hands. He wrote, without asking anyone: Somebody's cheating. Breaks by dark.

Hemi's column was the one after. Four bets in it, the last one dated the week before go-live. Nobody had written in it since and nobody had rubbed it out.

"Newcomer bets," Dae-jung said, and held the chalk out to Elena. "It's custom. You'll lose."

She took it. The slate had no column for her. She drew one, at the far right, ruling it with the side of the chalk, and wrote V. at the top because her whole name would not fit, and under it: Thunder off the gulf by four. No rain.

"Cruel," said Ines. "She's learning."

Mara had not bet. She sat at the end of the long table with a cup of hot water she wasn't drinking, watching the repeater as if Haven had never shown her weather. Then she turned her head, fast, toward the hatch to the ward.

Elena heard it a second later. Every monitor in the ward chiming the same note at once.

On the repeater over the slate the small boundary inset, which showed the rock's grey band all evening and which nobody looked at, stood up. One clean swell, like a held breath, and down again. Through the hatch the ten traces on the ward wall had stood up together. Then they lay down.

Nobody in the galley moved. Ines didn't even get out of the chair.

"That's the eight o'clock," Dae-jung said, and took a pencil from behind the slate and made a small mark on the frame's edge, beside a long column of small marks. Elena saw that there were thousands of them. They went down the frame and onto the bulkhead.

"What's the eight o'clock?"

"New York's eight o'clock." He wrote the time beside the mark. 20:21. "It comes every hour, at twenty-one past, these weeks. It moves with the light. In the winter it came later. Since November."

She looked at the galley clock, which kept New York's time because the crew did, and then at the column of times, and her hand went to the back of her other hand for a pencil that wasn't in it.

The floor changes chairs on the hour. Kaelin's photograph of the March rota, the clock column down its side. On the hour, and then the light's crossing.

"It's the Gate," she said. "That's the Battery changing chairs."

Mara put her cup down.

"Every hour?" said Mara.

"Every hour they change." Elena was still looking at the marks. Thousands. Every shift since November, read off this rock under this galley a light-crossing later. "It's the Spire line. They calibrated it against this rock in 2089. It still hears it. It's still—" She stopped, because the next word was tied, and she didn't know that, and she would not say what she didn't know. "It moves them," she said.

"It moves them," Dae-jung agreed. "Twenty seconds. Then they settle. Ines says it's nothing she can treat."

"It's nothing I can treat," said Ines.

Elena put the chalk down on the slate's ledge, square to the edge, and found Mara watching her hands do it.

· · ·

At three in the morning New York, the middle of nothing on Mars, Elena was on a stool in the ward with a pencil she had borrowed from Dae-jung and a column of numbers on the back of her hand. She had stopped counting the marks. She was counting breaths.

Ines had turned them all at two, Ines the shoulders and Mara the hips, because Elena had asked to help and Ines had said Watch first. Mara turned people as if she had done it before. She had. Elena had read her eleven. But Mars was twice Haven's weight, and after each turn Mara stood with a hand on the cot frame until her legs agreed to it.

Hemi's catch came, and went, and came.

Elena watched the clock over the hatch. Twenty-one past was coming. If he went at twenty-one past, the Gate had done it. She caught herself wanting that, and knew the shape of the want, and didn't put it down.

At nine minutes past three the catch came at the top of his breath and the breath didn't finish.

Ines was there before the monitor said so. She put her hand on his chest and waited, and her lips moved on her watch, and nothing came under her hand. She waited longer than the watch needed. Then she reached up and silenced the monitor with one finger, and the ward was the other nine breathing.

"Three oh nine," Ines said. "Hemi Rangi."

Elena looked at the clock. Twelve minutes before the next one. Forty-eight after the last. Nothing on the boundary inset over the hatch but grey.

No tremor. A chest, after a year. He had been asleep a year because she had sent a time.

She had built ride-throughs so that sessions wouldn't drop. She had built a cut so that one would. She knew the design of both. She sat on the stool with the pencil, explaining it to herself line by line, until she noticed her hands had gone together in her lap and were holding on.

"Get the log," Ines said. "Somebody."

Dae-jung had come to the hatch. He put his fist to his chest plate, sabi, and Mara was already going.

· · ·

The shower log was a clipboard on a cord by the shower hatch, its pages gone soft as cloth, a column of names and slots and minutes and initials, crossings-out in three colours, arguments written up the margins. SAID 4, TOOK 6. — I WAS RINSING A WOUND. — YOUR WOUND HAS RINSED FOR TWO YEARS. Mara brought it back on its cord stretched to the hatch and held it out to Ines.

"He'll go clean," Ines said. "His nan will want to know. Nobody's got to give. Everybody does."

She wrote her own first. VARGA. 4. TO RANGI. And handed it on.

The duty officer had come in from ops in his socks. He took the clipboard and wrote his line without saying anything. For him that was a speech. Two drillers off the night shift. Dae-jung, who used no water, wrote PARK. NIL. TO RANGI. FOR THE NOTE. and passed it on.

Mara wrote hers. Then she held the clipboard out to Elena.

Elena's slot was three days off. Four minutes. She wrote VÁZQUEZ. 4. TO RANGI. and her initials, and the pencil went through the soft paper a little at the second stroke.

Mara didn't take it back at once.

"You knew an hour before," Mara said. "You told me. What a cut does."

"Yes."

"I'm not saying it to hurt you." The Haven pause. "I sat with mine. Eleven. You didn't. So sit with this one."

"I'm sitting."

"I know. I'm saying you have to stay sat." Mara took the log. "That's all."

Ines washed him with Dae-jung holding the warm bag and Elena holding the basin, because Ines put it in her hands again without asking. The water ran into it in a thin patter. Elena's head turned left, toward nothing, toward rain on a roof a world away, and she turned it back and kept her eyes on the man in the cot.

Afterwards Ines sat on the end of the next cot, Selma's, and dictated to her own wrist.

"Kaelin. Hemi died at three oh nine your time. His chest. A week of it. He went clean, the whole station gave. Nobody else up, nobody else gone. Tell his nan if Compact's faster from you." She stopped. "Pole."

She sent it. It would reach him under the Battery before the next change.

"I'll send to his nan on Haven routing," Mara said. "Compact to Compact. It's quicker than yours and she won't have to read a Signatories header."

"Do," said Ines. "Sabi."

At twenty-one past the ward chimed. The nine stood up on the wall together and lay down. Hemi's trace was off the column.

Elena sat with them until the galley clock said morning.

· · ·

Dawn at the boundary was a word. There were no ports in the boundary module, only the wall of monitors and the reader set into the rock face at the end, a plate of grey composite polished by one palm over seven years.

Aur'k put his hand on it.

The grey band on the monitors moved. It answered in the same second, the rock lifting under his hand as a dog lifts its head when it hears its name.

"They're here," he said. "They know I'm back." He took his hand away. The band settled. "Now you."

"The chief said no lattice."

"This isn't lattice. It's them. And it's my log." He touched the log's tab, and it took his palm. "Boundary lead attending. On my name."

Elena took her glove off. The plate was colder than she had expected, and smoother, and she put her palm where his had been, and waited.

The hiss went on.

She waited the length of a breath, and then of ten. If there was a voice in there with her sister's laugh, it would know her hand. She let herself think it once, and held still for the answer. She made herself breathe slowly, as Mara had made her before the wall on Haven. Nothing on the monitors moved. Not the swell, not the answer, not even the small jitter the band carried when the station's pumps ran. Under her hand the plate stayed cold, and the band went on being grey, and gave her nothing at all.

Aur'k put his own hand beside hers, not touching. The band lifted for him at once, and lay down again, flatter than before.

"They've drawn back from the edge," he said. "From where your hand is."

"Why?"

He took nine seconds over it. On Mars, with his whole mind, she knew what nine seconds meant.

"They know who you are," Aur'k said. "You built the line it came through."

She kept her palm on the plate. She thought of Hemi's column on the slate, four bets and the chalk nobody would rub out. Of the marks down the frame onto the bulkhead, every hour since November. Of her own name in the shower log, the pencil through the paper.

"You don't ask it for anything," she said.

"No."

"I know what it costs. You told me. A hand's width, and it doesn't grow back." She took her hand off the plate and turned to him, where a face would be. "Ask them for me."
