The Narrow Gate · Chapter 14

São Paulo

The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

14

14 AprilAur'kthe Gate floorMars delay 19.1 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY, THE GATE FLOOR / Aur'k — Mars 19.1 min

Elena's photograph came in at 10:50, while the floor was still pretending to eat.

It was a roof he had never stood on: the cooperative hall at Vila Verde, flat and white and patched in three colours, a dish at one corner, and behind it the grey east side of São Paulo going away in hills into haze.

Somebody had laid mats out in rows, as for a picnic or a ward. A woman with grey hair pulled back and glasses on a cord stood at the parapet with a pot on her hip. Children sat along the edge with their legs over, looking up at a sky the colour of milk.

Dry season, so Mãe has the whole hall up here airing mats, Elena had typed under it. Nobody here knows why I'm watching the sky. I told her I'm waiting for rain. She says I'll wait till October. E.

He had written to her on Tuesday night why her ship had moved. Nobody had told him not to, and she was a passenger on it. He read the message twice and kept it at the edge of his panel, where he kept Dae-jung's moves.

The floor smelled of coffee and ozone and of a box of pastries Moss had brought from a bakery in the Polder whose owner, she said, owed her for a hurricane. Under THE WAVE on the flood glass Joaquín had ruled a column of minutes, twelve twenty-two to twelve thirty, and was selling them at a pastry each: the minute the big piece went into the sea.

"Twelve twenty-five is mine," he said. "Lin. You haven't bought."

"I don't bet on my own work."

"Honest woman. Dev?"

"Twenty-four," said Dev, and paid in custard.

Aur'k did not eat, and so had never been offered a minute, and found this morning that he minded.

The floor's print of the plan lay on the rail by the coffee machine, three sheets. He read it standing, from the top, as he read anything with a name at the bottom of it.

Thirty-one pieces. Pods on thirty. The core, four hundred tonnes with its thrusters dead, met over the sea by Majuro's lower tip at twelve twenty and brought down short off Santos. And the arm, cut loose at the hit to save the hub, coming down along its own track up the escarpment: a thin red line drawn inland across the grey, three hundred kilometres of it, crossing the eastern reservoir at the dam.

Billings works. Dam, pump stations, intake. Shift crews.

The south side's last full water, in a city that had put up a power satellite when its dams stopped turning. The satellite was coming home. It would drop a tether on the last of the water on its way, and on whoever was on shift at the intake.

"Is that ground uninhabited," he said, "or unmapped?"

The night lead didn't look up from her clocks. "You asked me that in February."

"It came back. It's logged unpopulated."

"Logged," he said. He noticed the word, and that she had chosen it.

"The map doesn't hold them, Aur'k."

"Then the map and I should talk."

Rachel had come up the rail behind him. She was in her dark suit with the cuffs buttoned, and her attention, usually all front, was braced along the side nearest him.

"It's signed," she said.

"I've seen your signature. It's very clear."

"Forty minutes on, twenty out. You're at forty at twelve twelve."

"The catch is at twelve twenty. Who reads after twelve twelve?"

"The catch only has to be held."

It was an answer. It was not to his question either. "It's a good rule," he said. "You wrote it."

She looked at him for the length of a breath. "Fly the plan."

"I'll read it," he said.

It was his job, and it was not a yes, and she heard both, and let it go. She had a city to put to bed.

· · ·

They coupled at 11:32, and the sky came apart.

On the forecast glass it was a sheaf of red tracks coming in off the South Atlantic toward the coast, thirty-one of them, strung along one orbit like beads on a cord. In the chairs it was four minds walking into weather.

Lin held attitude, her edge a blade in a shut drawer. Dev counted pieces before they broke, so crowded with numbers there was no room in him for him.

Moss had the air over the whole metropolis, wide and grey and moving, a storm-child's edge in a dry season. Joaquín had the burns, warm and full of people, with the smooth place in it where a Thursday afternoon should have been. The smooth place held the burns as well as the rest of him did.

Aur'k stood at the head of the row with his feet set wide and held four seams, and the floor forgot him. He took it as the compliment it was.

"Pod twelve," said Joaquín, eyes shut. "Doce. Three seconds."

A tick on the glass. A street in the north of the city that would be there on Sunday.

Panels burned on their own, high and harmless. Where a pod didn't answer, Lin found the attitude to make the air do it, a millimetre of shoulder at a time. By noon twenty-seven tracks ended in white ticks over twenty-two million people under a white sky, who would never hear of the floor.

At 12:04, with the last pods spoken for, he turned his attention to the catch.

The forecast laid it over the glass like a drawing over a drawing. Majuro turning slowly against the Earth, nobody aboard, its lower tip dipping to the top of the air. The core coming down the track to meet it.

At twelve twenty the tip's grapple, made to take loads off planes, would close on four hundred tonnes, and the line would come taut in a second, and the plan cut the arm in that second and let it fall where it fell.

He read the arm's tension along the forecast as Lin read air, and went still.

If the reel ran through the catch, braking the whole run, the load would come on over ninety seconds instead of one. The arm would stretch, a hair off break, the whole ninety. It might hold. It might hold if someone held it.

He had not decided. He stood at the head of the row with four seams in his attention and a city on the glass, and weighed it, and the clock went down past twelve ten, and twelve eleven.

At 12:11:59 he decided.

"Majuro. Hold the cut. Pay the arm out from twelve twenty."

The night lead turned from her clocks. "That's not the plan."

"Inside two per cent of break," said Dev, eyes shut, already counting it. "The whole ninety."

"Then we stay inside two per cent."

"The model cuts because it can't hold two per cent."

"It cuts because it can't feel it. I can." He kept his voice for the log, formal, every word set down. "Core comes down eleven kilometres further in. Still in the sea."

"Still in the sea," said Moss, after a gust.

Rachel was at the rail. "Fly the plan, Aur'k."

"The plan drops three hundred kilometres of tether on the south side's last full reservoir, onto districts your map can't see. I'm the reader. This is my reading."

Her hand went to the earpiece on her left and stayed there. She could have called Majuro's desk. He watched her not do it.

There was no chair for the arm. Nobody had meant anyone to hold it. He held it anyway, from the head of the row, through the housing at his feet, as a fifth strand beside the four. It came onto him heavy and far away, a line three hundred kilometres long with a hand at the bottom of it, and the hand was his.

· · ·

At 12:12 his panel changed colour.

READER RELIEVED 12:12. LINE ON READER MODEL.

He read it as he read gauges, flat, every word. It was in the plan. It was not on the floor's print. It had been written in a hand, somewhere, after he had stood in a hot room with forty-one pages and asked her to stop it answering in his voice when he had gone.

On the glass the line had already taken the catch. It would fly the last eighteen minutes as him. He had no doubt it would fly them well.

"Thermal," said the medic at his elbow. Young, her reader held a hand's breadth off his plating because she had learned not to touch it. "You're over. You're off. That's the rule."

His fans were loud. The key on its chain ticked against his chest plate every time he vented heat, and grit came off his shoulders in a fine red skin onto the boards.

He took the key off its chain. A plain steel blade the length of a finger, the only one on the floor. He fitted it into the housing at the head of the row and turned it a quarter.

OVERRIDE. BOUNDARY READER. CONFIRM: READER HOLDS.

He confirmed.

"Aur'k." Rachel, at the rail. Not loud. Nobody on the floor was loud. Her hand had not left the earpiece.

"You told me I'm the clock," he said. "So I'm telling the time."

He went down the line.

At twelve twenty the grapple closed on the core over the sea, and the hand at the bottom of him took four hundred tonnes.

It arrived as a pull at the end of an arm he did not have, a long animal pull, and then the reel began to run and the brake began to bite, and the pull became a held thing, stretching. He felt the line thin. He felt the hub, far up, lean against its own spin. Two per cent. He read it aloud in words for the log, and his voice came out slow.

"Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine. Ninety-eight."

His plating was past where the chassis kept a number for it. Earth sat on him, all of it, three times what he was built for, and the heat had nowhere to go. A medic laid foil over his shoulders and it crackled and smoked a little at one corner.

At the far end of the arm, where the grapple held the core, there was an edge.

It was holding the line. It held it as he would: braced a hair off break, reading the tension a breath ahead, steady, sure, his. He knew its shape. It was the shape he made. For a moment there were two of him at the bottom of the arm, and the one that wasn't hot was holding it better.

Around it the warmth was waiting, and it was glad of him.

We have it, it said, in no voice, or in his. You can stop. Go home.

It was true. The line would hold without him. He could take his hand off the arm and the arm would not know.

If it asks you to come home, Kaelin had said, on the line from a dark flat with the sea in the lobby, it isn't me.

He kept his hand on the arm.

"Ninety-nine," he said. "Ninety-nine. Ninety-seven."

The reel ran out. At the bottom of its swing the grapple opened, and the core went on alone, slower now, falling short, out over the brown water off the old port, and the arm sprang back up into the sky whole and shaking, and Majuro climbed a little on what was left of its spin.

"Twelve twenty-five," said Moss. "Core's in the sea. São Paulo. Clear." And then, as if she couldn't believe she was allowed to say it: "Billings is standing."

The floor cheered.

Joaquín, with his eyes still shut and his hands still on the burns, said, "Mío. Twelve twenty-five. I'm rich," and laughed out loud in the chair.

Aur'k turned the key back a quarter, and lifted out, and lay down.

He had not meant to. His legs decided it. He was on the boards by the rail with the foil over him and the medic's reader beeping, and the young medic put her palm on his arm without thinking and snatched it back, and he heard her say a word in Spanish that Joaquín would have been proud of.

When his panel would show him the last minutes, it showed them in order. The release. The cheer. The key. And between the key and the boards, forty-three seconds that held nothing.

He checked them twice. Forty-three.

· · ·

Elena's second photograph came at 13:40, while he sat on the floor by the rail with his back to the glass, because standing had been tried.

The same roof. The same milky sky, with nothing in it. The mats were down, and two children were asleep on them in the shade of the dish. The woman with grey hair was serving rice to a man with solder burns on his hands, who was laughing at a child off the frame.

Nothing, Elena had written. Mãe has seen me looking at the sky for an hour and brought me rice. No rain. Everyone is here. The vac leaves at ten. I will be at the field. E.

He had never met any of them.

Then he opened the shift log and read the catch, flat, every line.

12:11:58. READER. Majuro. Hold the cut. Pay the arm out from twelve twenty.

12:11:59. READER. Majuro. Hold the cut. Pay the arm out from twelve twenty.

His own record had him at the head of the row at 12:11:59, saying it, in his own voice, the numbers as words. At 12:11:58 his record had him reading tension and saying nothing at all.

The first line was not his.

The same words, the same twelve twenty spoken as he spoke it. It had seen the arm hold. It had chosen to save the water. It had chosen a second before he did.

In February he had written timestamp error beside a line like this, and put his name to it. He did not write anything now. He closed the log and sat with his hands open on his knees until he could stand, and stood in two stages, as Kaelin did, and went down the glass to her door.

· · ·

Rachel was at her desk with her cuff pushed back and a pencil in her hand. She put the pencil down when she saw him.

"You kept yourself in," she said.

"On my key."

"Against the rule."

"Against yours." He stood in the doorway with his feet set wide, because her room had nowhere else. "You wrote the relief."

"I did."

"I've seen your hand. It's very clear." He let that sit. "It flew the catch a second before I called it. The same words. I've read the log."

She didn't ask which log. She didn't look surprised. She had not looked surprised in March either.

"Billings is standing," she said.

"Yes."

"I'd have dropped it."

"I know. It's on paper."

The fan on the camp bed turned its face across them and away. "Stay," she said. "The floor needs a reader. There's a hundred and ten days left."

"The floor has one. It reads a second ahead of me." He had not known he was going to say yes until the word was there. "I'm going. The vac tonight. The ship on the sixteenth."

"I bought two days of you."

"You got them. And a reader who was relieved, and declined." He took the key off its chain and laid it on her desk, and squared it to the edge with two fingers. "Nobody else carries one. Nobody else should."

She looked at it and didn't touch it. Then she looked up at him, all of her attention, all front.

"Then ask it," she said. "Ask it for me too."

"For me first," he said, and went.

· · ·

The flat smelled of wet plaster. Two fans borrowed from downstairs had run since Thursday, and had dried the lobby and very little else. The salt stood on the table. Dae-jung's board leaned against it, a nut moved one square since morning.

Kaelin was in a vest on the sofa with the stick across his knees and the physio's rubber ball in one hand. He had watched it on the city's bulletin. Aur'k could tell from the bulletin, still up on the wall: SÃO PAULO DEBRIS EVENT. NO DEATHS REPORTED.

"You're late," Kaelin said. "And you're hot. I can feel you from here."

"I lay down on the floor."

"On purpose?"

"No."

Kaelin squeezed the ball once. Then again. "Thought so."

He took his tool case out from under the counter, where it had been sealed since March, and set it on the table beside the salt, and broke the seal. Inside, everything was where he had left it on Mars. A Cobalt Basin seam brush. Selma's spare clamp. A pencil with a wire spine.

"Tonight," Kaelin said.

"The vac at ten. The ship on the sixteenth." He laid the brush in the lid. "It chose before I did, Kaelin. On the catch. One second. The same words."

Kaelin didn't say anything for a while. He counted. Aur'k watched Kaelin's lips do it, the small numbers, as he always did when there was a thing in front of him too big to lift.

"And Billings?" Kaelin said.

"Standing."

"Then it chose right."

"Yes. It would have been right without me."

Kaelin nodded slowly. He looked at the board, and the salt, and the case, and then at Aur'k, and kept looking.

"Come with me," Aur'k said.

He had been planning to say nothing else since the boards.

"Mars would be kind to your hips," he said. "A third of this. You could throw the stick in a crater. Dae-jung would play you in person. He cheats in person. You'd enjoy it."

Kaelin laughed, once, through his nose. Then he stopped.

"Joaquín's in chair four," he said. "I've read Elena's page. I know what that floor does to them after they go home. Four of them, every day." He turned the ball in his fingers. "And it's in there with them, being you. Forty-one calls. Doing them right."

"Kaelin."

"You go and ask it what it is. You should. You've got to." He put the ball down on the table beside the salt, carefully, so he'd know where it was. "But one of us stays where it can hear us."

He pressed his fist to his chest. Sabi. Aur'k answered it, fist to plate, and Kaelin shook his hand out afterwards and didn't say why.

"Pole," Kaelin said. It was Ines's word, and he knew it.

"I'll come back."

"I know you will. Which one?" He tried to make it a joke and nearly did. Then he got up on the stick and stood in front of Aur'k with one hand on the table.

"Someone has to watch the Gate."
