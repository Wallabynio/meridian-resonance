The Fall

Fractured Horizons · III. The cut

30

3 JulyRachelNew York, the Battery; Joaquín's districtMars delay 4.7 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 4.7 min

"Item nine," said the lieutenant. "00:59. The civic director was asked once more, for the record, to open the door, and answered that it was under review."

She read it standing, hands behind her back, in the same dark high collar she had worn on go-live night, in a room at twenty-nine degrees that smelled of hot dust off the vents. Nobody else was standing. The council's inquiry had the long table where the safety review used to sit. The cooling was on its July ration. The chair had taken his jacket off at item three.

Rachel hadn't. She sat at the end of the table with her hands in her lap. Her own night came back to her in a voice that added nothing.

Nine items. Nine minutes. Her name at the end of every one.

"Thank you." The chair nodded to the lieutenant. He was a heavy man from the Polder with a paper fan he kept forgetting he was holding. "The Mandate's complaint stands, and Director Wáng asks that it stay open pending our findings. Director Iverson. Anything to add to the lieutenant's account?"

"It's accurate."

"All nine items."

"All nine."

She had a week of this, in pencil on the inside of her wrist where the tide times went. The council today. The Signatories' panel on Tuesday. The Charter's own people on Thursday, for Bellamy's file. One more week, and then the season would be the only thing anybody asked her about. She could do one more week.

The councillor from the outer boroughs leaned forward. She had voted aye in February because Iverson was on it, and she had sat through forty years of rooms like this one without once raising her voice.

"One question," she said. "Did Elena Vázquez tell you, before one o'clock, what she was going to do?"

Behind the councillor, through the flood glass, the harbour hung flat and green and warm. A jellyfish pulsed against the pane and drifted off.

Nobody had logged the call. Pump house nine's box showed on no board in the building. Elena had left Rachel's name off every address on the files and would never say it now, not to this room, not to anyone. Rachel could say no and walk out at eleven.

"Yes," Rachel said. "At 23:56 on the thirteenth. She called me on pump house nine's maintenance channel. Unlogged. She said she could cut the Earth end, and she asked me to look away."

The chair stopped fanning.

"And did you?"

"No. I held the door." She looked at the lieutenant, who was looking at the wall. "You look away with your hands in your lap. I don't want anybody reading this later to think I sat still."

"Did she tell you what a cut would do to the people on Haven's wall?"

"No."

"Would you have held it if she had?"

"I don't know."

It was the truest thing she had said all week, and she watched the clerk type it.

The councillor sat back. "Then let the record show," she said, "that the civic director has volunteered a call this inquiry had no means of finding, against her own interest. And that this city—" She stopped, and started again, plainer. "Thank you, Rachel."

Somebody at the back clapped and stopped, embarrassed. The chair let it go.

Rachel looked at the clock over the glass. 11:10. The funeral was at noon, out in the Shallows.

"Are we done with me?"

· · ·

The park at the end of Joaquín's street had been a marsh, then a car park, then nothing, and now it was forty metres of plane trees on ground the city had carried in by barge. At noon it was thirty-four degrees under them. The shade was the only reason anyone could stand there.

Everyone stood there in white.

Rachel had found a white shirt in the Battery's lost property, a man's, too big in the shoulders, and she stood at the back with the sleeves rolled. In front of her two hundred people from the fill and the boats and the pump crews stood shoulder to shoulder in white, fanning themselves with the order of service.

Joaquín was at the front with his aunt on his arm. He had no coat on. She had never seen him at a funeral without his coat.

The bed under the biggest tree was dug and ready, dark soil turned back in two banks. It smelled of wet earth and the city's compost, sweet and rotten, stronger than the people. Rosa Medina lay in it in a white shroud, small for someone Joaquín had always called tall.

She had come home on Haven's cargo slot, the one Rachel had forced back in April, three days down in a cold box between the water filters and a crate of seed. Mara had sent a note with her, folded into the shroud, and nobody had read it out.

A woman from the parish spoke the words in Spanish and then in English. Then the family went forward and put the soil in by hand, and after them anyone who wanted to. Most did. The line went past Rachel slowly, people wiping their palms on their whites and going home to cooling centres and night shifts.

She didn't go forward. She had come to stand at the back and leave.

Joaquín's aunt came to her instead. A small woman in her sixties, white blouse, black stockings in the heat because some things were not negotiable, Puebla in every vowel.

"You're Iverson."

"Yes, señora."

"Mara Ekeberg rang me from the Moon. At one minute to one you told them all, hands off the wall." The aunt held up her own hand, palm out, as if the wall were there. "Everybody's hands. They all took them off."

"Señora—"

"My Rosa didn't. She had the cook. His face was on it." She lowered the hand. "She held his head."

"Señora, I was downstairs at the Battery. I held a door shut so the line could be cut. If I'd opened it, the line stays up, and she—"

"Stays on the wall all night? With her hand on it?" The aunt looked at her with Joaquín's eyes. "No. She chose. That's hers. You don't get to have it."

Rachel had nothing to say to that, and nobody to say it to but the woman in front of her.

The aunt took Rachel's hand in both of hers. Her grip was hard and dry and she held on a long time.

"Thank you for her friends," she said.

Then she went back to the bed, and knelt, and put her bare hands into the soil.

Joaquín walked Rachel to the pod at the gate. Neither of them said anything until they got there.

"She wanted the sea," he said. "Rosa. She wanted to go in off the wall at Mariners'. My aunt said the sea's had enough of this family." He looked back at the trees. "She'll be a plane tree. She'll hate it. She'll drop leaves on everybody."

"Ay, prima," he said to the trees, and smiled. That was how she knew to get in the pod.

· · ·

At 20:40 he came up onto the floor still in his white shirt, with the grease pencil.

The July pool ran down the flood glass under the bad hurricane, his cinnamon bun with an eye, and the names and days in a column under it. The night lead's 1, crossed out by the sea. REYES 9. IVERSON 14, in Rachel's square hand, from a morning in June when she had still been hoping a forecast was wrong.

Joaquín wrote ROSA 8 at the bottom.

"Pool's closed," said the night lead, not unkindly. "Storm's got a name."

"It closes when the gauge moves." He capped the pencil. "She always took the early date. If she wins, the flan goes to my aunt."

Nobody argued. After a minute the night lead came over and wrote her initials under Rosa's, as a witness, and went back to her desk.

The storm had a name. Bret, since three that afternoon, three thousand kilometres south-east of the Battery, a hurricane over warm water east of the Leeward Islands, doing what Foresight had said in June it would do. It had come on time. That was the worst thing about it.

At 21:00 the Spire called.

It was the night engineer from the fourteenth, the young man in Spire black who had kept the Lantern's clock, now keeping Foresight's night desk because there was nobody else. His face filled the corner of her wall, very tired.

"Director. Track update for Bret. The landfall window holds, nine to twelve July. Footprint holds, Hatteras to Cape Cod. Landfall point unresolved."

"Same as June."

"We'll fix it at minus forty-eight hours, as before."

"As before, you had the Lantern."

"We have the satellites and the models." He didn't look away from her. "We're doing it the old way, Director. It's slower."

Behind him the Spire's anomaly floor ran back in the dark, the amber boards ranked down the far wall by insured value, a dozen desks lit. At the foot of the boards one desk was not. Its chair was pushed in square. Its surface was bare, wiped, with a yellow tag on one corner from compliance, and along its edge, where a threshold line had once stood up white, the strip was dead.

Her slate chimed.

Re-entry advisory. Format rejected. Director's review.

The ring dropped a bolt or a panel twice a month, and the desk filed the advisories. The ones it couldn't read came to her. Usually it was a dropped digit.

Rejected: event date outside advisory range (ninety days).

"Director?"

"Go on," Rachel said. "I'm listening." She muted him, and opened it.

Source: ring navigation relay NAV 14. Received in nine parts, 18:12 to 18:50.

Nine parts, four point seven minutes apart. On the wall the Mars clock read 4.7 min. It had read that all day.

She pulled the relay's record. NAV 14. Navigation and timing. Commissioned 2081. Calibration set: CB SURVEY 79. She had read CB on fifty reports since January. Cobalt Basin. The first survey's data, sixteen years old, the rock. On 14 June the Charter and the Mandate between them had switched off every relay with Mars in its paperwork. NAV 14's paperwork said navigation.

She opened the advisory.

RE-ENTRY. ORBITAL RING. CASCADE. ONSET: 2 AUGUST 2096.

It wasn't an advisory. It was a warning.

Under it the ring came apart in a table. Segment by segment, the stations and the power satellites and the tethers, each with where it would come down. Most of them came down in the sea. The rest came down on coasts, and beside each coast a city, and beside each city a figure.

Recife. Lagos. Chennai. Manila. Santos and São Paulo. New York.

She found her own city's line and read its number. Then she started adding them, because adding was what she did with numbers. At three million she stopped, because the table ran on for pages and she hadn't reached the bottom of the first.

The last page wasn't a table. It was a drawing.

A narrow channel. Four operators at a time, twenty-minute shifts, a hard limit on each, a reader set at the edge to watch them and pull anyone who went too deep. Monitoring on every stage, drawn in as carefully as the channel. Somebody had thought hard about how to keep people safe inside it.

She had. Four keepers, twenty minutes, Mara on the lever. She had written it in pencil with a carbon in April. Here it was back in clean lines, with her own limits on it.

It wanted a lattice. One sealed run, a single trough, calibrated live against Cobalt Basin's rock, and the dimensions were written down the side.

She had read those dimensions on her own floor at a quarter to one on the fourteenth, in pieces, off the cooperative roofs. A long trough of dark water beside a couch. The Spire line, under the Charter's evidence seal now, waiting for Bellamy's trial.

In the corner of every page, as on every packet since January, the frame carried its own epoch.

14 JUN 2095 04:59:58Z.

She did it twice. Two seconds before one o'clock, here.

It had written this knowing the minute Elena's cut would come. It had kept it nineteen days. It had sent it this evening, the day Bret got a name, the week her own name was in every inquiry room in the city.

On the wall the engineer was still talking, muted, and behind him the bare desk sat under the amber boards with its chair pushed in.

She could send it to Wáng. Wáng would read it whole, a finger at the edge of every page. He would count the dead on it as arguments and have the vault open by morning, and his complaint against her would close itself. She could send it to the council that had minuted her at eleven, and by Monday every coastal city in the Continuity would have a year to riot in. She could send it to that desk.

There was nobody at that desk.

"Drown it," she said, to nobody, under her breath.

She opened the bottom drawer. The old handheld was still in it, the one she kept for blackouts, unlinked for years, charged on the first of every month out of habit. She cabled it to the slate. The advisory went across in nine parts. She unplugged it and put it face down in her jacket.

Then she unmuted the engineer.

"Thank you," she said. "Minus forty-eight, then."

"Director." He hesitated. "Do you want it sent to you, or to the floor?"

"To the floor. Everything to the floor."

The call closed. The Spire went off her wall, and the desk with it.

The advisory sat in her queue. Format rejected. Three choices under it. She had pressed the bottom one two hundred times since she took this floor. A dropped digit. A corrupt frame. A station's clock run wild.

Discard malformed advisory? Payload will not be retained.

In February she had stood on this floor with the rain still on her and said nothing in secret. Not from the review, not from the city. Bellamy had said of course before she finished.

She pressed it.

The Battery took a second it didn't need.

Discarded. Iverson.

· · ·

At eleven the floor was down to the night shift and the hum.

Rachel stood at the gallery rail. Below her the map wall held the whole city, every district the grid could see lit in its colour, the Polder bright inside its wall, the Spire's needle, the saves map's green, the Shallows going grey at the Line, the Rockaways the dark the maps had always drawn them.

The night lead had laid the harbour radar over it and pulled the scale out to the whole western Atlantic, and Bret sat in the lower right corner like a white thumbprint, turning.

Its cone opened north toward them. Hatteras at one edge, the Cape at the other. The city sat in the middle of it, under it, lit to the wall.

Six days of warm water between it and her, to the window's first morning. Thirty-one people on the floor tonight, all of whom she knew by name.

She took the pencil off its string and did the other sum on the back of the day's agenda. The rest of this year: a hundred and eighty-one days. Next year was a leap year. January to the second of August: two hundred and fifteen.

Three hundred and ninety-six.

She ran it again, and it stayed. She folded the agenda and put it in her jacket with the handheld.

Joaquín came up the gallery stairs one at a time tonight, in his white shirt, with two cups of the machine's coffee. He gave her one and leaned on the rail beside her and looked at Bret turning over the sea.

"Spire had anything?"

"Same as June."

"And the queue? I saw you in it." He drank. "Anything?"

Rachel watched the white thumbprint turn.

"Noise," she said.
