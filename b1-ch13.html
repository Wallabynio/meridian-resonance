Fractured Horizons · Chapter 13

Ask me next time

Fractured Horizons · II-A. The bridge

13

18 MayElenaNew York and Luna HavenMars delay 4.3 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE POLDER · THE COMPACT — LUNA HAVEN, THE ROWS / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.3 min

Daniel had the appeal open on the cracked handheld, and his thumb was resting on the line where his name would go.

Elena stood by the door with her bag. The shuttle left the field at ten. Two days in the review's room had ended at five, with the fourth of June minuted as go-live. She had said nothing in them about Haven. Nobody had asked.

"They ruled," he said, without looking up. "This afternoon. Contract withdrawn from the twentieth. Forty-eight hours to appeal."

"Can I see?"

He turned it toward her. He never tilted it away.

Continuity network office. Ruling: repair contract withdrawn, Vila Verde mesh cooperative, effective 20 May. Grounds: unregistered route, 16 January, 00:49 to 01:12, from the cooperative's New York node. Appeal admissible within 48 hours, with attestation.

Under it, the attestation, waiting for a thumb.

The appellant attests that no member of the cooperative, or of a member's household, opened or used the route.

The flat was cool, the canopy over the street outside still holding the day's heat off the glass, and down at the corner the misting heads hissed on for the evening crowd. Elena read the line twice.

The handheld chimed in his hand. He looked at the name and groaned.

"My mother. She's heard. The whole hall's heard. Somebody's cousin works the network office's switchboard, God help us." He answered it. "Mãe. It's an appeal, not a funeral."

His mother's face came up small and sideways, lit by a ceiling light in a room Elena knew from a hundred calls: the cooperative hall at nine at night, Vila Verde time, after the heat. She was talking before the picture settled. Elena's Spanish carried her through most of the Portuguese and dropped her at every joke.

"She says the reservoir's gone under the line," Daniel said. "Monday. Two days in three, from tomorrow." His mother turned the handheld round and held it up to the hall wall, and there it was: the water rota, new, in thick marker, the columns tighter than the ones under it. It had been taped up crooked. Beside it hung the mesh map, every rooftop node a painted circle. "She did the rota herself. She says don't tell her it's crooked."

"It's crooked," said Elena.

"I know." He leaned toward the screen. "Está lindo, mãe."

His mother said a sentence that made him laugh and cover his eyes.

"She says the pump on the hall roof was going to be fixed out of the contract money, and now it's going to be fixed out of my wages. That is to say it isn't. So I'd better come home and fix it with my hands." He took his hand off his eyes. "She says you're too thin. She says that to the television. Don't take it personally."

His mother came close to the camera and said a sentence straight at Elena, slowly, as if the distance were a matter of volume.

"She says, does the review feed you on the Moon."

"Tell her it does."

"She won't believe me." He told her anyway. His mother looked at Elena a moment longer, the face of a woman who had been an organiser through bad years and read faces for a living, and then she said goodnight, and the hall went dark in his hand.

He put the handheld down by the fruit bowl and opened the node's log beside the ruling.

"Twenty-three minutes," he said. "The box says it did it. I pulled every joint on that board in April. I've had it apart twice since. Somewhere I left a path open, and I can't find it, and they're right that it was there." He rubbed a solder burn on his thumb with the other thumb. "So. My fault. Not the box's. I built it."

"The board's fine."

"It's not fine. It did a thing I didn't build it to do."

He picked up the handheld again and found the attestation. No member of the cooperative, or of a member's household. His thumb went to the line.

"Don't sign that."

He looked up, straight at her.

"Why not?"

There was a version she could have given. Counsel. Let the review's lawyers read it first. It was ready in her mouth, well made. She heard herself not give it.

"Because it isn't true," she said. "The route was mine."

The misting heads hissed off at the corner. Somewhere below, a pod opened and closed.

"When?"

"The sixteenth of January. Ten to one. You were asleep."

"From my box."

"From your box. Through the roof node." Her hands had found each other. She left them. "The Spire was going to re-seed a baseline at two in the morning. One night of data was going to stop existing. I needed one copy where the Spire couldn't see it, and your mesh was the one network in the city it couldn't."

"Which night?"

"Cobalt Basin."

He was still for a moment. "The two who died."

"Yes."

"The raw."

"Yes."

He set the handheld face down on the table, carefully, as if it might spill.

"You didn't ask me."

"If I'd asked you, you'd have been in it." She could hear the reasoning coming out, clause by clause, a method explained at dinner. "You'd have known. Anyone who asked you later, you'd have had to lie to or tell. This way you weren't in it."

"They took the contract, Elena. I'm in it." He said it without any heat. "My mother's in it. The hall pump's in it."

She had nothing that answered that, so she said nothing.

He looked at her for a long time. Then he asked the other thing, the question under the question.

"Is that all of it?"

"That's the route," she said.

It was true. She watched him hear what it didn't say. She watched him decide to let her keep it, and the deciding showed at the corner of his mouth.

She had not said door. She had not said that it was still there, in his box on the roof, as she had built it four years ago. He hadn't asked, and she let the question he hadn't asked go by.

"I'll go to compliance," she said. "Tomorrow. I'll tell them it was me."

"And then what? They give the contract back to the town whose engineer's partner went into the Spire through his roof?" He shook his head. "They wanted it anyway. Somebody with a logo. You'd just be handing them a better reason. And they'd take you off the Moon."

He picked the handheld up again. He didn't open the attestation. He wrote to the hall instead, short, his thumb fast on the glass, and she read it upside down because he did not tilt it away.

No appeal. Pumps on the rota. I'll come down in June and do the roof myself.

He sent it. Two days in three, and the hall pump on his hands, and the contract gone in a ruling and a line.

Then he got up and came round the table and stood in front of her with his burnt hands open at his sides.

"Ask me next time," he said.

"You'd have said no."

"I'd have said yes." His voice cracked on it, and he laughed at that, briefly, because he always laughed when something gave. "That's what I'm telling you. I'd have said yes, and held the torch, and complained about your soldering. Next time, ask me."

"I will."

"Say it properly."

"I'll ask you."

He nodded, as if writing it down. He kissed her forehead, and then he took her bag off her shoulder and carried it to the door himself, because the shuttle left at ten.

She had promised him in April that she would sleep. She went down to the pod with the new promise in her mouth, and for the length of the ride to the field it felt as good as that one had.

· · ·

She overshot the first long step out of the lock, a day of shuttle and a week of Earth in her legs, and caught the rail.

The slate at the first junction said ninety-eight. Someone had written it smaller than the hundred that used to be there, as if apologising.

Ravi Castellanos was waiting under it with a cloth bag held in both hands. He saw her and lifted it like a man showing a catch.

"For the architect," he said, his Talk coming to her a fraction behind his lips. "Asha says you read everything, so you can read these. Oyster ones. I kept them wet." He opened the bag to show her: pale fans, damp, smelling of a cellar after rain. "Asha has all her words back, you know. Every one. She's unbearable. She used intermittent at breakfast to annoy me."

"Thank you."

"You fry them in almost nothing. The trick is—"

He stopped.

His head turned a little toward the wall. His eyes went to the middle distance, where nothing was, and stayed.

Elena counted. Nine. Twelve. A cart came round the curve and went by them, and he didn't move for it. Fifteen. Mara Ekeberg had come up behind him, and she laid her palm flat on his back, between the shoulders, and kept it there. Eighteen. Nineteen.

"—the dark," said Ravi, from somewhere further along, and smiled at Elena, and put the bag in her hands. "Keep them in the dark. They like it." He patted Mara's hand on his back without looking round, and went off down the Tubes toward the Ward, slowly, with one hand trailing along the wall.

Mara watched him go.

"Seventy-two hours," she said. "That's what your page says."

"Page twelve."

"It's been twenty-six days."

· · ·

The session room was cooler than the Tubes, and dimmer, the couch lit from inside at its lowest. The Charter's drum was still clamped to the wall at its head, dark, its cable running out under the door to the mast. Mara stood by the lever. She did not put her hand on it.

"You came back up," she said. "I thought the review might keep you."

"The review sat. Go-live is the fourth of June."

"I heard."

"After the fourth this wall is part of a live system." Elena kept her voice for the review room, level and helpful. "Nobody knows what a session on a live wall is. I'd like to know what this one is before it's that. Bounded. Your scope, your lever. Ten minutes."

"No."

"The observer reads every report from this lattice and signs what it means. I've signed—"

"You've signed one." The Haven pause went out and came back. "No."

Elena set Ravi's bag down on the end of the couch. Her hands wanted each other, and she put them flat on her thighs.

"My note on the twenty-second," she said. "I can reopen it. Ravi's onset, the length of his stops, the seventy-two hours. Finding: review to sit. I can file it tonight."

Mara looked at her. It went on long enough that Elena heard the walls breathe.

"And I give you the couch," said Mara.

"I didn't say that."

"You said it in that order." Mara's palm came off the wall. "You'd sell me my own people's report. If it's true tonight, it was true on the twenty-second, and it was true at eleven sixteen when you watched Asha lose her word and kept your hands still. Write it because it's true or don't write it. Don't bring it to me in a bag with the mushrooms."

Elena didn't answer. There was nothing in the protocol that answered that, and she had read all of it. The note sat closed at the edge of her eye, and she left it closed.

"I asked you why in April," said Mara. "You told me so you'd know what you were signing. You've signed. I don't give my lattice to a reason somebody made for the occasion. Why?"

Elena looked at the couch, along the length where four keepers had lain in April. Nothing showed there now.

She had never said it. Not to Daniel, not to the review, not in the margin of a log. She had put it in a packet to Mars as a question and let it stand there, unsaid, a question being a thing she could withdraw.

"My sister died on a lattice line," she said. "Soria. In 2089. Under the Spire. She was a nurse, and she volunteered, and I was one of the people who built the line." Her voice was all right. She noted that it was all right. "I think my sister is in there."

It was out in the room now. She could not take it back into the packet.

Her knees went. She sank, slowly enough that she could have stopped it and didn't, onto the end of the couch beside the bag of mushrooms. The couch was warm.

Mara didn't move toward her. She stood by the lever and let the pause run, longer than any Elena had heard from her.

"How?" said Mara.

"I don't know." It was the first time she had said that in a room with someone else in it. "That's what the ten minutes are for."

Mara let that sit. "Do you want her to be?"

"Yes."

"Then you're the worst person I could send." Mara took a breath of her own counted air. "Four of mine went in for a test. Ravi's still going in, a little at a time, every day, and nobody's going in after what he hears. Somebody should go in for a reason."

She looked at Elena's face, the one she hadn't trusted, and seemed to look at it differently. "Not on no sleep. You're grey to the lips. Seven nights in a row, in a bed, lying down. The Ward medic signs every morning. Miss one, and the count starts again."

"The twenty-fifth, then."

"If you manage it. Nobody does, the first week."

Mara took a stub of grease pencil from the chest pocket of her suit. It was black, worn down to the length of a finger, the paper peeled back in a spiral. She went to the wall beside the lever, at the height a person lying on the couch would see without lifting her head, and wrote on the living wall in square capitals.

The honey light ran on under the letters and did not take them in. The wax sat on top, black, where both of them could see it.

E. VÁZQUEZ. ONE SESSION. TEN MINUTES. EXIT AT TEN, OR AT THE KEEPER'S WORD. THE LEVER IS THE KEEPER'S. IF PULLED, IT IS OVER. NOT REPEATED. M. EKEBERG, KEEPER AND WITNESS.

Higher up the wall, now that Elena looked, were others. Ghosts of them, rubbed out with the heel of a hand and still there in the grain: a date, a name, TWENTY, a word she couldn't read. Somebody's grandmother's scopes.

"Slates forget," said Mara. "Logs get edited. This stays where you can see it from the couch, and it says what you said." She held out the pencil. "Sign it."

Elena stood. Her legs were all right now. She took the pencil, warm from Mara's pocket, and read the five lines through once, as she read everything, to the end.

Ten minutes. She had asked for ten minutes in April, at this couch, with her hand on it.

She signed under Mara's name, small and plain. Then she looked at the lever, and measured, before she could stop herself, how far it stood from the couch.
