The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

03

19 FebruaryKaelinthe Reef Coast, QueenslandMars delay 16.2 min

THE COMPACT — THE REEF COAST, CARDWELL / Kaelin Drake — Mars 16.2 min

The road up the headland had the evacuation route painted on it, yellow arrows a stride apart, and Kaelin counted them because his legs wanted an argument, and the hill wasn't enough of one.

Thirty-nine. Forty. At forty-one the arrows bent away round the shoulder of the hill toward the shelter, where they were meant to go, and he left them and took the track up through the wet grass with the clinic's stick in his right hand.

Three months Earthside, and his body still hadn't forgiven him. Nine years at a third of this weight had taken bone off his hips and given none of it back, and the physio in New York said another year, and said it kindly.

The rain didn't help. It was the height of cyclone season, and the cyclones came further south every year. The rain came in off the sea in warm grey sheets and sat on his shoulders, and every step up the slope was a step up a ladder with somebody standing on the rung above.

He stopped twice. He told himself it was to look at the view.

From up here he could see all of it. The town strung along the shore on its stumps, tin roofs shining. The surge wall grey in front of them, the mangroves thick at its foot, and across the channel Hinchinbrook going up into cloud.

The beach under the wall was white coral, dead before he was born, broken and rolled and thrown up by every storm since, so that when the sea pulled back the whole shore rattled.

He could hear it from the top, and smell it: salt, and the mangrove mud at the wall's foot, rotten-sweet. A long hiss, and under it the rubble muttering.

The memorial ran along the spine of the headland in nine panels of dark stone, set low so the wind would go over them. No statue. Nobody had wanted a statue.

Each panel held its blocks of names, and each block had a heading cut into it: a group, and the route the group had taken. GROUP 2 — COAST ROAD NORTH. GROUP 5 — THE SCHOOL. Under every block, cut smaller, a line saying how many went and how many came home.

He hadn't meant to read the lines. He read them.

Group 1, forty-four went, forty-one home. Group 2, sixty went, fifty-two. Group 3. Group 4. He read each block's line as he came to it, under his breath, as he'd once read a manifest at a hatch, and the rain ran off the stone into the grass at his feet.

GROUP 7 — BRUCE HIGHWAY, CREEK CROSSING.

Twenty-three names. Fourth from the bottom: RHYS DRAKE, 12.

Under them, 23 went. None home.

It was the only line on the headland that came out even.

He put his hand on the stone beside his brother's name. It was warm. Everything here was warm, even in the rain, even the stone, and it had been warm that night too.

The route desk had been a trestle under the shelter's generator lights, with rain on the roof so loud the warden had to put her mouth to his ear. He was sixteen and the clipboard was his, because he'd done every Sunday drill since he was six and done them right. Seven groups. Seven columns. Eleven lines a column, each one a box.

"Seven's loading," the warden shouted. "Crossing?"

On the wall, the route board showed every creek on that stretch of coast in green or red, fed by gauges up each one. The creek crossing on the highway was green. It had been green all night.

The line on the clipboard said Creek crossing — confirmed clear by crossing warden, channel 4. You called the man standing in the water. You didn't take it off the board. They'd told him that every Sunday for ten years.

Channel four had been jammed for ten minutes with a man shouting about a car in a drain. Rhys was on the highway side, at a mate's place, because Kaelin had said he could go. Group 7's bus was loading in the rain with its engine running, and twenty-three people were waiting on one box.

He looked at the board. Green.

He ticked it.

The bus pulled out with its wipers going and its lights on, and he watched it to the corner, and wrote down the time in the margin, neat, because the warden liked them neat. The gauges up the creek read the creek. Nobody had put a gauge on the sea coming up the other way.

Twenty-one years. He had looked at everything since.

All but one thing. Every afternoon in New York, Aur'k went down under the Battery into a room Kaelin had never been let into, and came home at night and said it had been a good day, and Kaelin had no way to go and look.

"Hey, mate," he said to the stone. His voice came out rough, and he cleared it. "Heavy here. You'd laugh."

Behind him, on its pole above the headland, the siren began to wind up.

His body went before he did. He was half-turned toward the arrows, stick planted, counting the town below him out of its houses, before his head caught up and told him it was Sunday, and ten o'clock, and a test.

He stood in the rain and let it climb, and fall, and climb, and fall.

· · ·

By the time he got down to the shelter the town had already raced it.

The shelter sat back from the wall on the high side of the highway: a long concrete shed with its own panels on the roof, its own tank, its own diesel humming behind a cage, the doors rolled up for the breeze. Painted beside the main door was a board with the season's times in chalk. Somebody had just rubbed out the last one and written 7:52 and drawn a crown on it.

Under the times, smaller, somebody had chalked 19.2.75, as somebody did every year, and nobody had rubbed that out.

Kids were everywhere, wet to the skin and pleased about it. A boy of six came in off the arrows at a dead run, slapped the door with both hands, and was told by half the shelter that he'd missed it by a mile.

The smoke from the barbecue went out the vents in the roof and the shelter smelled of it, fish skin and lime and diesel. Under it, in the open bay where the generator was, his mother stood at the hotplate with a pair of tongs and a towel over her shoulder, turning barramundi.

She looked at him, and at the stick, and at his wet hair, and didn't say any of what she saw.

"Was it raining up there?"

"Yes."

"It always is." She lifted a fillet, looked at its underside, put it back. "Drown it, Kaelin, sit down before you fall down. You're the colour of a sheet."

He sat on an upturned crate by the tank. It was a relief so total it was almost funny.

A girl of about eight came and stood in front of him with her arms folded. Behind her stood three more, in order of height.

"You're the Mars one," she said.

"That's right."

"Mrs Drake says you can jump over a car."

"Over a rover. On Mars."

"Go on, then."

"This isn't Mars."

"Go on."

His mother was not looking at him in a way that meant she was. He got the stick under him, stood, set his feet, bent his knees, and jumped as hard as he had ever jumped off anything.

He left the ground. He was nearly sure of it. He came down a hand's width from where he'd started, and both knees told him about it at length.

The children considered this.

"That's rubbish," said the girl.

"It's heavier here."

"It's not heavier. It's just normal." She looked at the others for agreement and got it. "You're just old."

"I'm thirty-seven."

"Yeah," she said, kindly, and they ran off to tell someone.

His mother put a plate in his hands. Fish, a wedge of lime, bread gone soft in the damp. "Eat that. You've got no meat on you." She went back to the hotplate. "Does your fella eat?"

"He doesn't."

"Bring him anyway. He can turn the fish."

"He'd want to know why you turn it at forty seconds and not thirty."

"Because thirty's wrong." She pointed the tongs at the wall behind him. "Tell him that."

He turned on the crate.

The wall by the first-aid cupboard was covered in pictures. Photographs of a slate in a galley a long way off, one a month, eight years of them pinned in rows, the corners curling in the damp. Columns of bets in coloured marker. Dust storms, sunrises, the Ice-Works' output. Funmi's tall column in green, longer than anyone's by a hand, and after January it didn't go on.

There was a gap in the rows where May and June should have been.

"You missed two," she said behind him.

"I was busy."

"I know where you were." She turned a fillet. "Everyone here knows where you were. Eat your fish."

· · ·

The beach was empty after the drill. The rain had thinned to a drizzle that was mostly warm air with water in it, and he went down the steps in the surge wall one at a time and sat on the rubble where it was driest.

It wasn't dry anywhere. It didn't matter. The coral shifted under him and settled, and when the sea came up and drew back the whole beach talked, a long dry rattle like a box of bones being sorted.

He held the handheld up and called the flat.

It was ten at night in New York, Saturday. Aur'k answered on the second chime. His helm filled the screen, rust-red, smooth, lit from one side by the kitchen. Behind him on the table a printout of a scratched grid leaned against the salt.

"You're on a beach," Aur'k said.

"I'm on a beach."

"It's Sunday there. You're in tomorrow." The helm tilted a degree. "How is it?"

"Wet."

"Then it's the same as today." He waited. When Kaelin didn't fill it, he went still. "You went up."

"I went up."

Aur'k said nothing for a while, and it was the right thing to say. Out on the channel a ferry was crossing to the island, very slowly.

"I want to ask you about the Gate."

"Ask."

"I don't like it." He'd thought it for three months, and it came out flat and simple, like saying the weather. "That's not a question. I'm just telling you."

"I know."

"How?"

"You ask me whether it was a good day," Aur'k said. "And then you don't ask the rest. You do it with your face. You've done it every night since November."

Kaelin laughed despite himself, once, and the rubble rattled under him.

"I got told a thing, down there," he said. "Before they put me out. The voice your world is listening to is ours." He watched the helm. "I don't know what you listen to in that room, mate. I know what I was told. And you go in every afternoon."

Aur'k went still. It lasted long enough that Kaelin saw the kitchen light change on his plating.

"So tell me what it is."

"I can't tell you what it's for." Aur'k said it at once, with no softening on it, as he gave a reading. "That's the contract. I signed it. I can tell you how it's built, because that's the part I'm for."

He waited until Kaelin nodded.

"It's narrow. Four chairs. Short shifts, with a clock over every one. A reader at the head of the row. Me. Nobody goes deep. If they start to, I take them out, and I don't ask anyone first. There is no coupling to the Preserve. Nothing of ours touches the rock."

"Nothing."

"Nothing."

Kaelin looked at the water. A gull came down onto the rubble, found nothing, and walked off as if it had meant to.

There was a question under the question, and he had carried it since June, since longer than that. Since the night in April when Aur'k had said a name at the rock before anything spoke. Did you know? Before I came. What was down there, and what was next to it. It sat behind his teeth. He could feel the shape of it.

He thought about channel four.

You didn't take it off the board. You called the man standing in the water, and you believed what he told you, because he was the one who'd gone and looked. That was the whole of the rule. He had broken it once and spent twenty-one years keeping it.

Aur'k was the one standing in the water. Every afternoon, at the head of the row, with four people's edges in his hands.

"Okay," Kaelin said.

"Okay what?"

"Okay. I'll stop asking."

The helm didn't move. "You don't have to."

"I know. I'm going to." He breathed out. "You'd tell me if it was going wrong."

The helm didn't move. "I'd tell you before it went wrong. That's what I'm for."

"Then that's it. Sabi." He put his fist to his chest, where the screen could see it.

Aur'k put his own to his chest plate, and it made a small hollow knock in the kitchen in New York.

"Hold the handheld out," Aur'k said.

"What for?"

"I want to hear it."

Kaelin held it out at arm's length over the rubble. The sea came up the beach and went back, and the coral rattled and hissed and settled, and the drizzle ticked on the screen.

"That's Dae-jung's tin," said Aur'k when he brought it back. "When he shakes it to find the washer he wants. He says it helps him think."

"He's cheating."

"He's always cheating. I'm losing on purpose. It's a strategy. Go and get dry. You'll stiffen."

"Yes, Mum."

"Your mother," said Aur'k, "would have you dry by now."

· · ·

The house was on the old block behind the cane, on stumps higher than a man, and at dusk the verandah was the only cool place in it.

His mother had gone to bed early, as she did on this day every year, and said it was the heat. The rain had stopped. Out past the cane the sky had gone copper and then rust and was taking its time about red, the sprayers' sunset, too long and too pretty, and along the surge wall the lights came on one at a time.

In the drains between the cane rows the frogs started up.

He had his feet on the rail and the stick against the post and his eyes nearly shut when the handheld chimed. Compact routing. Luna Haven. M. Ekeberg.

Mara Ekeberg. Haven's lattice keeper. He had never spoken to her. He had sent her a post once, in June, on his last share, and she'd never answered it, and he had understood that.

Drake. We haven't met. You sent me a post in June and I played it to someone who wouldn't hear it, and then I lost eleven. So I'll ask you plain, since you were plain with me.

The Signatories keep a register for the licence-holders. Keepers read it. There are four licensed lattices off Mars. Three are on the Moon, and I know every keeper on them. The fourth is the Spire line, sealed in 2089 with a director's records. In November it came off seal. Status: in service. Authority: emergency, civic director, New York. Nobody asked the Commons. Nobody told the keepers.

Your boundary reader went to work for Iverson in November. The whole Compact knows where its best reader went. What is it breathing for?

M.E.

He read it twice. Then he sat up and put his feet on the boards.

He knew the Spire line. He had read Elena Vázquez's files on the cot by Petra's bunk the night they came, all of them, while Ines turned the ward. Bellamy's line, calibrated live against Cobalt Basin's rock. The rock he had put his own head against. A nurse kept on it six hours after she died.

Civic director, New York. Iverson. Under the Battery.

Four chairs. A reader at the head of the row. Nothing of ours touches the rock.

It could all be true. He turned that over and it stayed true. The line was in New York and the rock was on Mars and maybe nothing ran between them now but a band anyone could listen to. Aur'k didn't say what he couldn't back.

He also hadn't said this.

The frogs were loud now, hundreds of them, shouting in the drains. Rhys used to say they were singing for him. Kaelin opened the thread to the flat. The cursor sat under Aur'k's last line, Go and get dry, and his thumb typed before he'd decided to.

What's the Gate's lattice?

He looked at it for a long time.

He had said he would stop asking. He had said it on a beach with his fist on his chest, and Aur'k had answered him.

He took it out a letter at a time, from the question mark back, and when the line was empty he set the handheld face down on the boards, and sat in his brother's town in the dark, and didn't count anything at all.
