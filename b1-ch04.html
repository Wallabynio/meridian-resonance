THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY AND THE ROCKAWAYS / Rachel Iverson — Mars 11.1 min

The warning landed at 07:50, while Joaquín was losing an argument about the sea.

"Two ninety," he said. He had the grease pencil and the floor's attention, and he wrote it on the flood glass under his name, over the grey-green water pressing on the other side. "Two metres ninety at the Battery gauge, tonight's high water. I feel it in my knees."

"Your knees are sixty-one," said the night lead. "Two seventy."

"My knees were in the Evacuations. They have seniority."

The pool ran down the glass in grease pencil, eleven names and eleven heights, kept every storm season since before Rachel ran this floor. Nobody used the lace for it. The glass made people argue, and arguing kept a night shift awake.

Rachel took the pencil from him. Her reflection stood in the glass over the water, platinum hair cut blunt at the jaw, pale eyes, the dark suit she wore like a uniform, and she wrote IVERSON 3.05 at the bottom.

"That's not a bet," Joaquín said. "That's a threat."

"It's February. The sea's a metre up on the pumps and nobody's told the pumps." She capped the pencil. Behind her, the map wall changed colour.

It didn't flash. Nothing in the Battery flashed. One district on the wall's south-east edge went from grey to red, address by address, filled in with a fine brush, and the floor said, "Foresight advisory. Director's authority. Priority one."

"Read it."

"Pump cascade, Jamaica Bay barrier. First station trips at 18:41. Barrier pumps fail in sequence. Full failure at 18:55. Bay rises behind the barrier to meet the surge. Flood footprint attached. Clearance plan attached."

Eleven hours and five minutes. She was already walking.

The map wall ran the length of the floor, four metres high, the bay drawn on it in blue. She stood under Broad Channel, the stilt district in the middle of the water, and watched the warning fill it. Fourteen hundred and twelve residents. Every address red. Every name on a list down the side, and the streets in order, north to south, with the boardwalks that would go first marked first. Someone had done her morning's work for her and done it well.

The footprint spread out from the barrier like ink in a glass. Over Broad Channel. Up the north shore. Across the bay to the long strip of the peninsula, and over its bay side, house by house.

At the edge of the grid, it stopped.

A straight line on the sand. Past it the Rockaways sat grey and dry. No addresses, no names, no water.

"That's wrong," Rachel said.

Joaquín leaned on the gallery rail beside her, coat still on, as it always was, a camel coat in a building kept at nineteen degrees. "Good news," he said. "The bay's agreed to stop at the Line. Very civic of it."

"And the bad news."

"My cousin's wife's brother lives out there. Bay side. Fixes boat motors. Won't tell the city his name." He looked at the grey. "About three thousand people won't."

"Desk," Rachel said. "Crews to the Rockaways once Broad Channel's clear."

"The Rockaways are outside the service area," said the Charter. "No registered residents. Crew cover ends at the Line. Request declined. Handled."

Handled. She looked at the straight line on the sand.

"Iverson, Rachel. Director, civic emergency," she said. "I'm sending crews past the Line when Broad Channel's clear. On my name. My liability. Log it, and copy it to the director's office, and copy it to anyone who asks."

The desk took a second it didn't need.

"Logged. Liability assigned: Iverson."

"They'll have your job for that," said Joaquín.

"They can have it at nineteen hundred." She was already moving down the gallery stairs, fast, as she moved everywhere outside a meeting. "Get the van."

· · ·

The sirens in Broad Channel didn't make a sound.

Rachel stood at the landing in the rain with IVERSON across the back of her crew jacket and watched them work. The district stood on its stilts over the brown water, boardwalks between the houses, laundry already taken in, and at eleven in the morning every person on the boardwalk lifted their head at once, as if someone behind them had called. Then they went inside for their bags.

Each of them was hearing their own name. Leave now. Your boat is at the north landing. Rachel, linked and nobody's resident, heard none of it. She watched it go down a street like wind across a field.

The water taxis came in under their shade roofs, one behind another. Her crews counted on. Twelve hundred by one o'clock. Thirteen hundred and eighty by two.

At the corner of the last boardwalk a boy of eight or so was crouched over a bucket, pulling up a crab line. The street around him lifted its head and went. He didn't. No lace until twelve. Nobody had said his name.

Rachel started toward him. His mother got there first, back down the boardwalk at a run, and took him by the hood and the bucket in one motion, and he went with her protesting about the crabs.

"Broad Channel clear," said the floor at 14:40. "Fourteen hundred and twelve of fourteen hundred and twelve."

"Good. Crews to the van."

"Released crews reassigned: standby, barrier pump line. Handled."

"Drowned bloody desk," she said, to nobody. She looked at her crews on the landing. Wet, steaming, twenty-six of them, every one of them good. The desk was not wrong about the pumps. Somebody had to stand at the barrier when it went.

"Pump crews hold," she said, loud enough for all of them. "Anyone else who wants to come past the Line with me, the van's at the bridge. Nobody's ordered. Nobody's covered."

Nine came.

· · ·

From out here the city looked like what it was.

Its towers stood up out of the haze to the north-west, lit at four in the afternoon, and under them, thin as a pencil line across the water, the wall: a long back turned on the sea. Rachel loved that wall. She knew every pump house on it by number and most of the crews by their kids' names. She had never seen it from the wrong side.

The Rockaways ran east along the sand, a few hundred metres wide: salvaged houses, a boat hull turned over for a roof, solar panels on driftwood frames, wrecks half sunk in the dunes. On the ocean side the Atlantic came in grey and hard and broke white. On the bay side the water was already up to the first pilings.

Joaquín drove with the window down and the loudspeaker on the roof. "The bay is coming up behind you at seven tonight. Not the sea. The bay. Get your kids, your meds, your animals. Buses at the west end." Then in Spanish, the same thing, warmer. Then, not into the microphone, "Madre mía, they're looking at us like we're selling insurance."

They were. People came out onto porches and stood there.

Rachel got out at the third street and walked up to the nearest of them, a man in a fishing smock with a handheld on a cord around his neck.

"You need to go," she said. "Barrier pumps fail at 18:55. The bay comes over the back of this street by seven."

He looked past her, at the city. "Your sirens never said my name."

"No," she said. "They didn't."

"So why are you here?"

The rain came sideways off the bay. Behind her the van's loudspeaker went on down the street in two languages.

"Because our map stops at the Line," she said. "I didn't."

The man didn't move. A woman did. She came down off the next porch in an oilskin, in her forties, broad-shouldered, a handheld on a cord like his and a sheaf of paper in a plastic sleeve held against her chest.

"Miami Kerrigan," she said. "I keep the list. Who are you?"

"Rachel Iverson. Civic emergency."

"I know who you are." Miami looked at her steadily. "What do you want from us?"

"Everyone off this strip before the pumps go."

"We heard the forecast. Ocean surge. We're built for the ocean side."

"The bay. From behind. You won't see it coming because you're watching the wrong water."

"And how does the Battery know that, when the Battery doesn't know we're here?"

It was a fair question. Rachel didn't have a fair answer. She had a warning out of the Spire that had been right once, at a cost of two, and a footprint that had stopped at the Line as if the water could read.

"It's right," she said. "I've seen it be right."

Miami held her eyes a second longer. Then she slid the paper out of the sleeve and turned it round.

It was the Rockaways, by hand. Every house on every street, numbered in pencil, one to nine hundred and sixty. Beside each number, a count of people. Beside some, a note: oxygen, twins, won't open to Charter.

"Three thousand and forty-six," Miami said. "Paper. The only map out here nobody can read but us."

Rachel looked at it and felt the day go into a shape she knew.

"Split it," she said. "You take the dune end, I take the bay end. We tick them as they go."

Miami peeled a carbon off the back of the list and handed it over without a word. Paper people always kept a carbon.

The first door on the bay end had won't open to Charter beside it. Rachel knocked. Nothing. She knocked again, and a man's voice said through the wood, "We don't buy."

"Kerrigan sent me," Rachel said. "Number four-twelve, four people, one of them on a stick. The bay's coming in the back at seven."

The door opened on a chain. An old man looked at her crew jacket, at her name on it, and at the carbon in her hand with his house number ticked in someone else's pencil.

"Miami gave you that?"

"She did."

He took the chain off.

After that they went door to door. Miami's name opened doors that Rachel's didn't. The crews carried the oxygen. The twins went in a cargo trike under a tarp. The buses filled and went west along the road and came back empty. At 17:20 it was dark, and they worked by the van's lights and the lights of the houses, and the bay came up the pilings one rung at a time.

At 18:05 Joaquín came back from the west end, wet to the thigh.

"The road's going under at the dip," he said. He leaned on the van's open door and smiled. That was how she knew. "Ten minutes and the van's a boat. Twenty and it's a submarine."

"Eighteen thirty."

"Rachel."

"Eighteen thirty. Not before."

At the dune end, the count had stopped moving.

Miami met her at the foot of the dune with her half of the list. Above them, on the highest ground on the strip, stood a long timber hall on piles, lit from inside, raised on fill the town had carried up in buckets for ten years. People were going up into it, not out.

"A thousand and thirty-two," Miami said. "They're staying. So am I."

"The bay is going to come over this strip."

"Not over the hall. We built it for worse than tonight, and we built it ourselves." Miami's jaw was set. "You don't have an order out here that means anything. You know that."

Rachel knew it. She had known it since the desk said Handled. She looked up at the hall, at a thousand and thirty-two people she could count and couldn't move, and every part of her that had ever made a call alone wanted to make one now.

"Drown it," she said under her breath. Then she unclipped the crew handset from her jacket and put it in Miami's hand.

"Every hour. On the hour. You miss one, I come back."

"Through what?"

"I'll find a boat."

Miami looked at the handset, then at her, and her face eased by a few degrees. She clipped it to her oilskin. "On the hour," she said.

At 18:30 the van went through the dip with the water over its wheels, Joaquín swearing softly in two languages, the last trike behind them. On the bridge Rachel had him pull over. Below them the barrier lights ran across the mouth of the bay in a line.

At 18:41 the first of them went out. Then the next. Then the next.

At 18:55 the line was dark.

They watched the bay come up through Broad Channel. It came up through the boardwalks and the stilts and into the empty houses, fourteen hundred and twelve of them, and the district's lights went under street by street, north to south, in the order on the list.

In the back of the van a child was singing. Somebody's grandmother told it to stop, and somebody else told the grandmother to let it sing. Rachel had the paper on her knee and was ticking in the last numbers Miami had called down the handset. Both halves together, out and safe: two thousand and fourteen. She ran it twice.

She laughed. Joaquín turned round in his seat and stared at her.

"She laughs," he told the van.

· · ·

Bellamy arrived at 23:40, dry.

The crews were still steaming at the back of the floor in their jackets, eating noodles out of cartons. He came through them in his black high-collared coat dry from the collar down, said a word here and there, and took the place at the front with his back to the screens.

Rachel stood still at the side of the room and watched who watched him.

The screens held the city council, the Charter's board, and Beijing Command, where it was noon and Wáng Sù sat at a table with a glass of water and his hands folded. Above them all the saves map glowed. Broad Channel, green: fourteen hundred and twelve. Out past the Line the Rockaways were not on it.

The handset on her desk had crackled at 23:00. Hall's dry. Count's a thousand and thirty-two. Somebody brought a fiddle. She had the paper folded in her pocket.

"Twice now," Bellamy said. "In January, forty people at Cobalt Basin. Tonight, Broad Channel. Two warnings, both right to the minute, both from a source we don't control and can't question, arriving on our own relays from Mars with no return address."

On the council screen, two people turned to each other. Rachel saw it. Nobody on the floor had said Mars aloud before tonight.

Bellamy's voice stayed low. "We have been very lucky, and we have been passengers. I'd like us to stop being passengers."

He laid out the Lantern in five minutes. A relay at Cobalt Basin larger than anything built, wide enough to talk back. Costed, staffed, scheduled. The survey team was already there. Forty people alive because someone listened, he said, and we'd like to ask them to build us a better ear.

It was a good pitch. It was too ready. Twenty-five days since the first warning, and he had a schedule. Rachel had sat on Charter projects that took a year to agree a site. She noticed it, and called it competence, because tonight competence had a fiddle playing in a hall on a dune.

"Talk back to what?" said a councillor from the outer boroughs. "What source?"

"To whoever has been right twice," Bellamy said. "That's what the Lantern is for."

Not a lie. Not an answer. She filed it.

The council did what councils did. A review. A committee. Six months, a year. Treaty implications. The Compact would object on principle, and the principle had a veto.

"Beijing Command," said the chair.

Wáng Sù waited until the room was finished. Then he spoke in the Mandate's formal register, every sentence complete.

"In 2053 my mother's city was moved four hundred kilometres inland in nineteen days. A committee plan existed. It required nineteen months. My mother is alive, and I am alive, because a central system was faster than a committee. I was thirteen. I remember who was on the trucks, and I remember the estimates for those who would not have been." He folded his hands again. "I do not ask the council to trust a signal from Mars. I ask it to count tonight's dead, and to tell me the number. The Mandate supports the Lantern."

Nobody had to. The number was zero.

"We'll need a safety review the city trusts," Bellamy said. He was looking at her now. "Someone who'll tell us no and mean it."

The whole floor turned to look at her.

Rachel didn't move. Still in meetings, fast everywhere else. She had been told it like a criticism once, and had kept it like a medal.

In 2071 she had stood in a school gym with a list and a dead radio, and waited for a procedure to tell her who went out first. It came thirty-one hours late. By then she had chosen, and nine people on the list had died waiting, for her or for the procedure. She had never been able to say which.

The folded paper sat in her pocket, three thousand and forty-six names in someone else's pencil.

"Full access," she said. "Every packet, raw. A hard stop written in, that the review can pull without asking anyone. The review counts everyone the warnings touch, addressed or not. And nothing in secret. Not from the review, not from the city."

"Of course," said Bellamy.

He said it before she'd finished the list.

"Then yes. Iverson. On the record."

"If Iverson's on it," said the councillor from the outer boroughs, slowly, "then the Shallows will listen." She voted aye. The chair voted aye. The votes came in under Rachel's name, one after another, on the screen beside the saves map, and she watched each one land and knew what she had given them.

· · ·

At five past one the Walk was empty, and the wind had dropped.

Below her the pump house on this stretch of wall was still running, a steady hum through the granite under her boots. The tide was going out. The water at the foot of the wall had left a line of weed and foam on the stone, a little higher than her three-oh-five, a little lower than she'd feared.

She called Elena.

Elena answered on the first chime. Her face came up in the corner of Rachel's eye, hair pinned back tight, a kitchen behind her, the light in it dim.

"You're up," Rachel said.

"So are you."

"Bellamy's building a relay at Cobalt Basin. The Lantern. I'm on the safety review." She watched the water go down the stone. "I need someone on it who'll read the method and not the press release."

Elena was silent. Rachel waited for the objection. In twelve years Elena had never once said yes before the second question, and Rachel had relied on that more than she had ever told her.

"Yes," said Elena.

The pump hummed under Rachel's feet.

"Will the review see the raw captures?" Elena said. "All of them. Every packet."

"All of it. It's a condition. I'll put it in writing."

"Then yes."

Her hands were clasped in front of her, one over the other. Rachel saw it and didn't ask. It was one in the morning, and Elena had been right about the method in every room they had ever shared, and two thousand and fourteen people were asleep in cooling centres inside the wall.

"Thank you," Rachel said.

"Rachel." Elena's eyes went to one side, and came back. "Well done. Tonight."

The call closed.

Rachel opened the review roster. Her name sat at the top of it, alone. She put Elena's under it, and sent it.
