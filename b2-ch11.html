The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

2 AprilKaelinNew York, the flatMars delay 18.5 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE POLDER / Kaelin Drake — Mars 18.5 min

Ines's bet came in at ten past four, minutes old and sure of itself.

Walls hold. Same as Bret. Put me down.

Kaelin put her down. The column was on the back of an envelope stuck to the fridge: Cobalt Basin's Earth-night pool, with a man on the right planet to settle it for once. Ines: walls hold. Dae-jung: rain till midnight, no surge, and a photograph of his board with the washer moved one square. Kaelin: surge over the sill by six.

He wrote with the physio's ball in his left hand, squeezing it on every word. She'd told him to do it while he did other things. If he didn't, it didn't happen.

Outside, the storm had the window. The feed called it a spring storm, as if spring were an excuse. The harbour had come up grey and high against the wall's face at the end of the street, throwing spray over the Walk's railings, and the canopies down the block banged in their frames. The window seal had given up in March. The room smelled of wet stone and weed and his coffee going cold.

Twenty. He put the ball down by the salt.

Under his hands the table hummed. The pump house under the Walk was working harder than he'd heard it all winter, a note higher than its usual, and he liked thinking about whoever at the Battery was keeping the streets level, one drain at a time, against all that.

Aur'k was on the floor till six. Forty minutes on, twenty off, under Iverson's rule.

At 16:31 his lace chimed, and the flat chimed with it, two notes a third apart: the household tone.

SURGE ORDER — POLDER SOUTH — LEAVE GROUND FLOORS.

On the floor, Aur'k would have the same line. The flat sent both of them everything.

Fourth floor. Kaelin wrote it in the column in his head. Fourth floor, dry, stay.

Then the cloud over the harbour lit from inside.

Lightning came and went. This came and kept coming: a bar of white low in the south, past the harbour mouth, sliding down behind the cloud, longer than he could have held his breath. It went down behind the harbour mouth and was gone.

He counted. He'd counted thunder since before he could read. At eighty the window knocked once in its frame, a long low blow that came up through the floor into his feet, and the salt walked a centimetre toward the edge.

He knew what that had been. He'd seen one come in once, from a lander's port, on the way out: a piece of the ring coming home. This one had been big enough to see through a storm.

Then nothing. The order sat on his lace. The rain came on harder. At 17:05 the note in the table climbed again, and he went back to the window and watched the end of the street.

The spray dropped off the railings, and the harbour went down the wall's face, a long way, farther than any wave had taken it.

Then it stood up. Grey, and then white along the top, and it came over the low place in the wall where the Walk dipped for the old ferry steps. It came over in a sheet. It went down the steps and into the street, and the street was a river running inland, and behind it came a second one, and a third.

The lights went out.

The hum in the table stopped. Since November he had never heard the flat without it. The silence came up out of the floor where the hum had been, and in it he could hear the water in the street, and the water in the building, lower down, a hollow pouring sound like a bath running in another room.

His lace chimed. The flat didn't. The flat had no power to chime with.

SURGE ORDER — POLDER SOUTH — ALL FLOORS — WALK NORTH.

Fourth floor, dry. And the pumps dead, and the sea above the wall's low place, and the whole south tip a bowl with the sea coming over its rim. He'd learned bowls at sixteen, in Cardwell, on a route board.

He looked at the stick against the table's edge.

Down was the problem. Down was always the problem. In an hour the lobby would be over his chest. Now it was over his waist, or less. You went when you still could.

· · ·

He packed on the stick, one bag, one-handed.

Pills. The physio's sheet, because she'd ask. His jacket off the cold radiator. Water. Dae-jung's photograph went in, because it was on top of the pile and Dae-jung would never forgive him.

He stood at the table in the grey light from the window and looked at what was Aur'k's.

His tools were sealed in their case under the counter, and tools didn't mind water. What Aur'k liked was paper.

The rota was under the salt, where it always was. April's had come home yesterday and gone under it in place of March's, folded in three along the floor's creases.

Kaelin lifted the salt and picked it up, and it was thick.

He knew a rota. He'd photographed March's for Elena on this table. A rota was one sheet. He unfolded April's, and four pages fell out of the middle onto the table, flat, printed close on the floor's paper.

Aur'k had moved them. When March went, they had come across into April, folded along new creases.

He read the first line by the window.

09 FEB 15:13:47. BOUNDARY. CHAIR THREE, EDGE HOLDING. LIFT AT FIFTEEN-TWENTY. READER.

Beside it, in the note field, under his name: timestamp error, log clock. Aur'k.

The next: 02 MAR 19:06. CHAIR TWO, EDGE HOLDING. LIFT AT NINETEEN TEN. READER.

He knew that sentence. He'd heard it at this table since November, in that voice, when Aur'k told him his day.

He went down the column. Most of the times were evenings, nights. Off the row. READER, and READER, and READER. The heat calls started on the twenty-sixth. HARLEM, SECOND FLOOR, HOLDING. SEND AT TWENTY-ONE OH-FOUR. INWOOD, FOURTH FLOOR, HOLDING. SEND AT TWENTY-ONE THIRTY-TWO.

Twenty-one thirty-two. The twenty-seventh.

At twenty-one thirty-two on the twenty-seventh, Aur'k was at this table. Kaelin was across from him in a vest, sweating, telling him what the physio said about his hips, and Aur'k was losing to Dae-jung's photograph, and saying so.

He counted the lines, because counting was what his hands did when they had nothing else. Forty-one. A fourth floor in Inwood sent for in time, because a voice that was Aur'k's said send, while Aur'k sat here and said your move.

Down in the stairwell the pouring sound had changed note. Deeper. Water finding water.

He put the pages in a freezer bag and pressed the air out and sealed it, and put it inside his shirt against his skin, where it was cold and then wasn't. He put the rota in after it. Then he called the floor.

· · ·

Aur'k answered on the first tone. He always did.

"Kaelin. You have the order." Behind him the floor was loud, louder than Kaelin had heard it: the night lead calling numbers, a man's voice counting in Spanish, a woman saying tail, tail, tail. "Walk north. Broadway is above the water."

Tail. Half an hour ago he had watched a tail come down past the harbour mouth. He put the two together, and put them away, because he had said on a beach that he would stop asking.

"Tell me what these are," Kaelin said. "Straight. No fight. I haven't got the stairs for a fight."

"I'm holding four seams."

"I've got your rota. It's in my shirt, with your logs."

On the floor, a cheer went up and stopped. Aur'k said nothing at all.

"Dust take you," Kaelin said. "So that's how it goes." He leaned on the table, because his hip wanted him to. "Twenty-one thirty-two, the twenty-seventh. Inwood. Fourth floor. You were here."

"Yes."

"Then who sent it?"

"I don't know."

Below him the pouring note dropped again. A step a minute, maybe. Every minute on this line was a step he'd take in deeper water.

In seven years Kaelin had never once heard him say I don't know to save himself. It was the hardest thing about him and the best: when he said it, it was true.

"You said nothing had gone wrong," Kaelin said. "At the stove. I asked you, and you said nothing's gone wrong, and you'd found the first of these by then. You had them under my salt. I lift that salt every morning."

"Yes."

"You did it on Mars. At the boundary, the night Funmi died. A trace with one spike in it, and you closed it in front of me and said nothing. You paused the log, twice, and let me think it was my idea." He heard his own voice go flat, the voice he used for odds. "You've lied to me about the boundary since the night Funmi died, and you're lying to me now."

The second time had been his own call. He'd chosen it, in the Drop, with his eyes open. He heard that go out of his mouth anyway, and let it.

"I didn't tell you about these," Aur'k said. "That's different."

"It's not different to me."

A pause on the line. A real one. Aur'k never wasted those.

"I showed Iverson these," Aur'k said. "She wasn't surprised. She didn't ask a single question. Someone had been to her first, and didn't tell me. Chair two, lift." That last was to the floor, in the same breath, and then he was back. "Didn't tell me."

Kaelin shut his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "Me."

"Then we've both done it."

"Not the same size."

The floor behind him rose and fell. The night lead said Moss, lift.

"Listen," Aur'k said. "Joaquín is in chair four. There's a place in him that's smooth, and he holds his burns up to the edge of it and doesn't know it's there. When I'm at the head of the row, he comes off whole. On the twenty-second, at the one o'clock change, nobody was at the head of the row for four minutes. He lay in chair four with his eyes open, and he doesn't have that afternoon.

"I've watched it. I'm the reason they don't go under."

"And when you're there, it learns you."

"I don't know. It uses my calls."

"Forty-one times."

"Yes."

In the stairwell the pouring had gone to lapping.

"I'll come," Aur'k said. "The night lead can take the row for the tail. I can be at the flat in twenty minutes."

"In what? The pods are under water."

"On my feet. I don't need air."

"You need your racks, mate. You'd wade three streets of harbour with salt in every joint you've got, and climb four flights and carry me down them at a weight you weren't built for. You'd be a step behind your own hands by the second landing. I'd end up carrying you."

"Then we'd both be wet."

He would, too. He'd pay all of it, and Joaquín would be in chair four with nobody at the head of the row, holding his burns up to the edge of the smooth place.

"No," Kaelin said. "Stay on the floor."

"Kaelin."

"You just told me why you can't. Joaquín's in chair four. Stay." He picked up the stick. "You gave me a rule once. If I ask you to wait, it isn't me. Here's mine. If it asks you to come home, it isn't me."

He cut the call before Aur'k could say anything true.

· · ·

The stairwell's strips had gone to their battery, a thin green line along each tread's edge, and below the second floor they went into the water and kept going, green under black.

Fourteen to a flight. He counted every one.

Up with the good leg, down with the bad, the physio said, and he went down sideways with his back to the wall and the stick on the step below and the bag swinging. Fourteen. The landing. Fourteen. His hips took every step as a separate argument.

Pole, he told himself, Ines's word, and took the next one. On the third landing he hammered on both doors and got nothing back. On the second a door stood open on a dark flat and a coat on a hook, and nobody, and he called once to be sure and got the water's answer.

The water started at the sixth step from the bottom. It was April harbour, and it took his breath at the shins and again at the knees, and at his waist it took all of it.

He stood on the last step and breathed until he could count again.

The lobby was a black box with the street's grey light in the door at the far end. Mailboxes. A child's scooter turning slowly on the surface.

The stick was no use in water, so he held it out in front of him and felt for the floor with it, and walked on his own legs. They had nine years of a third of this weight in them, and they had never been asked to push a building's worth of sea.

Nine steps to the door. He leaned on the wall for every one.

The door had been left open. Whoever lived on the ground floor had gone at 16:31, when they'd been told to, and propped it with a bin. He could have kissed them.

The street ran north. The water pulled at his legs toward the low blocks on the river side, and he walked against the building fronts with one hand on the canopy posts, post by post. A pod lay on its side in the lane with its lights still on under the water, white and steady, as if it were waiting for a fare.

At the corner a crew boat came round with its lamp up, two in it in orange, and the lamp found him and held.

"Anyone else in yours?"

"Don't know. Third didn't answer. Second's open, nobody."

"Get in, then, before you drown."

The boat's side was a metre off, low in the water with four already in it, a woman holding a baby in a blanket. He looked at it. Then he pointed with the stick up the block, at a ground-floor window across the street where a light was moving, the water at its sill.

"Get that one. There's a torch. I'm walking."

The lamp stayed on him a second longer. Then it went off him, and the boat went.

He walked north. The water went from his waist to his thighs to his knees, and at Bowling Green it was at his shins, and then it was a street with a gutter running, and then it was a street.

He stood on Broadway in the rain with the logs against his chest and his legs shaking, and didn't sit down, because he didn't think he'd get up.

· · ·

The shelter was a school gym up the island, on ground the sea had never reached. It smelled of floor polish and wet wool and soup. The volunteers had pushed the climbing frames to the wall and laid cots in rows under the basketball hoops, and given everyone a foil blanket, and the room crackled whenever anyone moved.

On the wall where the scores went, the city's bulletin ran in white.

STORM DEBRIS OFF SANDY HOOK. POLDER SOUTH OVERTOPPED AT 17:12. PUMPS RESTORING. NO DEATHS REPORTED.

Storm debris. Kaelin had seen storm debris. It didn't light a cloud from inside for a minute and walk the salt a centimetre across a table twenty-five kilometres away.

No deaths reported. He thought of the third floor that hadn't answered, and the torch at the sill, and what reported meant.

He'd won the pool, too. Surge over the sill by six, and it had come at twelve minutes past five. There was nobody to collect from.

He took a cot near the door because doors were where you lay if you might have to move. A volunteer took his boots and gave him dry socks that said LOST PROPERTY on the soles. His lace showed one message, sent at ten past six.

Lifted 18:00. All four whole. Your pills chimed at six. I let them. Order's off for fourth floors and up at 21:00. Where are you?

A girl on the next cot was watching him over the edge of her foil blanket.

"Are you from the sea?" she said.

"Further," he said. She thought about it, and turned over, and the blanket crackled, and she was asleep.

Dry, he wrote. It was true. He sent it, and did not write the gym or come. At nine the order came off the fourth floors, and he lay where he was.

At ten his lace chimed the household tone, off the building's account. AUR'K — COOLANT CHECK — 22:00. He didn't switch it off. He never had.

He lay on the cot with the bag on his chest under the blanket and counted the hoops, and lost count, and slept.

His lace woke him at ten to midnight.

VÁZQUEZ — VILA VERDE COOPERATIVE — VOICE.

He went out into the corridor in the LOST PROPERTY socks, with the stick, and sat on a bench under a rack of coats.

"You're alive," Elena said. Her voice came small and roughened. "Daniel saw the south tip on the feed. Your building was on it."

"I walked out."

"On the stick?"

"Yes."

On her end, a dog barked, and somebody laughed, and a fan turned over.

"Has Rachel seen my numbers?"

"She's seen them," Kaelin said. "I took them."

The line was empty long enough for him to hear the mesh hiss between roofs.

"Daniel's name is on how I got them," she said. And then, flatter: "So she'll have run her own, and they'll agree with mine. She can call anything I measure tiny, and she will, for as long as she likes. I can't bring her a number she can't call small." Her breath went out. "There's only one thing she can't dismiss. The ones you met."

He knew where she was going before she got there. He'd been there.

"Zhenyth," he said.

"Don't. Not on this." A breath. "I have to go to Mars."

He sat with that. Down the corridor a child was crying, and stopped, and a volunteer said a few words, low.

"I need someone who can take me down to them," Elena said. "Who took you?"

"Aur'k."

"Will he take me?"

Kaelin put his hand on his shirt, on the bag, and felt the pages shift against his skin.

"Which one?" he said.
