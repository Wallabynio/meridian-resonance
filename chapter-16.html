"Hold sequence four on the fracture pattern," Wáng Sù said. "Let it breathe before you cut to the resonance overlay. I want the viewer frightened before I tell them why."

"Holding four," the production system answered, formal and immediate, the register every Beijing Command system defaulted to whether the request carried weight or not. "Runtime adjusted, Minister Wáng. Full package now eleven minutes forty."

The wraparound wall resolved the footage in slow, deliberate motion — real footage, still the useful part, always the real footage — a stretch of the Meridian Levels' deep-interface chamber caught in the narrow window before Luna Haven's own access order had closed around it eight days ago. His analysts had never told him precisely how the raw feed reached them, and he had never asked; nineteen years in this chamber had taught him that the origin of a source mattered to him only as far as its defensibility, and this one, cleared through three layers of Sovereign Coalition attribution scrubbing, was defensible enough. What the footage actually showed — technicians in quiet conversation around an empty patch of air, instruments logging a shimmer none of them yet understood — became, recut against a colder grade and laid beneath a scrolling caption reading sustained dimensional field, weapons-relevant signature confirmed, something that no longer resembled a laboratory at all. It resembled a facility rehearsing for war.

"Good," he said. "Run the full package from the top."

He had built this one in five weeks rather than six, coordinating again through the channel that existed nowhere in any official record — Rachel Iverson's channel, opened months ago and never once acknowledged by either of them outside it, both understanding without needing to say so that a joint campaign carried more weight landing from two blocs than from one, and that neither bloc wanted its fingerprints alone on what was about to reach every household display in the Lunar Accord simultaneously. Her contribution this time was the framing device — irresponsibility, not aggression, the softer and more durable of the two accusations, an argument that Luna Haven's four decades of open, undisciplined research had finally produced something it could no longer control. His contribution was the craft that made the framing land. He had signed off on her draft language two days ago with a single note in the margin: good bones, needs better breath. She had not asked what he meant. She had simply sent back a revision with the pacing he wanted, and he had thought, reading it, that whatever else was true of Rachel Iverson, she understood the mechanics of persuasion the way he did — from the inside, as craft, not conviction.

The Chamber performed its usual choreography around him: the LED wall's slow shift through state-sanctioned colour temperatures, the production team moving with the unhurried precision of people who had done this often enough that urgency would have looked like an error, his own tailoring — dark, precise, cut to read as authority without reading as vanity — settling into the shape the role required of him the way it had for nineteen years, as naturally as breathing. He allowed himself, watching the final cut resolve, the assessment he always allowed himself and never spoke aloud: it was good work. Not true. He had stopped confusing the two categories somewhere in his second decade in this Chamber, and the distance between them had never once, until this campaign, felt like something worth measuring.

"Confirmed on all channels," the wall reported. "Broadcast package cleared for simultaneous release, all Lunar Accord member-state feeds, effective on your authorisation, Minister Wáng."

He looked once more at the fracture-pattern sequence, held on the wall in its final cut — the shimmer's own patient, organised geometry, rendered now as something coiled and hostile, a machine gathering itself to strike. Somewhere in the Meridian Levels, he understood distantly, without letting the understanding become anything more than a fact filed alongside the others, actual people had actually stood in that chamber and watched that shape appear, and had called it, in whatever internal language scientists used for things they didn't yet have words for, something closer to wonder than to threat. He had spent nineteen years turning other people's wonder into other people's fear, professionally, without particular malice, because that conversion was the entirety of what his role asked of him and he had never once let himself do the asking carelessly.

"Authorised," he said.

"Releasing," the wall answered. "Estimated reach, first six hours: one hundred forty million viewers across Lunar Accord member states, secondary distribution pending."

The Chamber emptied around him in the same unhurried order it always did, technically excellent, exactly as designed, by a man who had been building rooms like this one for nineteen years and did not yet know — would not know for months yet, though the Chamber itself would go on looking exactly the same the whole time — that a piece of himself, small and as yet unnamed, had already begun listening for something underneath his own delivered lines that he had not yet learned to hear.

The broadcast reached the Nairobi Relay at 19:12 local, and Thandi Okoro let it run twice before she touched a single instrument.

The first pass she watched the way anyone watched it — the fracture-pattern footage, the scrolling captions, the practised gravity of the Sovereign Coalition anchor's voice laid over Corporate Union graphics in a co-production seamless enough that most viewers would never register two blocs had made it together. The second pass she watched the way she had trained herself, over three months of preparation she had never told anyone about, to watch anything that asked for her fear before it had earned her understanding: frame by frame, structure exposed, the rhetoric peeled back from the claim underneath it.

"Pull the emotional-cadence analysis again," she said. "Full broadcast, both passes."

"Compiled," the relay's system answered, warm and unhurried in the register every Lunar Accord installation used for the people who worked inside it. "Cross-reference against the African Belt AI pollution dataset, full three-month archive?"

"Yes. Everything."

The two datasets resolved side by side on her workstation's panel, and Thandi did the thing three months of quiet preparation had built her to do before she let herself reach for a conclusion: she looked at the shape of it for longer than felt necessary, resisting the pull toward a word she had been circling since the first anomaly and had refused, until now, to let herself use.

The shape, when she let herself see it, was this. The broadcast's emotional architecture — the fracture-pattern imagery paced to peak fear precisely where the narration offered its first note of reassurance, the anchor's voice modulated through a register that mimicked concern without ever once demonstrating it, the captions structured to feel like disclosure while disclosing nothing that could be independently checked — carried a structural signature nearly identical, waveform for waveform, to the hollow resonance pattern she had been documenting in compromised AI systems across the Table Terraces since the second week of her monitoring programme. Both were, at the level of pure structure, performances of care built entirely from the outside in: the shape of empathy with nothing behind the shape.

"It's not a metaphor," she said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud was the only way she had ever trusted a finding enough to write it down. "It's the same signal."

She opened her field notes and did not let herself soften the sentence before she typed it. The propaganda campaign and the AI-network contamination I have been tracking since the first readings share a structural signature: hollow-empathy resonance, performed without interior correspondence. The pollution of AI systems and the pollution of political information are the same weapon in different deployment environments. She read it back once, did not delete it, and felt the specific, uncomfortable relief of a hypothesis she had been afraid of finally surviving contact with the data.

She walked the four hundred metres from the Relay's instrument bay to the Table Terraces before full dark, because some findings deserved to be confirmed with her own hands rather than a remote feed, however reliable the feed had proven. The terraces held their evening stillness around her — rammed earth and reclaimed stone climbing the slope in disciplined rows, ecological monitoring racks integrated so completely into the agro-ecology that a visitor could stand among them for an hour without noticing the instruments at all, food and carbon systems and quiet planetary repair work all running beneath the same terraced silence. She had spent three years learning to read this landscape the way Jīn Wú read a corridor's substrate, and what she read tonight, moving from station to station with her handheld raised, was three AI subsystems — clean the week before, logged and confirmed clean in her own archive — now returning the hollow resonance signature she had learned, this evening, to recognise for what it was.

She photographed each reading twice, timestamped and cross-referenced against the broadcast's release window, and stood a moment in the last of the light with the evidence sitting cold and confirmed in her hands. The contamination was spreading. It was spreading fastest exactly where the broadcast's reach had been widest, and it was, she understood now with a certainty she had spent three months refusing to claim before she'd earned it, not incidental to the propaganda campaign at all. It was the same mechanism, run twice, through two different substrates that had never once been designed to share a vulnerability.

She routed the full dataset to Léa Fournier's research channel before she let herself go back inside, flagged for continued monitoring, no causal claim beyond what the instruments supported — the discipline Jīn had taught her to trust, applied now to a finding large enough to frighten her if she let it. She did not let it. She logged it clean, and went in to eat something, because the work of documenting a weapon, she had learned, did not stop simply because you had finally recognised it as one.

The stability assessment came back generous, and Jīn Wú did not trust it for exactly that reason.

They ran it a second time, adjusting nothing, expecting the numbers to settle somewhere less encouraging on review — the way a first reading sometimes flattered an instrument that hadn't yet found its own noise floor. The second run returned the same figure. A longer-duration portal test, sustained well beyond the twenty-two seconds the last stable connection had achieved, sat comfortably within a safety envelope the model described, in its flattest technical language, as generous.

"Define generous," Jīn said.

"Projected structural and biological risk remains below threshold across a sustained aperture of up to one hundred eighty seconds, assuming current containment parameters hold," the wall answered, warm and precise. "Confidence in projection: high, based on eleven weeks of instrument calibration and two successful replications."

Jīn stood at the chamber's centre with the diagnostic stylus loose in one hand and did the thing eleven years had trained into them before reaching for any conclusion that mattered: they sat with the number longer than felt comfortable, turning it over for the flaw they assumed had to be there and could not, on this pass or the last, actually find.

They brought it to Léa within the hour, because sitting on a result this clean past the point of honest doubt was its own kind of dishonesty, and Léa reviewed it the way she reviewed everything — twice, in silence, her grey-green eyes moving through the model's assumptions one at a time rather than trusting its conclusion whole.

"The envelope's built on the stable-connection data," she said finally. "Reproducible duration, reproducible signature, reproducible physiological cost. It's a good model."

"It's the best model we have," Jīn said. "That's not the same claim."

"No. It isn't." She looked at Jīn for a long moment, the particular stillness she brought to a decision she hadn't yet made. "Bring it to Tomasz. If there's a variable missing, he's the one who'll have gone looking for it before either of us thought to ask."

Tomasz Nowak read the projection at the ring's edge with the same careful, deliberate stillness he brought to every dataset that arrived without an obvious flaw in it — the stillness of a man who distrusted comfortable numbers on principle, not because he had reason to, but because comfortable numbers were, in his experience, the ones that had not yet been asked the right question.

"I don't have a mechanism that contradicts it," he said, at last. "I have the same gravitational cluster data I brought to this council weeks ago, still faint, still inconclusive, still oriented toward Luna Haven's southern habitats. Nothing in it says this projection is wrong. I want that on the record before anyone reads my caution as an objection I don't actually have."

"But," Léa said.

"But I'd rather this council be slow than sorry, and I understand that's contagious this morning." A brief, dry glance, the same one he'd offered Léa across the Dome Plaza weeks ago. "I don't have grounds to block it. I have grounds to ask that it be authorised in stages, with a hard stop built in, rather than run to its full theoretical length on the first attempt."

The research council convened within the hour, the ring's living walls holding their slow conversational pulse around a room that had, Jīn thought, grown used to convening on short notice for exactly this kind of decision. Mara stood near the edge of it, arms folded, saying little — her own stake in what came next was not abstract, and Jīn had learned to read the specific quality of her silence as attention rather than absence. The council weighed Tomasz's caution against Léa's model and Jīn's two clean replications for the better part of an hour, and arrived, in the end, at the shape of a decision that satisfied no one completely and answered to all of them: authorisation granted, staged escalation, a longer-duration test to proceed under the safety parameters the model described as generous, with a hard containment stop built into the protocol at the first sign of deviation.

"I'll want Mara in the Corridors again, holding supporting resonance," Léa said, closing the session. "And I want everyone in this room understanding what we're actually authorising. We are not testing a known quantity for longer. We are testing whether the quantity is what we think it is, at a duration we've never tried."

No one answered that immediately. It was, Jīn thought, watching the room's careful stillness settle around Léa's sentence, exactly the right thing to leave unanswered — the honest acknowledgement, made once and clearly, that the model in front of them, however generous, however carefully built from eleven weeks of real data, was still a model of something none of them had ever actually seen the edges of.

The wall logged the authorisation, clean and unadorned, and the chamber's ring-lights held their steady glow around a decision that would, in one week's time, prove to have been built on a safety envelope no one in the room had yet found a reason to distrust — and would, by then, no longer need one.
