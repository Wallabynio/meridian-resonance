Fractured Horizons · III. The cut

24

13 June, nightRachelNew York, the BatteryMars delay 4.2 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 4.2 min

At twenty to nine the cooling on the gallery coughed, caught, and went on, and the whole floor looked up at it as at a sick relative.

"Twenty-four," said the night lead, reading the wall. "Twenty-four and a half."

"It's thirty-one outside." Joaquín had his camel coat over the back of a chair for the first time in twelve years, and his shirt stuck to him in a dark stripe down the spine. "In the harbour it's twenty-eight. The fish are cooler than we are. I want that minuted."

Nobody minuted it. Both shifts were on, and half the day crews had come back in their jackets because nobody wanted to watch this from home, and the Battery was built to hold nineteen degrees for half as many bodies. It smelled of them. Sweat, hot cable, the coffee machine on its tenth cycle, and under everything the pumps, a hum through the soles of Rachel's shoes.

She stood at the flood glass with her back to the harbour. The water pressed green and warm on the other side. The tide knocked once on the bronze seam.

The map wall held the city in its ordinary grey. In its corner sat a clock the Spire had put there that afternoon. THE LANTERN. 20:41.

Bellamy was on the floor in person, on somebody else's floor, at night. He had come at seven with his collar buttoned and taken his place with his back to the screens, and spoken to almost nobody. He had a handheld in one hand, low by his coat. He looked at it more than at the wall.

Beside the clock Wáng Sù sat in daylight. Nine in the morning in Beijing: a white window behind him, the lamp off, the glass of water full at his elbow. He had wished the floor good evening at half past seven. Since then he had said nothing at all.

Joaquín came down the glass with the grease pencil. The July pool was still up, the bun and its eye, and IVERSON 14 under it in her hand. Beside it ran the nightly tide: eleven names, eleven heights for high water at the Battery gauge.

"Last night of the tide pool," he said. "After tonight the thing tells us to the centimetre and I have to find a new way to lose money."

"Two eighty," said the night lead.

"Two seventy-five. My knees."

"Your knees are retired."

Rachel took the pencil and wrote IVERSON 2.82.

"That's not a bet," Joaquín said. "That's—"

"Working." She gave it back.

At 21:00 the clock went from white to green. "The Lantern," said the Spire's voice. "Live." That was all.

The wall did nothing. Someone at the back laughed, too loud, and stopped. Then nobody laughed. The clock ran on in its corner, and the cooling coughed, and a hundred people in a hot room watched a grey city and waited for Mars.

Then the city began to fill in.

They came small and white and everywhere at once, tags rising out of the grey like frost on a window, faster than she could read.

Pump house nine. Impeller two, bearing. Fails 20 June, 14:10.

Red Hook. Cooling feed, Van Brunt clinic. Trips 17 June under load.

Battery gauge. High water tonight, 23:52. 2.81 m.

"Two eighty-one," said the night lead, in a small voice.

Joaquín looked at the glass. Then he took the cloth off the rail and wiped the tide pool away, all eleven names, in one long stroke, and the floor groaned like a crowd at a missed goal.

"You were one out," he told Rachel. "I'm disqualifying you anyway."

The tags kept coming. A transformer under the Walk. A water main in Harlem in nine days. A wet-bulb Thursday a week out, named for its day, with the cooling centres it would fill. An asthma clinic's backup cell. A stair lift. A ferry's port engine. Each with its date and its hour, plain as a parts list.

The crews started calling them out. Then they started taking them. Somebody had the Red Hook clinic on the line before its tag had finished drawing. The night lead put a crew on nine for the twentieth with a bearing in a box, and laughed while she did it, because it was a week away and that was obscene.

Rachel laughed once, out loud, at the wall, and the floor heard her and grinned at each other.

Twenty years on this floor. Every one of them spent finding out afterwards.

Twenty minutes. Thirty. The saves map in its corner turned over in ones and twos now, a woman on a stair lift, a man on dialysis in Inwood, and nobody in the room was watching the number.

The storm stayed where it had sat since the ninth, one smear down the coast in the corner of the wall, Hatteras to the Cape. Landfall point: unresolved. Half an hour of the wide channel, and it hadn't moved.

"It'll come," said Joaquín at her shoulder. "Give it the night."

At the back of the floor Bellamy hadn't moved either. The handheld was up now, close to his chest, and he held it as a man holds a gauge in a storm. His face had held all evening. It wasn't holding now.

· · ·

At 21:38 Luna Haven's line went white on her board and rang.

Nobody rang the Battery on that line. Rachel took it at the glass.

"Iverson."

"Thirty," said Mara Ekeberg.

Joaquín was beside her before the word was out.

"Thirty of mine, at once. Twenty-one thirty-eight. Everyone with a hand on the wall when it came up. A cook in the long galley. Two crop hands asleep in the warm rows, where they always sleep. Two of my keepers." A breath. "They're fading, Iverson. All of them in the same minute. And they're smiling."

"Rosa," Joaquín said. "Rosa Medina. Is she—"

"On the corridor floor with them, turning them. The Ward's been full since the fourth."

"Gracias a Dios," Joaquín said, and then, lower, "Dios mío," and put both hands on the rail.

Behind her voice was a sound Rachel took a second to place. A lot of people breathing, close together, on a hard floor.

"I pulled my lever at the first one," Mara said. "It's a test lever. It isn't on this. The drum's the programme's now." Her voice didn't rise. "You wrote a stop. Who's on it?"

"I'll find out."

"Find out now."

Rachel put her on the wall's edge, a white square with Haven's name, and left the line open. Around her the floor had stopped calling out tags.

While Mara was still breathing on the wall, the Mars queue chimed. A post flagged raw, from Cobalt Basin, and not in the station's grey. A personal address, medical priority. Iverson at the Battery.

She put it on the wall and played it standing, for the floor and for Beijing.

"Iverson. Ines Varga, medical, Cobalt Basin. Kaelin's share is held this month, so you get mine, and it's medical, so nobody gets to sit on it." A woman's voice, quick and dry, with an alarm going behind it that somebody had stuffed a towel into.

"Nine of ours at once just now. Two at the frame, five in the hab, one in the galley with a spoon in his hand, and Hemi Rangi on the rover ramp, who says he's listening to the wind."

The alarm, through its towel.

"I've got Selma and Bao on mats beside Petra and I'm out of floor. Their traces stood up together. Same as the twenty-eighth. Same as Haven, I'd bet, if Haven's talking to you. I'm told it's a power bus. Traces attached. I'm going to go and turn people now. Full air, Iverson."

A pause, as if she were deciding whether to leave it there.

"It isn't."

The traces came up on the wall under the post, nine lines, and every one of them climbed at once. Rachel read the station's stamp and turned it into New York time without thinking, as she turned tide tables.

Cobalt Basin had gone first. Haven had gone after it, later by the length of the light between them, to the second, as the first warnings had once come spaced.

Not weather. Not a bus. Nobody on either rock had pulled anything. Something had come up out of Mars at the frame and travelled the channel, and taken Haven's wall when it got there, and the only thing that ran from one to the other was the thing her floor had been cheering for forty minutes.

Primo, ¿fuimos nosotros?

She looked at the wall, at the white tags still rising, the stair lift and the clinic and the bearing in its box, and knew what was paying for them.

· · ·

"Safeguard one," she said, loud enough for the floor. "Any keeper or crew in the loop who loses a word, it stops. Stop it."

Bellamy lowered the handheld. He came to her, not fast.

"As written, Rachel. In a sweep's loop. A sweep is a calibration pulse. The calibration finished this morning. Tonight isn't a sweep. It's the channel." His voice stayed low, for her alone. "And it tells the review within the hour. The review stood down on the fourth."

She had the slate up already. Her three lines above the names, in the Spire's grey. Any keeper or crew in a sweep's loop. Her words from May. She had read them on the ninth, a comma different, and called it competence.

"Then the stop. The review's hard stop. I asked for it in February, in this room. Pulled without asking anyone." She didn't wait for him. "Desk. Hard-stop channel."

"Hard-stop channel: sealed," said the Battery. "Director's authority."

"Then mine." She found her name on the slate and held her thumb on it until it asked. "Withdraw. Civic director, host city. Iverson."

"Withdrawal logged. Status: live. A stop is a command action. Command codes required: the Charter, New York. The Mandate, Beijing."

"Your name opened it for the city it stands in," Bellamy said. "It was never the lock. The codes live in two cities so that nobody frightened in either can close what both opened, on one bad hour. It's a good rule." He looked at her. "You'd have written it."

"Then use yours."

"No."

"Drown it," she said, under her breath, at nobody.

She turned to the wall. "Director Wáng."

"Director Iverson." He hadn't moved since half past seven. "I have heard Luna Haven, and the medic. Thirty, and nine."

He had counted. Of course he had counted.

"Five metres," he said. "From Cape Hatteras to Cape Cod. The channel has been open forty minutes and it has not yet seen where. If we close it tonight we will know where with only hours left, and you know better than anyone on this call what hours buy."

He moved his glass a centimetre.

"The Mandate will not turn its code tonight. I ask you to bring me tonight's people by the morning. Every name, and how they are. I give you my word I will weigh them against the coast, and not against my convenience."

"And if they don't wake up?"

"Then I will count them, Director. I do not ask you to agree with me."

The window behind him was white with a Beijing morning. A bird crossed it.

She turned back to Bellamy, and he had the handheld at his chest again.

"What's on that?"

He didn't answer.

"You've watched it since nine. You haven't looked at the board once, not at the pump, not at the clinic, not at Haven. What's on it?"

He looked at her for a long time. Then he turned the screen.

A room in blue-white light. A bed with its rails up, and on the blanket a man's hand, long and thin, the nails cut short by somebody else. Above the bed a green trace. It was moving. Climbing and falling and climbing, a line of hills where there should have been a level road.

"My son," Bellamy said. "Theo." His voice didn't change at all. "He has been in that bed for fourteen years, and I have sat beside that line every week of them, and I have learned not to look at it, because it never once did anything but lie there. At eleven minutes past nine it moved." He turned the screen back toward himself. "It hasn't stopped."

Rachel looked at the hand on the blanket. Then at the white square on the wall, where thirty people breathed on a corridor floor.

"Mara's people have sons," she said.

"Yes," Bellamy said. "I'm sorry for them." And he was. She could see that he was. It made no difference to the code.

She went up the gallery stairs to her room and shut the door. The room was hotter than the floor. The cooling never reached the end of the glass. She petitioned the council.

"Council. Emergency sitting, on petition. Iverson."

"Petition filed," said her wall. "Two further members required. Earliest quorum: 06:00."

Dawn. By dawn Wáng would have his names.

She stood with her hand on the back of her chair. She had the city's sirens and its crews and its boats and its liability. She had kept every rule in this building to the letter, bar one red flag she had cleared in April under her own name and never mentioned, so that when she broke one in public the room believed her. Every one of them reached as far as the wall of this room. None of them reached the switch.

She sat down. She was still. She was good at still. Below her through the glass the floor went on working, because the tags were real and the woman in Red Hook was real, and the night lead sent a crew to an Inwood dialysis flat and didn't look up at the gallery.

After a while Rachel took a pad and a pencil from the drawer and opened Haven's line on her own wall.

"Mara. Give me their names."

A pause the Moon's length.

"Why?"

"Because somebody in Beijing has asked for them by morning. And because I want them."

"The cook from the long galley," Mara said. "Smiling. Breathing well."

Rachel wrote it. The pencil slipped in her damp fingers and she wiped them on her suit and wrote it again.

"The first crop hand. Smiling."

She wrote them down the left side of the page, one under the other, and for every one how they were, and Mara gave them in Haven's order, row by row along the wall.

At eleven Mara said that two of them had stopped smiling, and Rachel wrote that too. Then Ines's nine, from the traces, the names under each line. Thirty-nine names, and above them all, alone, Petra Lind, from the twenty-eighth. She had done this before, in a school gymnasium with a dead radio, and it had never once told her what to do.

At 23:52 the Battery gauge read two metres eighty-one.

· · ·

The crew handset rang at 23:56, on pump house nine's channel.

Her board showed nothing. No caller. No line. Only one thing on the wall rang through nine and showed on nothing.

She thought it would be Daniel Rocha. She picked it up already knowing what she couldn't tell him.

"Rachel."

Not Daniel.

Behind the voice the line was full of a big room: a fan turning, somebody's music off the mesh a little behind whoever was singing it, people talking low in Portuguese. And under all of it, at this end, pump house nine, loud as ever.

"Don't say my name on your floor," said Elena Vázquez.

Rachel didn't. She turned her chair to the harbour.

"Haven?" Elena said.

"Thirty. Nine at Cobalt Basin. Petra Lind's crew." Rachel looked at the page. "Nobody's on the stop."

"I know." A breath, close to the handset. Then the review-room voice, level and exact, the one Rachel had trusted at the last sitting. "I can cut the Earth end. I need you to look away."
