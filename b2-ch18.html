The Narrow Gate · II-B. The old story

18

1 JuneRachelNew York, public broadcastMars delay 21.0 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 21.0 min

"First name," said Joaquín. "The day it gets one. A pastry a square, and I hold the pastries."

He had ruled the grid down the clean end of the flood glass before anyone else was in: thirty boxes for June, and over them, in his big grease capitals, FIRST NAME. Twenty-nine in the street at seven and wet with it, and Joaquín in the camel coat regardless. The glass sweated on its inner face, and the bottoms of his columns had already started to run, as if the season were raining on them early.

On the night lead's second screen the weather service had the Atlantic up for the first day of the season. Nothing turning. One tropical wave coming off Cape Verde, a grey smudge at the sea's far edge, three thousand kilometres from any coast on the list.

The council sat at ten. Wáng had flown in overnight to move that the Gate never close, and Rachel had been up till two with the agenda and found nothing in it to sit across from him with.

"That's June," said the night lead.

"Every year she says it." Joaquín wrote NL in the eleventh. "Every year she pays."

Moss took the fourteenth. Dev Raman took the twentieth and said the wave would die in the dust off Africa, and the floor booed him. At the head of the row, on his bolted stool, Kaelin had his stick across his knees and a coffee he wasn't allowed to bring within two metres of a chair, and Joaquín pointed the pencil at him.

"Drake. You've seen weather from the top."

"I've seen dust from the inside."

"Then you've got an edge. Pick a day."

"The ninth," Kaelin said. "I've a feeling about the ninth."

DRAKE, in the ninth. Joaquín turned with the pencil held out, keys going round on his other finger, the floor's coffee and the morning's pastries on the air round him.

"Jefa. Before your council has you."

She took the pencil. "The fourth."

"The fourth. You always go early. You think the sea's got a deadline, you think it's—"

He stopped.

Not with his face. His face stayed where it was, halfway to a grin. The pencil stayed up. His head turned a few degrees toward the harbour side of the glass, as you turn when someone in the next room has said your name, and the keys stopped on his finger.

Rachel counted without meaning to. Four. Five. Six. Outside the glass the harbour pressed green against the windows and nothing moved in it.

At the head of the row Kaelin tapped his own temple twice and pointed.

Joaquín tapped once on the glass, with the pencil's end, and came back.

"—a forecast," he said. "Not a deadline." He grinned the rest of the way and set the keys going again. "Write it, jefa. You'll lose."

She wrote IVERSON 4 in the fourth box. Her hand was steady. She gave him back the pencil.

· · ·

Kaelin came to her room ten minutes later and didn't sit. Sitting cost him the getting up.

He put a sheet on her desk. The back of a coolant requisition, his tally in a spacer's blunt pencil, one line a day.

TUE. 11:20. 31 s. Clock 28, green. WED. 15:05. 44 s. Clock 27, green. THU. 09:40. 38 s. Clock 27, green.

Under them, fresh, the pencil pressed hard: FRI. 07:41. Glass. No chair.

"He tapped back," Kaelin said. "One tap. Here."

"You taught him."

"I never taught him. He's never had a visor on in his life." He put both hands on top of the stick. "He's lovely when he comes back. That's the trouble. He makes a joke and everybody laughs, and I'm the only one counting."

Green, every time. The audit had Joaquín's after-logoff pattern the highest on the floor and climbing since February. She had put him back in chair four the Monday after the medic's week, because she had four chairs and he had asked.

"Take him off," Kaelin said.

"I'm going to."

"Today."

The clock over her door said 08:02. At nine tonight, if she did what she had lain awake deciding, she would open this floor to the city, every chair and every log, and a stand-down filed this morning would be a recovery event at the top of the log on the day the press first read it. Operator harmed. By morning it would be the only line anyone quoted, and Wáng would quote it kindly.

"Not today." She put the tally in her drawer, on top of the blackout handheld, and shut the drawer. "Watch his hands."

"You're keeping your record clean," Kaelin said. It was her own phrase, and he gave it back to her flat. "I watch everybody's hands. It's the job you gave me." He swung the stick round toward the door.

· · ·

He was in the old review room before her again.

The glass of water was already at his elbow, already moved. The room smelled of old varnish and cold coffee and, faintly, through the vents, low tide.

Behind him the long flood glass held a white-sky morning, the harbour flat as poured milk, the far shore shaking in the heat. The chair sat on the wall screen in an office where it was afternoon. The Charter's senior member was in the room with the board's pin on her lapel, and this time she had brought no brief.

Wáng waited until she had sat.

"Director. Thank you." He folded his hands. "I will be short, because the council has read it. I move that on the third of August the Gate does not close. That it continue as a standing service of the Continuity, under this council, for every city it now serves and any city that asks for it."

"The Gate runs under the civic director's emergency authority," said the chair, for the record.

"Which ends with the emergency," Wáng said. "On the third. I do not move against the Director's authority. I move for the fourth of August."

He was right about it. On the fourth the Gate was the council's, and he had the hands: Rachel had counted them herself on the back of the agenda, the Mandate's and the Charter's member from up the coast, who had watched the São Paulo bulletin with her eyes wet and said so.

"Your reasons," said the chair.

"São Paulo." Wáng let it stand alone a moment, and moved the glass a centimetre. "I counted four hundred and fifty-two that the Director signed, before April, and at São Paulo I stopped, because I could not count that high in an afternoon. In 2053 a central system worked, and my family is alive. I would like this one to go on working past a date on a page. A sky is not a date."

"And the people in the chairs?" said the Charter's member.

"Counted, relieved and paid, on the Director's own limits. I would keep every one of her limits." He looked at Rachel. "I would keep the Director."

She looked down the table at the woman who had moved to tell the coasts in February and lost on Rachel's word.

"I can't stop your vote," Rachel said. "On the fourth it's yours."

"Yes."

"Until the third the warning's mine. It sits under my authority. This council's known since November. The cities on it haven't."

She didn't stand.

"Tonight at nine I tell them. The date. The cities. What's steering it. And that it closes on the third, on my name, on the record. Vote on the fourth to keep a thing the whole coast heard me promise to shut. Do it with the door closed and I'll read your names out on the Battery steps."

The Charter's member wrote one line and underlined it.

Wáng did not touch the glass.

"I ask you not to, Director."

"No."

"Then I ask it again, for the record." He waited until the chair nodded. "Two months is a market."

"In February you said five was."

"In February I said four days was a road. You will have both." He said it without heat. "Those who can leave will leave. Those who cannot will try to be where they are counted. I have seen it. You have counted it."

"Then count it."

"I will. Every one." He looked down at his folded hands and up again. "May I ask one thing?"

"Ask."

"Tell them what saves them," said Wáng. "Do not tell them a forecast saves them. A forecast saves no one. Four people in chairs save them, and the line under them. If you tell a coast it will be safe, tell it why, or you are lying with your name on it. I do not think you will."

It was true. A year and a half ago she had written Nothing in secret into her conditions for this council, and she had not kept it, and the not keeping was on the inside of her wrist in pencil under a buttoned cuff.

"I'll name the Gate," she said.

"Thank you, Director." He drank a little of his water, for once. "The council will vote on the fourth."

· · ·

At 20:58, with the city sitting down to the season's opening outlook as it did every first of June, plates on knees, the year's storm names in a column down every civic screen, Rachel opened a civic event on the authority, logged it to herself, and took the slot.

They stood her at the flood glass with the floor behind her. Four chairs under their clocks, the evening block coupled, the operators' backs to the lens, the cables going down into the frames.

She had asked each of them at six. Lin had said yes for the Mandate before Rachel finished. Moss had asked if she could keep her hair as it was. Dev had said yes. Joaquín was on the wrong block and had gone home to his aunt's, and had said, going, Turn my good side to the city, jefa.

The lens light came up, hot on her face. In every laced ear in New York a name was said, each its own, and after it hers.

"This is Rachel Iverson, civic director, at the Battery."

She had nothing written. She didn't need it.

"On the second of August the orbital ring comes down. Not all of it. Enough. One station breaks and hits the next. Most of it falls in the sea. Some of it falls on coasts." She gave them the coasts. "Recife. Lagos. Chennai. Manila. Santos and São Paulo. New York. Others. New York is on the list."

Behind her, on the debris board, a tag went from white to amber and back.

"A forecast reached this building last July. We've checked it every day since. Everything it's sent this building has been right." True, as far as it went. Every word of it was true. "I'm not going to read you numbers. From nine o'clock the table's on the Battery's public record, every city, every figure. Read your own."

The light was hot. A drop of sweat went down inside her collar and she let it.

"Under this building there's a floor. Four chairs. Four people at a time, linked, under a clock, a reader at the head of the row."

The reader was the line now, in Aur'k's voice, and a spacer on a stool with no cable in reach. She let the word stand.

"We call it the Gate. Since November it's turned the ring's falling pieces off cities. The block over Quebec. The tether Barbuda. São Paulo. You saw São Paulo. On the second of August it steers the ring."

She did not turn round to show them. They could see.

"I won't give you their names. They go home at the end of a block like anybody. Leave them alone."

"The Gate runs under my emergency authority. It closes on the third of August. On my name."

She let that sit, for the record and for the council.

"Stay where you are. Watch the sea as well. It's hurricane season."

The light went down.

· · ·

They had the Battery's steps lit when she came up, and the harbour wind did nothing against thirty degrees at half past nine. Lights held at her. The flowers already on the steps had begun to brown in the heat, and the whole stair smelled of them, sweet and going off. Questions from the dark under them. She stopped for each one as long as the answer took and not longer.

"How long have you known?"

"Since July."

"Why now?"

"Because there's still time to move."

"Who's in the chairs?"

"People who work for me."

"Is it safe?"

"Nothing's safe. It's good."

Then a man's voice from the back, by the railing, the Shallows in his vowels, louder than the others because he had no lace to be heard through.

"Who does it save first?"

The lights swung toward him and back to her.

"Those it can see," Rachel said. "Then the rest."

She heard it go out over the water. Her own heading, off the back of a school timetable on a gym floor in 2071, in her own voice, to everyone.

· · ·

By ten the map wall was moving.

The night trains north from the Polder ran full, then fuller, a white thread at each station going solid. The flood gates at the bridges logged their counts to the floor in tens of thousands, outbound.

And from the south, from the Shallows, where half the city's ears had heard nothing at nine because nothing had said their names, it came secondhand and fast and the other way: lights on the water, water taxis and flat boats and anything with an engine, making for the wall's lowest place, the old ferry steps at the south tip, where you could climb out of the dark onto the lit side with a bag.

On the steps' camera a man stood waist-deep in black water at thirty degrees and handed a sleeping child up to hands she couldn't see.

At the street door the clerk sent up a picture: flowers on the steps, a pile of them already, wilting in the heat as they landed. A woman lifting a baby to the door camera. A man with his fist on the glass.

"Thirty-four thousand messages," said the night lead at eleven. "Most say thank you."

"And the rest?"

"What about us."

Kaelin had stayed on his stool through all of it, the mute switch on, the floor's voice down to text on the panel under his hand. He hadn't got up when she came down from the steps, and he didn't now. He had heard it like everyone else, a name in his ear and then hers.

"The second of August," he said.

"Yes."

"His contract runs to the second of August. I rang him from a beach and he said it was under contract." He turned the stick between his hands, end over end, slowly. "He knew."

"He kept his word to me."

"He's good at that." Kaelin looked at the four chairs, the evening block coupled, the clocks green. "That's what was under contract."

At 23:14 the boat channel opened on the dispatcher's desk.

A water taxi out of the Red Hook stilts, licensed for six, twelve aboard, making for the ferry steps across Buttermilk Channel, had gone over in the wake of a bigger boat making for the same steps. The Battery's two boats were already on the water at the south tip. Rachel stood at the rail with the channel in her ear and the harbour radar on the wall, the two blue tags turning circles in the black.

Ten out. Eleven. The crew lead calling them in by what they were wearing, the boats' own lamps the only light on the channel, because none of them were laced and none of them were on anyone's list.

Then the circles widening, and widening, and the crew lead's voice gone flat.

"One not found. A woman. Her husband's on our deck asking for her by name. He can't give us her district. They haven't got one."

"Keep looking."

"We are."

"Bloody water," Rachel said, under her breath, at the radar.

At 01:30 they stood the boats down. RED HOOK 1 went on her wrist under the others, and the cuff buttoned over it. Wáng's road, the first night. She had told him to count it. She had counted it herself.

· · ·

The listings came to the floor at seven because somebody on the night block had sent them to the dispatcher's screen as a joke, and nobody was laughing at it.

Overnight, in every city on the list, the flats on the relay grid had gone up. New York's raised districts by a third. Recife the same, Manila more. Every listing carried a line it had not carried yesterday, in the plain type the agents used for sea view: On the relay grid. The dark addresses had no listings. Nobody had bothered to try.

Under the listings on the same screen the morning bulletin ran her file photograph and then her on the steps in the lights, and a strip she hadn't been asked about: THE WOMAN WHO TOLD US.

Wáng's post had come at 06:40, to her alone.

Director. The Mandate withdraws its motion. At four this morning Recife's civic director asked the council to keep the Gate after the third. Lagos at five. Manila at half past. Your own council's member from up the coast at six. It is not mine any more. Thank you for naming it. Wáng.

She put the slate face down on the desk and sat with her hands in her lap, and looked through her door's glass at the floor.

The Gate would close on the third of August on her name, and on the fourth every coast on the list would ask her why.

Joaquín came in at five past seven in the camel coat, thirty in the street, keys going round on his finger, a paper bag of pastries for the pool in his other hand. He came straight to her door and didn't knock.

"Three offers on my aunt's flat before seven," he said. He was delighted. "One from a man off the stilts with money in a bag. Cash, jefa. Who's got cash?"

"Is she selling?"

"She's not speaking to me." He put a pastry on her desk. "I told her sell. Take the money. Go up to my sister-in-law's for the summer, sit in the shade, let somebody else pay her electric. It's a good flat. Two bedrooms on the relay grid and she uses one. The other's a spare room nobody uses. Three windows on the park."

He didn't say August. In February he had asked her what was on the second, and she had said Ask me in August, and last night every ear in the city had been told, and he had not come to her door about it.

Rachel knew the park. Forty metres of plane trees at the end of his street, on barged-in ground. She had stood at the back of it in a borrowed white shirt.

"Whose room was it?" she said.

"Nobody's." He thought about it, honestly, his head on one side. "She keeps it nice. Sweeps it. I don't know why." He shrugged and turned the keys. "Old women."

She opened the drawer and took out Kaelin's tally and laid it on the desk between them, beside the pastry.

He read it at arm's length.

"Off four," she said. "From today. You're on the desk. Crew notes, the pool, the boats. Nothing with a cable on it."

"Jefa." He laughed, and then saw her face, and stopped laughing. "I'm green. Every time. Look at it. Green."

"You're green and you're not there."

"I'm there. I'm very good when I'm not there. Ask anyone."

"I'm asking Drake."

He looked at the sheet a long while. Then past her shoulder, through the glass, down the floor to chair four.

"Let me stay on," he said. "One more month. Through the second. You need four."

"No."

He didn't argue. She found she had picked the pencil up off its string and was holding it in both hands. He stood in her doorway turning his keys, looking at the chair, and his face went soft and grateful, as a man's goes at the end of a long day when he sees his own door.

"It's quieter in there," he said.
