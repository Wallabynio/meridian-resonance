The Narrow Gate · II-B. The old story

25 JuneAur'kMars, the GlowMars delay 21.7 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, THE DROP AND THE GLOW / Aur'k — Earth 21.7 min

The washer had sat in the corner since the storm last June. Dae-jung's nut had sat against it since February, and in May Dae-jung had moved on it again and called it a strategy. For a week, with his whole mind back, Aur'k had stood at the shelf and failed to find an answer. This morning it took him four seconds.

He took a second washer from the tin and set it in the corner from the other side, so that the nut was held between them.

Dae-jung looked at the plate on the instrument shelf for a long time. The galley was empty except for the two of them and the smell of the morning's porridge, burnt in the usual place. On the repeater over the slate New York's morning was coming up grey over the harbour, minutes old.

"That's a fork," Dae-jung said.

"It's a strategy."

"You've never had a strategy. You've had a losing record and a personality." He reached out with one sage-green finger and slid three of Aur'k's washers off the far edge of the grid into the tin, one after another, so they rang. "Punished."

"For what?"

"I'll tell you when I've decided." Dae-jung folded his hands. "Welcome home."

The galley speaker clicked, the morning's post from Earth coming in behind the cargo office's. Aur'k had it at the edge of his panel before the click had finished. Kaelin had answered Ines on the nineteenth, in four lines. This one was to him.

His nan has it. Compact, the next day. She said to thank the station for the water, and she said it twice.

I've been on the stool all week. You knew about August when I asked you on the beach. Under contract. I've worked out why you couldn't say. I'm not angry about that one.

K.

He read it twice, the second time for the shape. That one. Kaelin did not waste words on Mars and wasted fewer on Earth. He had put two of them there on purpose, and left a space after them the width of seven years.

Dae-jung was watching him. With Dae-jung that was hard to tell from watching the wall.

"Bad news?"

"Kind news," Aur'k said. "From someone who's guessed."

He opened a reply on his panel. He began it where it began: The tenth of August, 2089. I was on the boundary in my first season. He wrote the name Kaelin had heard him say at the rock, and the promise, and the tally, and what the rock had rung with for six hours, and whose six hours they were. It came out whole. There was no lag on any of it.

Then he did not send it.

Today he would go down and have an answer from the rock, or a refusal. He had never asked it for anything. He meant to ask it to let her in.

A post that said I have kept this from you since my first season should carry more than the keeping. He held that reason up and looked at it, and saw that it was the shape of every reason he had used for seven years, and held the post anyway. The fold of paper on his empty chain lay against his chest plate where it had lain since shelter four.

· · ·

Medical's second bunk had been stripped to the frame and wiped down, and the wipe had dried in streaks. Ines sat on the end of it with a slate on her knee as if it were any other seat.

"Read that," she said, and held it out without looking up.

SIGNATORIES — TRANSIT. MEDICAL RETURN, COBALT BASIN. LIND, P., AND EIGHT. BERTHS ALLOCATED, JAKARTA, DEPARTING 25 JUNE. BOARDING BY 17:00 STATION. REF: VARGA, I. / DRAKE, K., 28 MAY 2095.

"A year and a month," Ines said. "They recommended it into the sea, and then they went and fished it out."

"Why now?"

"Because the ship's empty." She took the slate back. "Nobody on Mars wants a seat to Earth in August. They've got nine bunks and a hold that came light. They'd sell the berths to the dust if it could pay."

Through the hatch the ward's sleepers breathed in their two rows. Across medical, in the fifth bunk, Petra lay with her hair brushed out and the lighthouse card in her fingers. Somebody had moved the card to the other hand since last week. Aur'k noted it, as he noted the drips and the angle of the cots, because a room full of people who didn't move was a room where every small change had been made by someone who loved them.

"You'll put them on it," he said.

"No."

He waited. Ines didn't make him wait long. She had never liked silences she hadn't made herself.

"The lander climbs at four times what they've lain at for a year. Nine minutes of it, on their backs."

She tapped her own chest, below the collarbone. "Hemi had a week of water in there lying still. Put that on its back at four of his weights and see where it goes. Then nine weeks of ship." She turned her head toward him. "You were in the cupboard. Shelters rated for three, by mass. A storm came in and you sat in water for thirty hours."

"Thirty hours and six minutes."

"Who turns them? Every two hours, nine of them, strapped in a cupboard. Who suctions Petra on her back with the ship trimming?" She didn't wait for him. "Nobody. They come off that ship with sores to the bone, the ones that come off at all."

She wrote on the slate with her finger. NOT FIT TO FLY. VARGA, I. Then again, for the next name. He watched her do it nine times. Her writing got smaller toward the end, as if she were trying to make it take up less room.

"He co-signed it," she said, at the bottom. "Kaelin. It's his berth too. He fought them for it on a cot by her bunk." She held the slate a moment longer than she needed to, then put it face down on the frame. "You tell him. You're always telling him things. Important thing first."

"I will."

"Say it as if you mean it. My knees are listening."

"Your knees are always listening."

"They've nothing else to do. They don't bend." She stood, one hand on the frame and then the other, and swore at them. "Dust in your seals. Both of you."

Then she nodded at the slate lying face down on the frame. "There's another name on that list. Go and see."

He knew whose.

Elena was in the ward with the basin and a cloth, washing the galley man's hands one finger at a time, because Ines had said once that hands mattered and Elena had written it down. The Jakarta's list was up on the ward's wall slate beside the traces, under the nine: VÁZQUEZ, E. — RETURN (ONE). BOARDING BY 17:00 STATION.

She had seen it. She was keeping her back to it.

"Your slot," he said.

"I know." She finished a finger. "When's the next?"

"December. The Signatories' next."

He gave her the lag on purpose, a second, so she would know he was choosing his words. He had nothing to give her that she didn't already have. Daniel on a roof. Six more months.

"If I go down and they say no," he said, "you'll have stayed for nothing. And six months is dose. You haven't any left."

"Under a berm. The berm's thicker than the ship's water. I checked."

"Then I'll have stayed." She wrung the cloth into the basin and the water pattered, and her head went left after the sound, toward a roof that wasn't here, and came back. "I didn't come this far to go home with a sentence. Tell the chief to take my name off."

"You could tell her yourself."

"She'll hear me out without interrupting." Elena looked up at his helm. "I'd rather you did. You'll do it faster."

· · ·

The tally lived on the shelf by the boundary monitors, where it had lived for seven years. He opened it at the Drop's head on his knee.

Columns, in his own square hand. Date. Initials. Minutes. A figure with no unit. A narrow column at the right for anything else, empty all the way down but for one line. His own initial ran down the left margin dozens of times. Kaelin's twice, near the bottom. Under the second, in small capitals, A HAND. DOES NOT GROW BACK.

He wrote 25 JUNE. A. and then, in the narrow column, where his own reason had never needed writing because it had always been to listen, he wrote ASKED.

Then he put his palm to the panel and let it log.

The cage took him down the dark at a speed it had never been allowed to use with anyone soft inside it. Steel collars went by every ten metres. He did not count them. He watched for the place, thirty-four down, where the rock began to show blue in the cracks, and when it came he felt it on the cage's frame first, as a change in the cold, and then as light.

At the foot of the Drop the lock cycled for no air. He walked out into the Glow without a lamp, because he had never needed one, and the caverns came up around him lit from inside, the veins faint and branching across the walls and the roof, a coast seen from a long way off at night.

Twenty metres in, the membrane tent stood pegged over the knot, no bigger than a bunk, its heater cold. He went in on his knees, because the knot was under it. Where Kaelin had laid his head last June a patch the width of a hand was black. He knew it was there. He looked at it anyway.

He knelt where he always knelt, and put his palm to the rock beside it, and the rock knew him in the same second.

Kaelin had come up from here with words for it, plains of slow light, and Aur'k understood why a man would reach for a landscape: the attention down here was so large and so far off that only distance would describe it.

To him it was edges. Every mind he had ever read had an edge, and he had spent twenty-two years learning where they stopped. Down here the edges went on and on, folded into one another, old and very tired, and between them one mind kept watch.

There was no air down here to say a name into. He said it through his palm, as he always had. Zhenyth.

The attention came. They had grown more tired in a year. He felt that as he would feel a bridge that had started to sag.

He had built his case on the way down, collar by collar. He gave it in order.

There are four chairs on Earth that go on flying when the people in them have gone home. A man came out of one with his cousin's name and nothing of her. The woman upstairs wants to know what the chairs are. You're the only ones who might.

Zhenyth heard him out and did not interrupt. Then they gave him a hand, closing, and a door, and a long cold silence with listening in it, and that was all.

He knew the closing hand. It was a no.

She built the line it came through, he said through the rock. I know. You told her so this week, by not speaking. She cut it too. On a roof, in the rain, by a clock. Both ends.

Silence. A door, half open, and an ear on the far side of it.

He understood then that he could go on making the case until the next tremor, and that the case was not what they were refusing. They had never refused him anything. He had never asked them for anything to refuse.

So he stopped arguing.

I'm asking. Not her. Me. For her.

Zhenyth turned on him fully then, and he felt how much of them there was, and how little was left.

Out past the tent's membrane, along the near wall, a vein of blue the length of his forearm went out.

The light left it between one of his moments and the next, as a lamp goes when a hand closes on the wick. He knew what it was. It was the cost of being considered. He had written figures like it in the margin of every visit for seven years, and he had never once watched one spent on his account, for his own asking.

A small thing came to him out of the dark. A plate of metal, scratched in squares. Round things on it, some with holes.

What did you move this morning?

He nearly laughed, in the rock. They had put small questions to Kaelin. They had never asked him one.

A washer. Into the corner, beside my other one. The nut's caught between them.

Who wins?

Nobody knows how it's scored. We've played it five years.

He felt them consider that, a game nobody could win, and felt them like it. Then the terms came, one after another, in pictures he could have drawn.

A breath, held and let go. Short.

A threshold, the place where the station's rock met theirs, with a hand laid on it. His. You at the edge.

And a third. It took him a moment, because it came as a person he knew from the inside of her habits. A woman's lips moving on a count against her watch. A hand laid on a chest and left there, waiting for the breath before anything was said. The one who counts breaths with her lips. Beside the body.

Ines.

Beside the body.

Then, because they never told anyone less than was true: The one who built the line is loud. The hungry one knows her. Be quick.

He had his own question ready. He had carried it from Earth. It had been the reason on his leave form. What answers on the floor when I've gone. Is it me. Rachel had said, with his key on her desk, Ask it for me too, and he had said For me first.

The dark vein lay in the wall at the edge of his sight.

He did not ask.

Thank you, he said instead, and took his palm off the rock, and the Glow lay down around him, one vein dimmer than he had found it.

On his way out he laid his forearm along the dead vein, because there was no other ruler for it, and kept the length. At the top he opened the tally on his knee and wrote the figure in. It was the first in seven years beside his initial that had a word next to it.

· · ·

Ines heard him out in medical, standing, because she had sat down once that day and didn't trust her knees to do it twice.

He told her all of it. The three terms in the order they came. What they said about the hungry one. The vein. What she would be, down there: a medic in a tent at minus fifty, the length of the Drop under a station, beside a woman with her head on the rock, and him at the top on the end of the cable.

"I don't know what it costs you," he said. "I won't say it's nothing. I don't know why they asked for you, except that they watch who keeps people breathing. I'm asking you anyway."

"You don't ask people things."

"I'm doing it today."

Ines looked at him for a while. Then she put her hand flat on his chest plate, as she did with patients, and left it there for a count.

"You've gone warm," she said. "Is that you or the rock?"

"Me."

"Good." She took the hand away. "I drove out to the Ice-Works under a falling ice load. Funmi stayed at the valve. I came back." She went to the cabinet and began taking things out of it without looking: a suction line, a bag, a cap for a socket. "My knees have been to worse places."

"Name one."

"Your galley chair. The one without arms." She shut the cabinet. "Yes. Go and tell Vázquez she's got her medic."

Ines took Elena's wrist in medical. Earth had locked that lace to medical under the inquiry, and since then it had told her nothing but her pulse. Ines laid her own wrist across it, lace to lace, as if taking a pulse with the wrong part of her arm.

MEDICAL LOCK. ATTENDING: VARGA, I. RELEASE FOR PROCEDURE?

"What procedure?" Elena said.

"I'll think of a name on the way down." Ines pressed it. RELEASED. VARGA, I. "That's on mine now. If the inquiry wants it they can come to Mars and ask me."

Elena looked at her wrist for a long time.

"Thank you."

"Don't. Eat something. You'll be sick in a suit otherwise and I'm not cleaning a helmet at minus fifty."

The chief heard them out in ops at four, the three of them in front of her desk and the programme's old schedule still on the wall behind her. Aur'k gave it as boundary lead: a descent at twenty-three twenty-five, after the hourly tremor, logged on his name. Ines gave it as medic: attending, the lock released on hers. The chief did not interrupt either of them. When they had finished she did not say anything for some time.

"I told her the boundary is the boundary lead's," she said at last. "I told her the lattice is nobody's, on her licence." She looked at Ines. "And a medic's release outranks a licence on a body. Earth wrote that, not me. I can't stop it." She wrote on her slate. "Objection logged. Command. Against both of you, by name."

"Logged," said Aur'k.

"You understand what I'm writing."

"I understand it's going in the record," he said. "That's why we came to you."

On the repeater over the chief's desk the pad camera showed the lander standing in the late light, butterscotch sky going grey toward the east, its legs in the dust. At 17:00 the board beside it closed its boarding list. Nine lines read NOT FIT TO FLY. One read WITHDRAWN. At 17:40 its engine lit, white at the base and then gone, a bright point climbing out of the frame toward a ship nobody on the station could see.

Elena watched it go from the back of ops with her hands in her jacket pockets. Mara stood beside her until the point had gone. Then she turned to Aur'k.

"While she's down there," Mara said. "Ask them about mine."

"Short, they said."

"Then ask short." The Haven pause. "Eleven of mine are in the ground. Thirty breathe. Somebody owes them a sentence."

Elena took one hand out of her pocket and looked at it, and put it back.

· · ·

They went down at twenty-three twenty-five, four minutes after New York's hour had trembled through the rock and lain down.

He stood at the reader with his palm on the plate. Through the monitors the grey band answered him, steady, waiting. The cage camera showed two suits in the cage's light, one tall and narrow, one short and favouring a knee. The winch sang. The band carried Ines's breathing and her running commentary on it.

"Ten. My left knee would like it on record that it didn't volunteer."

"Noted," said Aur'k.

"Twenty. My right knee volunteered. It's an idiot."

Elena said nothing for a long time. Then, at thirty-one, "Ines. You're counting."

"Somebody has to. He won't." The suit lamps swung against the steel. "Thirty-four. Oh. Look at that. That's blue."

On the camera the short suit tapped twice on the tall one's visor and pointed. The tall one tapped once. Here. He kept his palm where it was.

The lock at the foot cycled. The lamps went off the camera's edge, along the wall, twenty metres, and he heard them go into the tent on their knees, Ines swearing at it (Mafan. Dust and mafan.). The gauge brought the air up. Collars unsealed on the band, one and then the other, and the heater began its ticking.

"What is it out there?" Elena said.

"Minus fifty outside, the gauge says." Ines's voice had warmed itself on the joke before it arrived. "It's the only thing on two worlds that isn't getting hotter. Don't tell Earth. They'll want it shipped."

"Pole," Aur'k said.

"I'm always pole. My knees don't do anything else." A rustle on the band. "Socket cap. Give it here. They stick in the cold."

"He told me," Elena said.

"There. Lie on your side. Head where the blue's thickest. No, there, away from the dark bit, that's spoken for." The band went soft with Ines close to the pickup. "Hand on you. I'm here. I'll count you."

On the plate under his palm the grey band rose to meet the two of them, slow, considering, a held breath. Zhenyth's terms. Short. Him at the edge. The one who counts, beside the body.

"Going in," Elena said. He heard her cheek meet the rock.

"Twelve a minute," said Ines. "Good. Thirteen. You're fine. My knees are—"

The plate jumped under his hand.

No tremor. It heaved, all the rock at once, as a deck heaves when the sea stands up under it, and the module rang to its bolts, and the dust came down out of every seam in the roof in a red sheet. The monitors went white from edge to edge. The cage's cable, the whole Drop of it, sang on its drum like a struck wire.

The band was open, and on it there was nothing at all.

He kept his palm on the plate.
