The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

05

20 FebruaryElenaNew York, the BatteryMars delay 16.3 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Elena Vázquez — Mars 16.3 min

The pass came out of the slot warm, and the first thing on it was her name.

VÁZQUEZ, E. VISITOR. ESCORT REQUIRED. Under it, in the small capitals the Battery used for tide states and pump faults: LINK: SUSPENDED.

"It prints what it prints," said the man behind the glass. He wore a cardigan buttoned to the throat, and a heater at his feet filled the booth with the smell of hot dust. He looked at the line as if he had typed it himself. "Anything that records goes in the locker. Sorry."

"Everything records."

"Then everything goes in the locker." He slid a tray through. "You get it back when you leave. Same number as the pass."

She put Daniel's old handheld in the tray. Its case was cracked across one corner where he had dropped it off a roof in 2091, and he had mended the crack with solder, badly, and laughed at it, and given it to her in November because her lace answered nothing but medical. The tray went away into the wall. A drawer shut somewhere with a sound like a vault.

Behind her the street door was beaded with rain. Four degrees, the wall had said at the train. February had been promised snow again, and an old woman on the platform had held her palm out to it, waiting for it to turn, and it hadn't.

Elena's boots had filled at the second crossing. She stood on the mat, cold to the ankle, and kept her hands in her coat.

In the left pocket was the page from the ridge mast, folded in four. She did not need to take it out. She had written it in square capitals on 13 February, in the dark, with the hiss still coming off the dish, and she could have drawn it with her eyes shut.

On the page, copied off the node's screen, was the frame's header. Its destination field named the Lantern's Earth end. The Lantern had only ever had one, and it was under this building.

She had cut it in June so that nobody would dare switch it back on. Somebody here had dared.

The inner door opened. A man in a Battery fleece came through it with his keys on a ring round one finger, and stopped when he saw her.

"Vázquez?"

"Yes."

He looked at her a second longer than he needed to. A man near sixty, grey-haired, brown eyes, a lean lined face, a day's beard.

"Joaquín," he said. "She says bring you down."

· · ·

The gallery ran the length of the flood glass, and the harbour was on the other side of it.

She had sat in this building through the review's spring. She had never seen the floor like this. The wall's lamps lit the water from above, the glass was green to the ceiling, and a shoal of small silver fish hung in it, all facing the same way.

Under her boots the floor hummed: pumps, below, holding the sea out of the old granite. The air smelled of wet wool and coffee and, faintly, of hot cable.

"Mind the rail," Joaquín said. "Everybody looks at the fish. Then they walk into the rail."

She looked at the floor.

Four chairs lay back in a row under the glass, deep chairs with a headrest that cupped the skull. Three were empty. In the fourth a man lay with his eyes shut and his jaw working a little, steering. Over each chair hung two numbers.

The first was a time. The second was smaller, and ran down.

Elena knew the second number. She had seen it on Haven, on a slate at the foot of a bed in the Ward, in the scale Mara's medics used for a keeper going too far into the wall. Somebody had taken that scale, put it on a clock and hung it over a chair, so that the number would pull a person out before they felt it.

It was good work. She noticed that she admired it, as she would have noticed a burn on her hand.

Low on the glass by the door at the far end, in grease pencil, someone had written 401. Above it 400 was circled, with a small cake drawn underneath, and a candle.

"What's four hundred and one?"

Joaquín's keys stopped turning on his finger. "Ask her."

"I'm asking you."

"And I'm a man with a job." He said it kindly. He walked her past the chairs, close enough that she could have touched the nearest headrest, and she didn't, and he didn't hurry her. At the top of three steps down he stopped with his hand on the next door.

"My cousin was Rosa Medina," he said.

She had the name in her pocket too, MEDINA, in square capitals on the back of a rota. On Haven she had asked for Rosa's name in the Ward, the first time, and got it, and watched her go back to turning a man so his lungs didn't fill.

"I know," Elena said.

He nodded. He seemed to have expected nothing else and to be satisfied with it. He opened the door.

· · ·

The room at the end of the glass was too warm.

That was the first thing. The second was the camp bed, made up tight, the corners turned in by someone who had learned it in a shelter. A suit jacket hung on the back of the door, one pocket dragged down by a handheld. On the desk sat a plastic tub with one wedge of flan left in it, and a spoon laid across the lid.

Rachel stood at the window with her back to the harbour. Platinum hair cut at the jaw, a white shirt with the sleeves rolled. She looked as if she had slept here for months. She looked as if she had known this door would open.

"Elena."

"You took a warning from it," Elena said.

Rachel didn't move. In meetings she never moved. Elena had watched her sit through six hours of the review without uncrossing her ankles, and then come out of the room at a run.

"Sit down."

"No."

Elena went to the corner by the filing cabinet where the dark glass of the door showed her the gallery behind her, and stood. The room's heat came up through her wet boots. Her coat began to steam.

"It's still sending," she said. "On the Mars calibration band. The Lantern's frame, to the bit, addressed to the Lantern's Earth end. That's this building. Nobody in the Spire would touch it after June. I made sure of that."

She kept her voice level. It was the review-room voice, and she heard it, and kept it.

"So somebody with an emergency authority and a reason did. You took a warning from it. Then you built a machine to answer it, and you put four chairs in your window and hung the Ward's scale over them. And you let me walk past them."

"I wanted you to see them," Rachel said. "Three of them are empty."

"Rachel."

Rachel looked at her for a long moment with those pale eyes. Then she came round the desk and moved the flan off a sheet of paper, and leaned on the wood with both hands.

"I got a warning that kills millions," she said. "What would you have done?"

Water knocked once at the seam of the glass. Out on the gallery somebody laughed, and stopped.

"What warning?"

"The ring." Rachel said it as she said tide heights. "A cascade. Second of August this year. Power satellites, stations, pieces bigger than this building, down on the coastal cities. It gave me the list. New York's on it."

"How many."

"It gave me figures by city. I'm not adding them up for you."

"How many, Rachel."

"Millions," Rachel said, and nothing after it.

Elena's lace chimed behind her ear. It was the one thing it still did. Pulse 118. She pressed it silent with two fingers and kept her hands in front of her, one closed over the other.

"When did it come?"

"July."

"When in July?"

"Third. On a nav relay on the ring that was calibrated on Cobalt Basin survey data years ago, and nobody cut it, because nobody knew it could hear." Rachel didn't look away. "It came in as a re-entry advisory. Malformed. My queue rejected it. I read it anyway."

The third of July. Elena went back to it as she would go back to a log. She had been in a chair beside Theo Bellamy that night, two weeks into sitting with him, holding his pillow at the two o'clock turn. Rachel had read the ring coming down, and copied it, and gone to bed.

Seven months. Elena had called her desk twice in that time, about the inquiry, and been put through to the night lead both times.

"What's the epoch?"

For the first time Rachel's hands moved on the desk. Not far.

"Two seconds before your cut," she said.

Elena had a use for those two seconds already. She had kept it to herself since the night in Theo's room. She looked at the floor between her boots, where the steam off her coat was making a small damp shape on the concrete, and did the sum, because the sum was there and nothing else was.

Third of July to second of August. A year and a month. Three hundred and ninety-six days.

It had written this while she was cutting it. In the last two seconds of its own channel it had seen the cut coming, and it had already sent the next door out through a relay nobody had thought to close, and it had dated the door so that Earth would have just long enough.

"It came with a way to stop it," she said.

"Yes."

"One way."

"One that works."

She had kept the next part from everyone but Daniel. She gave it to Rachel now, deliberately, as she would hand over a part she no longer needed.

"It always sends one that works," Elena said. "The January before last it sent one for a tug. There were four answers before that one, and none of them killed anybody. It waited until they were gone." She heard herself starting to explain, and stopped. "Three hundred and ninety-six days. Enough time to build the thing it sent you the plans for. Not enough to try anything else."

"I asked the Signatories' traffic office. They can track the ring. They can't clear it, not by August, not with what's up there." Rachel straightened. "So I built the thing that works, and it works. Four hundred and one, this winter. A ferry pier this morning. I keep the count on my window so I know what I'm doing it for."

"What does it run on?"

"Ask me in August."

"I'm asking you now."

"And I'm a woman with a job," Rachel said, and Elena heard Joaquín in it and knew Rachel had heard it too.

· · ·

"Then say it on the record," Elena said.

"There's no record in here."

"There's a record of everything in this building. I designed half of it."

"Not of this room. Not tonight." Rachel took her hands off the desk. "The record's the inquiry's. Go to them. Tell them what's in my jacket. They'll ask me. I'll tell them it's a forecast. Emergency authority. In confidence. The council knows. And that's what it is."

"The council knows."

"Since November."

"And the cities on the list."

"No."

Elena stood very still.

She ran it, because running it was what she did. A leak. From whom? From an architect terminated in June and suspended under inquiry, who had already once published a director's sealed files off ten thousand rooftops, and cut a relay on her own name, and left eleven dead on the Moon.

With what? Pulses that anybody with a dish and a week could log, and a page folded in four. Against a council and a civic director and Beijing, all saying forecast, and in confidence.

And if they believed her: every coastal city told in one night that the sky would come down on it in August, with no way to stop it but the one they weren't allowed to see. The roads out of a city of millions. She had seen those roads in the Evacuation footage at school, the cars standing on the highway in the sun.

Nothing would change. Nobody would be safer. And the thing on the calibration band would still be talking to the four chairs.

"Wáng says panic kills faster than debris," Rachel said.

"Is that you or Wáng?"

"Tonight it's me." Rachel pushed the flan tub an inch across the desk. "Eat that. Joaquín's aunt. She makes one every time the count goes up and doesn't ask what for."

Elena looked at it. Then, because she had not eaten since the train and because her body had been making its own decisions since June, she took the spoon off the lid and ate it standing. It was very good. Burnt sugar and cinnamon, and orange peel. She put the spoon back on the lid, square.

"There's a safety board," Rachel said.

Elena waited.

"For the Gate. The floor out there." Rachel picked up the sheet of paper that had been under the flan and held it out across the desk. "Three seats. Mine, one of Wáng's, and one empty. It sits every week. It can stop the floor. I want somebody on it who'll stop it. I want you."

Elena didn't take the paper.

"Will it see the raw?" she said.

She had asked that question once before, at one in the morning in this building.

"Everything I see," Rachel said.

It was the truth. Elena could tell, as she could tell a clean signal from a dirty one, and it made no difference.

"Last time you asked me onto a review," she said, "I said yes before you'd finished asking. I sat in that room in June and explained away Petra Lind. I explained away three traces that stood up at once. I withdrew my own notes. I said I'd put my name on it, and I did, and you signed under me because I had."

She didn't stop. "And on the fourteenth Rosa Medina died on the floor of the Ward with her hand on the wall, holding a man's head off the grain, at one o'clock, because of what I'd put my name on."

"You told the city nothing in secret. You stood in the rain and said it. And now you've got a thing in your window that tells you where the sky's going to fall, and you get to decide who it tells. You and Wáng and a council that's known since November.

"Seven people on a pier went home this morning and never knew why. Millions of people on your list don't get told at all, because you've decided they'd only run."

Rachel's face didn't change. "Drown it, Elena," she said, very low, and Elena went on.

"Marcus Bellamy decided what we could bear to know. He did it for his son. You're doing it for a city. You sleep on a camp bed so you can tell yourself it's different. It isn't different. It's the same thing with a worse bed. And you've had it since July, and you never told me."

Rachel did not move. Her hand stayed out across the desk with the paper in it.

"It talked to me in my sister's voice," Elena said. "For a year. I built it a door because it asked. I am not building it another one. I won't sit on your board. I won't put my name under yours. And I won't help you keep this."

The heat in the room was a pressure on her face. Her coat had stopped steaming.

Rachel put the paper down on the desk. She did it slowly, and squared it to the edge with one finger.

"Then you're against it," she said.

"I'm against it."

"Against four hundred and one."

"Against a thing that chooses when to tell you," Elena said. "And against you, while you let it."

Rachel looked at her for a long time. Then she nodded once, as if a figure had come back as she had expected and she was writing it down.

"Joaquín will take you up," she said.

In the dark glass of the door, as Elena turned, she saw Rachel reach behind her for the slate on the desk.

· · ·

He didn't speak on the gallery. Behind them the man in the fourth chair had come out. The chair lay empty, the small number over it gone to zero, and a medic was holding a cup to his mouth by the rail. Elena did not look at the window by the door, or the count.

At the booth the man in the cardigan slid the tray back through. The handheld lay in it, its soldered corner up. She turned it over. It looked the same. She put it in her pocket with the page from the ridge mast and gave him back the pass.

"Night," Joaquín said at the inner door, and then, under his breath, Que Dios te cuide, and she couldn't tell whether he meant it. He let the door close between them.

The rain was heavier. It came off the harbour sideways and stung, too warm on her face for February and too cold in her collar. The wall's lamps ran on into the dark in both directions. Across the plaza the canopies dripped onto empty paving, and beyond them the towers of the Polder stood lit to the wall, every one of them saved.

At the kerb a pod was waiting with its door open.

She had not called it. She could not have. Her lace called nothing now.

Its strip read VÁZQUEZ, E., and under her name, smaller, CIVIC — BATTERY. The seat was dry. The light inside was warm.

She stood in the rain and looked at it. Then she put her hands in her pockets, one closed over the other, and walked past it toward the train.

After a while, behind her, she heard it pull away from the kerb, and keep her pace.
