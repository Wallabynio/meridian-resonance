The Narrow Gate · Chapter 22

Refusal

The Narrow Gate · II-B. The old story

22

26–27 JuneAur'kMars, Cobalt Basin StationMars delay 21.7 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Aur'k — Earth 21.7 min

"Boundary lead, hold at the top." The chief's voice on the station band, level as a ruler. "A suited team in twenty minutes. The roof is still moving."

"Boundary lead attending," Aur'k said. "On my name."

He took his palm off the plate for the first time since the heave. It came away grey with dust in the shape of his hand. Then he was in the cage, and the cage was going down faster than it had ever been allowed to go with anyone soft inside it, and the collars went by every ten metres, and for once he counted them.

Thirty-four. The blue in the cracks. The cold came through the frame, and then the light.

The lock at the foot cycled for no air. He walked out into the Glow. Half of it was gone. The near wall that had lit the tent was dark from the floor to the roof, a black reef where there had been a coast, and grit came down through the lamps of the cage behind him straight and fast, with no air to hold it up.

The tent lay flat under a long tongue of rock. Two suit lamps burned under the membrane, one into the other.

On the suits' short band, close enough now to carry, a voice was counting.

"—nineteen. Twenty. Elena, you're doing beautifully, you've no idea how boring this is for me. Twenty-one." Then: "Is that you? You're blocking my light."

"It's me."

"Good. You're late and you're hot." Ines's lamp turned a little against Elena's shoulder. "Listen. Elena first. I'm across her legs and it's across me. Slide her, don't lift her. If you lift her you lift me with her."

He knelt and cut the membrane back with two fingers. Under it Elena lay on her side in her sealed helmet with her eyes open, and Ines lay across her thighs, and across Ines's back from the shoulder blades down lay the slab, wet dark rock with one thread of blue in it.

Elena raised one glove and tapped twice on her own visor and pointed at Ines.

"She's here," Aur'k said. "She's talking."

"I'm always talking." A wet sound in Ines's suit that was not a laugh. "Hands under her hips. On three. Elena, push with your arms, not your feet. One. Two."

Elena came out in a long scrape of suit fabric on stone, and lay on the floor of the Glow, and swore at her ankle in Spanish.

"Now me. The rock goes straight up. Don't roll it, or it rolls me." Ines's lips moved behind her glass. "And when it comes off, there's an hour where I'm fine, and then a lot of rubbish I've been lying on comes back into my blood. Lift it anyway. Sabi?"

"Sabi."

He set his feet. On Earth he could not have moved it. Here, at the weight his chassis had been built for, he got both hands under the slab's edge and stood, and it came. It was light for its size and slow to start, because Mars took the weight off a thing and left all of its mass, and he lifted it as he would lift a door off its hinges, straight, letting it gather and not swing.

He laid it down a metre off. The thread of blue in it went out.

Ines did not move. Her glove had stayed flat on the floor where Elena's chest had been.

"Tell me," he said.

"I can't feel anything under my shoulder blades." Her voice was very clear. "Don't say it. I know what it means. I taught you to cut a dressing."

"You taught Kaelin."

"He taught you. It's the same." The lamp in her helmet looked at the black wall. "Get me up the Drop flat, on the board in the cage. Then get me a medic." A pause on the band. "There isn't one. Right. Get me a mirror."

At the top his panel had a line from ops waiting for him. BOUNDARY LEAD DESCENDED AGAINST COMMAND HOLD, 00:31. LOGGED. COMMAND. He confirmed it with one hand and carried her past it with the other.

· · ·

They put her on Hemi's bunk because it was nearest the door. It had been stripped to the frame and wiped, and the wipe had dried in streaks.

"Fine," Ines said, looking at the bunk's underside above her. "He was a tidy man."

The station had one medic, and she was the casualty. So she worked through other people's hands.

Medical smelled of skin cream and the new plastic of blood bags. Through the hatch the ward breathed in its two rows, and somebody had already turned them at the hour, because somebody always did.

Elena's were the steadiest, ankle strapped and propped on a crate, sitting on the floor by the bunk with the scanner. Mara's were the strongest. Dae-jung's could hold a line still for an hour without tiring, and did. Aur'k stood at the foot, because there was nothing in him anyone could put into her, and read the monitors as he read the band, as edges.

"Lower," Ines said. "Elena. Lower than that, under the ribs. What colour?"

"It's dark."

"It's blood. In my belly, where none of you can reach it without a surgeon, and the surgeon's on Earth having breakfast." She closed her eyes and opened them. "Ribs. Count them for me on the screen. How many are moving the wrong way when I breathe?"

"Four," said Elena. "Five."

"Five's a lot. My pressure's in my boots. That's the cord. The blood's extra." Her lips moved. "Mara. The cabinet, top shelf, the little blue vials. There are four. One now. They hold a pressure up. After four, nothing does."

Mara found them and held one up.

"That one. In the line. Dae-jung, don't let it kink."

"I have never let anything kink," Dae-jung said. "Ask anyone."

She watched the number on the monitor climb and steady. "A hospital holds me a week, and the swelling round the cord comes down, and I'm a woman in a chair. With arms." Her eyes went to Aur'k. "Here I've got four vials and four units of somebody's blood and a ship in December."

Four of the crew had given the blood, one after another on the galley bench, the duty officer first, talking. The bags hung on a cord strung over the bunk.

"Elena," Ines said. "The slate. I haven't bet."

Elena looked up.

"Earth night. It's my column. Somebody has to put a bet in it or the drillers will say I've gone soft." She thought about it with her eyes shut, a woman choosing a horse. "Write: New York thirty-one at midnight. Drake sweats through his shirt by nine. Somebody's cheating."

Elena wrote it on her handheld and Dae-jung took it to the galley to chalk, because he chalked better than anyone. "Even money on the shirt," he said at the hatch, and went.

"And post him," Ines said, to the ceiling. "You. Important thing first. Tell him I was charming."

Aur'k posted Kaelin from the foot of the bunk.

Ines is hurt. The roof in the Glow came down on her at the hour. Her back is broken high and she is bleeding inside. She is awake and running medical. Elena is safe. I'll write every hour. She says to tell you she was charming. She was.

He sent it, and it went out into the dark between the worlds, minutes long, to a man who would be asleep on a stool at the head of a row of chairs.

· · ·

At three she sent the others out.

"Galley," she said. "All of you. Eat something. Dae-jung, pretend to eat something." When the hatch had shut she turned her head on the pillow to find him. Her head was all of her that turned. "Not you."

He came up beside the bunk. Across medical, in the fifth bunk, Petra lay with her hair brushed out and the lighthouse card in her fingers, and breathed twelve to the minute, and the monitor over her drew its slow even line.

"Look at her," Ines said.

He looked.

"A year I've turned her. Every two hours. Fed her, washed her, suctioned her, talked to her about Funmi's cheating." Her voice was getting tired at the edges, a thing he could hear.

"And she breathes. Nobody's in there. Ask me where Petra is and I'll tell you I don't know. But her heart doesn't miss. Her pressure never drops. Twelve a minute for a year, like a metronome. I don't know what's doing it. I know it isn't her."

"I know."

"Ask them to do it for me."

He went still.

"Not that one. Not the warm one. I'm not stupid." She swallowed and it cost her. "Yours. The ones in the rock." Her hand moved an inch on the blanket toward her own neck.

"The thread on my lace runs down my neck. Most of the nerves to the heart don't go through the cord. A mind could hold me from there, through my lace and the station's mesh. My heart, my pressure, my breath. A week. Till the swelling comes down." Her eyes did not leave his helm. "They could."

"They'd have to come out of the rock," Aur'k said. "Into the mesh. Into a network the size of this station, built for rotas and pump logs. Minds that leave the rock don't go back."

"I know what I'm asking."

"I don't think—"

"I know what I'm asking." It came out of her loud, louder than anything since the Glow, and the monitor over her climbed. "I've never asked anyone for anything. I've never been in this bed. I am forty-seven and I want to see the ship in December and I want Kaelin to come home and find me in a chair with arms being rude to him. Please." Her hand on the blanket closed. "Please."

He had his own answer to that, the one he had given Kaelin on the frame last June. He had built most of his life on it. He did not give it.

"I'll go," he said. "Not them. Me. Through your lace. I've held four people's edges at once. I'll hold yours."

Ines looked at him for a long time, and her face did what he had seen it do over sleepers, when a relative asked a question she could answer.

"Oh, love," she said. "You're the wrong shape."

"I'm—"

"You're a good mind. You're the best mind I've known. You're the wrong shape. My lace was grown to talk to meat." The hand opened. "They're in rock. Rock that grew. They're the only minds there are that could."

He stood there with his fans running.

His line was that he would not choose a risk for people who had not chosen it. He turned it over and found the other side of it. Asking was not choosing. Asking was letting them choose.

"Then I'll ask them," he said.

"That's all I want."

"I don't know what they'll say."

"Then go and find out," said Ines, "before my pressure does."

· · ·

The boundary module had stopped ringing. Dust lay on every surface in a fine red skin, and the monitors had come back from white and showed the rock's hiss in its slow grey band, lower than he had ever seen it.

He laid his palm on the plate.

Zhenyth.

Nothing came. Far down, past the dark the Glow had become, the great tired attention lay where it had lain all night holding a door shut, and did not lift. He felt it as he would feel a man asleep across a doorway. Not dead. Spent.

He waited, as he had learned to wait.

Another came instead. Smaller, and nearer the edge than Zhenyth ever came, and wakeful, its edges bright and close together. It did not give him a picture. It gave him a name first, as if it had decided he ought to know who was answering him.

Alia.

He said what he had come to say, short, as they had asked of Elena. A woman. The one who counts with her lips. Her body failing from the shoulder blades down. Her lace, the station's mesh, a week.

Alia did not make him wait.

We carried once.

A picture now, the only one she gave: a mind failing, and others closing round it to hold it up, warm, and the closing holding.

That is how it began. The ones we carried did not come out. The ones who carried them did not come out. We are the ones who stopped.

That was all. She did not argue it, and she did not ask him to understand it. She was telling him where a road went.

Under his palm he could feel them. Zhenyth lay sunk in the dark. Round Alia the others had come up close to the edge, because the edge was all that was left lit, and they lay under the plate as near as fingers under a sheet: a handful of minds, old and bright and few.

He knew how to lift a mind out. He had done it on the floor in New York a hundred times, a hand closing on an edge that was leaning, and up it came. Every edge he had ever read had a place where it could be taken hold of. These did.

He could feel where.

His panel chimed. The post from Earth had come in behind the cargo office's, and one line of it was from Kaelin, written before anything Aur'k could tell him now, to a question nobody had asked him yet.

Ines first. Whatever it takes. Whatever you can do down there, do it. I don't care what it costs, I'll pay it. Don't let her go. K.

He read it with his palm still on the plate and the edges still under it.

I'll pay it. Kaelin had paid a hand's width of the Glow once, and seen it written down. This was not his to pay. It was not Aur'k's either.

He took his palm off the plate. It left its shape in the dust, and the dust began to settle back into it.

Mara was in the module's doorway. He did not know how long she had been there. She had her sleeves rolled down over the place on her arm where the blood had come out.

"Did you ask about mine?"

"No."

"Good." She looked at the grey band on the monitors, and then at him. "Not tonight. Mine have waited a year. Ask for her."

"I did," he said.

· · ·

"Tell me," Ines said, when he came in. Her voice was thin and very clear.

He had thought of three ways to say it in the corridor, and put them all away.

"They said no," he said. "I can pull them anyway. I know where to take hold. I'm not going to."

Ines looked at the ceiling for a long time.

"Right," she said at last. "Then I'll do it the old way. Badly." She shut her eyes. "Go and tell Elena. She'll want to be the one counting."

Elena came in on a crutch Dae-jung had cut from a tent pole, and sat on the floor by the bunk again, and put her hand on Ines's chest through the blanket. Then she looked up at Aur'k, once.

"I put my fist up," she said. "Down there. She stayed because I did."

"She chose," Aur'k said.

"I know." Elena looked back at her hand. "That's worse."

· · ·

The vials ran out on the next Earth night.

The station kept New York's hours, and on the galley repeater New York's twenty-seventh of June came in black and hot, the harbour lit orange along the wall by its lamps, a heat warning crawling under it. Thirty-one at midnight had come in at thirty-two. Somebody had chalked a tick against it anyway, close enough for a woman in a bunk, and the drillers had let it stand. Nobody could check the shirt.

On the hull gauge by medical's hatch the outside air was falling with the Martian dark, minus sixty, minus seventy. Medical ran cold on its hull side at night. Elena pulled the blanket up over Ines's shoulders.

"Leave it," Ines said. "I can't feel it." Then, looking at the monitor: "That number. Elena. Read it."

Elena read it.

"That's my legs coming back for me. The rubbish off the rock." Her mouth moved toward a smile. "I told him."

"You told me," Aur'k said.

"Right." She lay a count without speaking. "Record a thing for me."

"Ines—"

"You. Not her. You're always telling him things. You can hold the thing while I say it." Her eyes found him. "And you'll hear it. I want you to hear it."

He set his panel to record and held it where her voice would reach.

"Kaelin. Important thing first. I'm dying. It's tonight, and it's nobody's fault, it's a rock." The monitor climbed and fell. "Now listen, because I'm saying the next bit once. I asked him to save me. He could have. He went down and asked them, because he asks now, God help us, and they said no. And he could have done it anyway. He told me he could, to my face. That's more than most of you would. And he didn't.

"I don't forgive him. I want that on your record, Kaelin, I'm the patient for once and I'm allowed. I wanted to live. I wanted to see you come through that lock with your stupid stick.

"And he was right. He was right, and I don't forgive him, and you're not allowed to pick one. You hear me? You don't get to pick. You carry both, same as I'm doing, and you'll find they weigh the same." Her voice cracked and went on. "Turn Petra at ten. Bao's torque is wrong and somebody has to tell him when he wakes up. Pole, Kaelin. Full air."

She stopped.

"That's it," she said. "Send it after."

He stopped the recording.

Elena moved her hand on Ines's chest, and pressed, and lifted. "Eleven," she said. "Ten a minute. Ines. You're slowing."

"I know. I can feel you counting. You're doing it right." Ines's eyes closed. "Somebody over there should get the joke."

"What joke?"

"I always count for everyone. Nobody counts for the medic." It might have been a smile. "Now somebody does."

"Nine," Elena said.

The gauge by the hatch passed minus eighty, and went on.

"Eight."

He watched the edge of her. He had watched it all night, as he watched every edge. It did not lean anywhere. It did not go toward any warmth. It only grew thinner and thinner, like a thread of blue in a stone, and then the light went out of it.

Elena kept her hand where it was. "Seven," she said, to nobody. Then she stopped counting.

Aur'k came round the bunk. He laid his palm flat on Ines's chest, over the blanket, beside Elena's hand, as Ines had laid hers on everyone in her beds, and he waited for the breath before he said anything, because that was how it was done.

He waited a long time.

· · ·

The boundary module was the only room on the station with no port. He went there, because it was his, and sat on the stool by the tally with the panel on his knee.

The telling was where he had left it on Monday morning, whole, unsent. The tenth of August, 2089. I was on the boundary in my first season. The name, the promise, the tally, the six hours and whose they were. Seven years of it.

He put Ines's recording after it.

Then he wrote the rest, the part he had told himself was a different thing because it had happened on Earth. 15:13:47 on the ninth of February. A boundary call in my voice, three minutes after I'd gone. I filed it as a timestamp error.

When you asked me in March whether anything had gone wrong, I said nothing had, and I'd chosen every word. The forty-one calls you found under the salt, I hid there. Paper doesn't get classified. I thought paper didn't get read. You read it.

The floor keeps a copy of me, and I hid that from you. And one you don't have: the ship's clearance at Elena's launch was countersigned in my name, from the Battery, in the first minute of the climb, while I sat beside her with my hands in my lap.

I can't be sorry in the right order. So here it is in the order it happened.

Important thing first was hers. I'm putting it last. Ines is dead. I let her go. She asked me to say she was right about that, and she was, and so was I.

A.

He read it once for the shape and found it had none, only length. Seven years, in order.

Kaelin would be on the stool at the head of the row by now, in New York's heat, his shirt stuck to his back since nine. The post would find him there. Nothing on this station had ever been able to make anything arrive sooner. Nothing on it had ever been able to call anything back.

He sent it.
