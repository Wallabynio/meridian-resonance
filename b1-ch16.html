The convert

Fractured Horizons · II-B. The drift

2 JuneRachelNew York, the BatteryMars delay 4.1 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 4.1 min

At low tide the flood glass showed its seam.

Rachel had the straightedge from the plan room flat against the glass, a metre of steel, her cheek almost on it. Along the bottom of the pane, where the harbour had drawn back and left the weed hanging green and slack, the cable channel ran in its strip of old bronze. At the left end the steel touched it. At the right end she could get a fingernail in.

"Well?" said Joaquín.

"Two millimetres."

"He told you that on the stairs. Six floors of it, while you carried him."

"He was right." She stood back. Along the gallery rail the night shift gave her a slow round of applause. Somebody said, "Crooked since sixty-one," and somebody else said, "So's Joaquín."

Joaquín took the grease pencil off its string. Over the dead May bets he wrote JUNE, drew a line under it, and wrote REYES 2.40. "Storm season," he told the floor. "The pool is open. First surge of the season, height at the Battery gauge. My knees have been consulted."

"Your knees are sixty-one," said the night lead.

"My knees are a public service."

He held out the pencil. Rachel took it, and the map wall changed colour before she could write.

It came in along the south edge of the Shallows, one boardwalk at a time, a thin red ribbon drawn along the waterline, as if somebody had run a brush down the outside of the city.

"Foresight advisory," said the floor, at 06:51. "Director's authority. Priority two."

"Read it."

"Coastal surge, night tide, 3 June. One metre forty over the walk-line, south boardwalks, 23:10 to 01:30. Barrier holds. Footprint attached. Clearance plan attached."

Forty hours. Twenty-two streets of stilt houses, four hundred and six households to move up a deck or out, and the boats to be moved off the low moorings. A day's work for a floor that knew its job. The night lead was already calling the pump crews.

"Take it," Rachel said. "Desk plan as written. Crews on the boardwalks by noon tomorrow."

At 07:31 the weather service put its own bulletin into the queue. Coastal surge watch. 3 June, night tide. Monitor local advisories. It settled under Foresight's 06:51, while the floor was already moving boats.

Nobody looked up. The warnings had told the floor first, off the same relays that had told it about Broad Channel and the dome, and the floor had stopped finding that strange. Joaquín wrote a second height under his first. Rachel watched the two bulletins sit one above the other, and said nothing either.

She put the pencil back on its string without a bet. The sitting was at ten. It was the last one before the window, and she needed one of two things from it.

Three lines had come into the review's register since Saturday night, raw, under her own safeguards. Cobalt Basin. Medical status, P. Lind: unresponsive since the morning sweep, 28 May. Medical transfer, P. Lind: refused. Return berths allocated under the Lantern programme. Test pulse, 28 May, 21:00 station time: traces of P. Lind, S. Haug, B. Tran rose together.

She had read them on Sunday, and on Monday, and on the Walk last night with the pumps humming under her boots. A woman on Mars had stopped answering. Two others had stood up on a monitor at the same second as she had.

If the review couldn't say why, she would pull the stop she had written, and the window would close with the Lantern dark, and whatever the next warning would have seen would go unseen.

Clean, or the stop. Nothing in between went to Mars.

· · ·

She had moved the sitting to the Battery. Her room, her glass, the harbour pressing on it. Seven round the table, the orbital engineer with a flask of tea, the council's two with their slates, Foresight's two actuaries. Elena's chair was empty at three minutes to ten.

She came in at two minutes to, with the field still on her.

A Haven-issue shirt under the Spire black. Her hair was down. In twelve years Rachel had never seen it down in a room with a table in it. She crossed to her chair with long, careful steps and sat as if the chair had come up to meet her too fast, and laughed at herself, once, and put both hands on the table.

"Two weeks at a sixth," she said to the orbital engineer. "I weigh a tonne."

"Wait till you meet a staircase," said the engineer, who had done a year on the ring and still took the lift.

Rachel put the three lines on the glass behind her. The room read them. Nobody spoke, because everybody was waiting for the same person. Rachel sat perfectly still and let them wait.

In April Rachel had cleared a red flag under her own name to keep this woman at this table. She had never told her. She had never once regretted it, and she didn't now.

"Elena," Rachel said. "You've read page forty. You're the only one here who has. Is this the coupling?"

"No."

Rachel had her second question ready. She didn't get to it.

"Lind put a bare fingertip on the core on the third of March," Elena said. "Through a split glove. It's in her medical file, and the transfer request carried the file to us. That was a contact injury, two months before any sweep had a safeguard on it. She was hurt in March. She's still hurt. It's terrible, and it isn't the coupling."

One of the council's two lifted his stylus. "The berth refusal—"

"Is a scandal, and it isn't the coupling either." Her voice was level, helpful. "On the first of May Haug lost a word and Tran stopped to listen. Under your first safeguard they were taken out of the loop, and nobody in the loop has lost a word since. The stop works. I've read every reading since the safeguards came in."

Rachel had read more about Lind, in a post from Cobalt Basin the review had never been shown: a swell in her brain that rose on every sweep. She couldn't put it on this table without saying how she had read it.

"And the pulse."

"Three beds. Three different brains. One second." Elena turned her palm up on the table. "Brains don't keep time like that. A power bus does. The pulse went through the station, and every monitor on medical's bus jumped together."

"Was there a monitor on that bus with nobody in the bed?"

"Medical had three beds in use."

"So no control."

"No." Elena smiled at her. "It's a station on Mars, Rachel. They don't keep a bed empty for science."

The orbital engineer laughed into her tea. One of the council's two laughed with her, and then looked as though he wished he hadn't.

Rachel didn't laugh. In twelve years Elena had never made a joke in a review room. She had asked for the control first, every time, before anyone else in the room knew there should be one.

"I'd also like to withdraw my notes," Elena said.

She read them aloud from her slate. Engineering annex, objections: trace held at site, not shown to the review. And: Page 40, live coupling: precedent requested, not supplied.

"Page forty was your question," Rachel said.

"It was a question about a dead line." Elena's hands lay open on the table, palms down now, fingers spread on the wood. "This is a living one. I watched Haven's lattice take a sweep in April. Four keepers, twenty minutes, a woman on a steel lever. It held. I've seen what the Lantern couples to. I'd put my name on it."

Rachel looked at the hands. She had watched them for twelve years. When Elena was holding back, her hands found each other, one closed over the other, and Rachel had learned to wait for that, and to ask the second question when she saw it. They were open on the table, a hand's width apart, and they stayed there.

"And if it isn't the bus?" Rachel said.

"Then the stop catches it." Elena said it warmly, as if Rachel had asked to be reassured. "You wrote the stop. It's the best thing in the programme."

The room was looking at Rachel now. She felt her shoulders come down from where they'd been since Thursday.

"Motion," she said. "The review finds no cause to halt. Go-live on the fourth under the three safeguards, as written. The review recommends a medical berth home for P. Lind on the next available ship, programme reserve or none." She looked round the table. "Cobalt Basin's afternoon readings come in at five. If they're clean, I sign at six."

Seven ayes. Elena's was first.

At the door afterwards Elena touched her arm. She had never done that either.

"You did a good thing with those safeguards," she said. "Now go home and sleep. Sleep's only practice, you know."

She was gone down the corridor before Rachel could tell her that was rich, coming from her. Through the flood glass at the end of it Rachel saw Daniel Rocha get up from the bench in the lobby with Elena's bag over his shoulder, and Elena walk into his arms as if the sitting had taken a year.

· · ·

The call came at 15:12, on the crew handset, on pump house nine's channel.

Her board showed nothing. No caller. No line. Only one thing on the wall rang through nine and showed on nothing: the mesh box Daniel Rocha's crews had cable-tied to the pump house the summer the grid browned out over the Shallows, when it was the only way hers could talk along the wall. She had sent them a crate of beer and a letter. They had framed the letter.

Every call on her board was logged. This one wasn't on her board.

She took it.

"Rachel. It's Daniel."

"You're on nine."

"I'm on nine because this is about Elena, and Elena doesn't go on a Charter line." She could hear a street behind him, misters hissing, somebody's radio. "I won't keep you. I know what today is."

"She was very good this morning."

"I believe it." Then nothing for a moment. "She ran. At the field. Across the arrivals hall with her bag, in front of everyone, at Earth weight. She's never run to me in her life. She walks, and she gets there first."

"She's been on the Moon two weeks. People come back strange."

"In the pod she held my hand the whole way and looked at the wall out of the window like a tourist. She said the city was beautiful." His voice was plain, careful, a man reading off a list he'd made. "She sang. A song in Spanish. I've never once heard her sing. She asked me nothing. Not about my work, not about my mother. Two weeks, and nothing."

Rachel looked at the harbour on the glass. "She's happy."

"Yes."

"That's allowed, Daniel."

"Not overnight. Not her." The radio behind him changed songs. "You'd stand a crew down for less. One of yours came off a shift laughing and singing and saying yes to everything, you'd sit them in the van and look at their eyes."

"She isn't my crew."

"She's on your review." He let that sit. "What did they do to her up there? Did she go into the wall?"

It was the question. She had heard it asked in a hundred meetings, by nobody, in the silence after the slides. The review's scope had covered four keepers and a sweep. It had never once covered its observer.

"Not on my scope," she said.

The line hissed. Pump house nine ran under it, loud as ever.

"I didn't ask about your scope," Daniel said.

"She testified this morning. She was the best thing in the room."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

"She's tired, Daniel. She's earned a good day." Rachel heard her own voice and didn't like it, and went on anyway. "Let her have one."

She closed the line. The board still showed nothing.

In the review's register Luna Haven's channel sat two lines below Cobalt Basin's. She could ask the Ward. It would take a minute. She looked at it, and opened Cobalt Basin's instead, and waited for five o'clock.

· · ·

Cobalt Basin's afternoon readings came in at 17:03, raw. Five on the frame, all with their words. Haug and Tran out of the loop and stable. Lind unchanged. No stop.

At 17:58 Joaquín came up the gallery stairs with two coffees and put one by her hand. "You didn't bet," he said.

"I was busy."

"Pool closes when the gauge moves. Tomorrow night." He looked at the motion on her screen, and then at her. "Clean?"

"Clean."

"Bueno," he said, and meant it, and went back down to his boats.

At 18:00 Rachel signed. R. Iverson, chair. The minutes went out at 18:04 to the Signatories, the council, Beijing Command, Cobalt Basin and Luna Haven, every party to the Lantern, with the motion and the seven ayes and the berth recommendation. It would cost the Signatories nothing to ignore.

Behind her the floor was changing shifts. The south boardwalks had gone amber on the map wall, the boats coming off the low moorings one by one. On the glass Joaquín's two heights stood alone under JUNE.

At 18:49 a message came into her queue from Luna Haven, the Rows. M. Ekeberg.

Read your minutes with everyone else. Your observer was on my wall on the 31st. One session, ten minutes, my scope, signed. She broke the exit, and I pulled her. Here's the Ward's report, unedited. You told me nothing in secret.

The report was one page.

Ward after-report. E. Vázquez, observer, safety review. One session, 31 May. On the coupling: ten minutes, twenty seconds. Exit: keeper's hand.

Under it, the Ward's scores, from the exit and every six hours after, nought to ten. Fog: four, three, two, two. Sleep: none, broken. After-images: six, five, four, four.

Emotional bleed. Nine. Ten. Then the column stopped holding numbers. Above scale, in the medic's hand, twice running, and beside the last one: Laughing in her sleep. Reports rain. Discharged at own request, 1 June, 06:10.

At the foot Mara had added one line more. For scale: the worst of my four in April peaked at six.

"Drown it," Rachel said, to nobody.

She read it twice. The minutes had gone to every party an hour ago, Cobalt Basin and Haven and Beijing among them, with her name at the foot, and nothing she could send now would get there first. She opened her board to call Daniel back, and there was nothing on it to call.
