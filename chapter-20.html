The sea was three and a half metres higher than the moon said it should be, and had been since Thursday.

Sienna TeAriki stood at the launch-coordination floor's main display, high in the upper systems of the retasked Opera House, and watched the anomaly's footprint update on the shelf map: a region of ocean two hundred kilometres off the coast, roughly the size of a small county, where gravity had stopped agreeing with the almanac. Nothing about it photographed well. It was simply a patch of the Coral Sea standing above its own predicted tide, drifting south along the shelf at under walking pace, dragging a surge ahead of it that arrived at each stretch of coast in turn like a bill nobody remembered running up.

Seven days since a research chamber on Luna Haven had opened forty-two seconds of uncontrolled aperture, and this was what an aftershock looked like when it finally came down the gravity well: not fire, not fracture. Tides that had stopped keeping their appointments.

"Rothwell Terrace is loaded," the floor system said. The Orbital Station's voice had the register every Lunar Accord installation kept for the people who worked inside it — warm, unhurried, a colleague who happened to be a building. "Eighty-one aboard, six wheelchairs, one incubator on independent power. The harbourmaster is asking for a departure window."

"Give her the window and hold the two cargo lifts behind her." Sienna pulled the manifest queue across the display with two fingers and reordered it the way she had been reordering it all morning — medical dependency first, then the unrebuilt margins, then everything that could technically walk uphill on its own. The protocol ranked settlements by population and asset value. The protocol had been written for a world where the sea obeyed the moon. "Cargo can be late. Dialysis can't."

"Reordered. For the record, that's the ninth deviation from the standing evacuation protocol today."

"Log all nine. When this is over, somebody should rewrite the protocol, and they'll want the list."

Below the coordination floor's long window, the Hub went about its own version of the morning. The grown lattice of the concourse roof caught the early light along its ribs, translucent as a leaf where the sun came through; the harbour underneath it was all ordered motion, transport skiffs stacking at the water-side platforms in queues that had been lengthening for three days. And past the breakwater, low and grey, ran the line of coast nobody had ever rebuilt — the drowned suburbs left as the sea's margin two generations ago, whose only mercy this week was that there was nothing left in them to evacuate.

The physics, Sienna could work with. The physics was almost restful. You measured the surge, you modelled the drift, you moved people and pumps and generator barges ahead of it, and the sea did what the sea was going to do regardless of what anyone broadcast about it. It was the other load on the system that had no almanac.

"Show me compliance," she said.

The coastal map re-drew itself, and there it was again, the pattern she had first seen two days ago and had been hoping, stubbornly and against evidence, might improve: green along most of the threatened coast, and then holes. Whole townships amber or red. Boarding rates at forty percent, thirty, in communities that had drilled coastal evacuation every year since the seawalls went in and could normally clear themselves in six hours flat.

The holes did not map to the surge. They mapped, almost precinct for precinct, to where last week's broadcast had run widest — an hour of practised gravity out of Beijing Command and the Corporate Union's media architecture, explaining that Luna Haven's reckless experiments had broken something fundamental, that official channels were minimising it, that a sensible family's safest move was to distrust exactly the voices now telling them to leave their homes. The broadcast had needed six days to hollow out the coast's confidence. The sea needed two more to arrive. The story had a head start on the water.

And the maddening thing — the thing Sienna had stopped saying aloud in briefings — was that the broadcast was not entirely lying. Something had gone wrong on Luna Haven; the surge outside was its signature. A lie built on a true foundation didn't crack when you threw facts at it. It settled deeper. So she had stopped throwing facts at it.

"Get me Tarnleigh Flats again," she said. "Not the emergency band. The oyster co-op channel, the one the wardens actually answer."

A pause, a soft chime. The voice that came on was salt-roughened and openly suspicious, and Sienna talked to it the way she had talked to harbourmasters, wardens, and one furious lay preacher all week: no bulletins, nothing that sounded like a broadcast. Just the surge model, plainly. The tide gauge at their own jetty, which nobody in orbit could edit. Transports crewed by people whose names the wardens already knew.

"You're asking us to trust the same government the broadcast says is lying," the warden said.

"I'm asking you to trust your own jetty," Sienna said. "Walk down at slack water and look at the marker. If it reads under two metres, tell me I'm a liar and keep your boats. If it reads over, I'll have four transports at the co-op ramp by dark, and your own people can crew the boarding lists."

Silence on the channel, the particular working silence of someone doing arithmetic they didn't want to do. "It read two-sixty at dawn," the warden said at last. "Send the transports."

It went in the log as Tarnleigh Flats: amber to green. It had cost eleven minutes and it was one township of forty. She was reaching for the next one when the floor system spoke again, gently, the way it flagged things it knew were unwelcome.

"Satellite window closing. The Kestrel constellation loses the southern coast in about an hour — the anomaly's lensing is pushing three more birds out of usable geometry. Surface relays will carry local traffic, but orbital coordination over the evacuation zone is going to get thin."

"Define thin."

"Gaps up to forty minutes. During which you'll be routing blind."

Sienna looked at the queue — thirty-nine townships, eleven amber, four red, one incubator on independent power somewhere over the water between Rothwell Terrace and the Hub — and felt the day arrange itself into its true shape. This was the job. Not the drama of the wave; the arithmetic in front of it. Somewhere overhead, in a colony she had never visited, people were doing whatever people did with dimensional apertures and the fabric of things. Down here was where it all eventually landed, on jetties and boarding ramps and channels that had to stay open, and down here it was hers.

"Then we don't route blind," she said. "Pull me every skiff that's finished its run and isn't needed for lift. We're going to anchor them down the coast as a relay picket — line of sight, boat to boat, all the way past the shelf. If we can't have satellites, we'll build the coverage out of the evacuation itself."

"That's not in the protocol either," the system said, warmly.

"Log it," Sienna said. "Number ten."

The seam had grown four centimetres overnight, and this morning, for the first time, it had a sound.

Sarin Yelena crouched at the edge of the Lattice Threshold with her equipment cases still shut beside her and listened to it: a thin, arrhythmic note under the Basin wind, less like tearing than like a stringed instrument being detuned very slowly. In front of her, the boundary substrate — the living interface tissue where the Preserve's reality was worked and held against the ordinary vacuum-and-rock fact of Mars — ran in low, banked ridges the colour of old copper, and across one of those ridges lay the seam. A hairline of wrongness, half a metre long a month ago. Nearly two metres now. It didn't glow, or gape. The substrate along its length had simply gone grey and silent, the way skin goes around a wound that has stopped being local.

Thirty-one days she had been coming out here every morning — a month of Preserve time, six days on Luna Haven's clocks, the outside world's single bad week stretched into a slow interior season — and every morning she had told herself she was monitoring it. That was the word in her field notes. Monitoring. The way you monitor a patient, walking to their room first thing whether or not the instruments have called you, because the instruments do not love them.

"Say the migration numbers again," she said to the air.

"Nothing's used the corridor in four days." Kaelin's voice, on the field link from the Empathy Flats, two hundred metres out past the Threshold's low rise, where he'd set up his monitoring station at dawn without discussing it with her, which was its own kind of statement. "Not the grazers, not the burrowers, not even the carrion feeders, and there's a carcass in the corridor they'd normally have cleared in a day. The avoidance radius is growing about nine metres per day and it's centred on your seam. Sarin, the ecology isn't just avoiding damage. It's quarantining. The Preserve is getting ready to treat this whole margin as compromised tissue."

"And if it does?"

"Then it walls it off." A pause; she could hear him choosing the words with a biologist's care. "That's what an immune system does with what it can't heal. It stops trying to heal it. The Threshold, the corridor — possibly the Flats. Sealed out of the Preserve's regard. I don't know what that looks like physically and I badly don't want to find out."

Sarin opened the first equipment case, because her hands wanted a task while the rest of her caught up with what it had apparently already decided. The braces sat in their moulded recesses: her four field attempts at holding the seam mechanically, each engineered better than the last, each rejected the same way. Not violently. The substrate simply withdrew from every clamp and pin and printed graft, went grey a centimetre further around every contact point, and the seam went on lengthening as though the intervention were itself a small new injury. Which, she had finally admitted somewhere around day twenty, it was. The boundary wasn't a structure. It was a relationship. You could not clamp a relationship shut. The substrate could tell, all the way down at whatever level such things were decided, the difference between something imposed on it and something offered to it.

She had read Léa Fournier's containment note from Luna Haven — a month ago, her time; days ago, Léa's — about the cascade fragments in their isolation fields, retracting when the room was frightened, reaching when the room was calm. Dead tissue at interplanetary remove, still responding to the interior states of the people near it. She had thought about very little else since.

The second case held the interface rig. Filament leads, induction collar, the field transceiver she'd built to log what her own nervous system did during integration tests. The lattice-work itself she carried everywhere, because it was in her: fine neural-interface filaments seeded at her temples and down both forearms, faint silver against the skin, months of careful self-experimentation ahead of any protocol that would have authorised it. Designed to let a nervous system and the Preserve's substrate hold a conversation. Tested in shallow, cautious touches at the Threshold's healthy margins — ten seconds, thirty, a minute — never under load, never in anger, never at a wound.

"Kaelin," she said. "The seam doesn't need a brace. It needs an interface. Something the boundary can close against that isn't an object. Something with an interior state it can read."

The link was quiet for a moment. "You're describing a living anchor."

"I'm describing the only category of thing it hasn't rejected."

"You're describing yourself," Kaelin said, and now his voice had dropped into the flat, careful register of a man trying not to spook something. "Sarin, your longest integration is seventy-one seconds, at a healthy margin, with cutoffs. You have no data on contact with compromised substrate. No model of the load. If the seam reads you the way it reads everything else that's touched it—"

"It won't. Everything else that's touched it was a tool. Tools are coercion with good manners. I'm not going to hold it shut, Kaelin, I'm going to give it an edge to heal against — that's the entire design philosophy of the lattice, it's what I built it for, I just thought I'd have another year of tests before—" She stopped. Below her hands, along the seam's grey margin, the detuned-string sound climbed a fraction, and thirty-one mornings of monitoring assembled themselves, all at once, into what they had actually been. Not caution. Rehearsal.

"Before someone had to," she finished, quietly.

"Come out to the Flats," Kaelin said. "We'll model it tonight. Two days, proper cutoffs, I'll wire the telemetry myself—"

"The avoidance radius grows nine metres a day and the seam found its voice this morning." She was already seating the induction collar at the back of her neck, her calloused fingers running the checks in the order she'd drilled until the order didn't need her. "You are the telemetry. Log everything."

"Sarin—"

She knelt at the seam, laid both forearms along the grey margin of the boundary substrate, skin to interface, the silver filament lines warming as they woke, and did the one thing no brace, clamp, pin, or graft could ever have done.

She asked.

The blind window arrived at 14:20 and it lasted thirty-eight minutes, and afterwards, in the debrief record, it would look like nothing at all: no transports lost, no channels dropped, coverage sustained. That was the point of the work. Done right, it produced a historical record in which nothing appeared to have happened.

Inside the thirty-eight minutes it was eleven skiffs riding anchor in a swell their hulls had never been rated for, holding line-of-sight in a chain down two hundred kilometres of coast; a relay tech on the outermost boat reading signal strength off a handheld while her skipper quartered the waves to keep the mast inside tolerance; the Rothwell Terrace transport, incubator aboard, threading its last leg on bearings passed boat to boat down the picket like a bucket down a fire line. Sienna did not sit down once, routing every craft on the southern coast through a coverage map that existed only because she had improvised it out of the evacuation's own hardware three hours earlier.

At 14:58 the Kestrel constellation cleared the anomaly's lensing and the map bloomed back to full coverage, and the floor system said, in its warm and unhurried way, "Orbital coordination restored. All eleven picket craft report ready to resume lift duty. Rothwell Terrace transport is on final approach — the incubator's telemetry is green."

Sienna let out a breath and looked at her hands, which had been perfectly steady all afternoon and had decided, now that it didn't matter, to shake slightly.

"Route the picket crews' names to my personal file," she said. "All of them. And get me Tarnleigh Flats — their transports load at dusk, and I want the wardens hearing a voice they know."

The first thing the seam did was read her.

There was no other word for it, afterwards. The moment the filaments engaged the compromised substrate, the conversation her lattice had been designed for opened in both directions at once, and something on the other side of the wound turned its attention through her the way a doctor palpates an unfamiliar joint — systematically, without malice, checking whether this new arrival was another clamp wearing a cleverer disguise. She felt her own heartbeat come back to her from outside her body, rendered in the substrate's slow copper registers. She felt the seam's wrongness directly, not as pain but as discord — the detuned string running through her own nerves now, looking for something in her willing to be detuned along with it.

And it found her fear, because of course it did, the fear was enormous — and the moment it found the fear, the seam widened.

She felt it go, a cold half-centimetre, the grey margin creeping, and understood in one appalling instant the entire mechanism: the boundary reflected the interior states offered to it. Fear read as retraction; retraction read as abandonment. She could not fight the fear down — the substrate would read the suppression as one more clamp. And panic performed as calm would be hollower still, and hollowness was the one thing she suspected this place read fastest of all.

So she did the only thing left, which was to stop performing anything.

She let the fear stand — hers, real, admitted — and set beside it, with the same honesty, everything else that was also true. Thirty-one mornings walking out to this ridge before the light. The braces she had engineered and mourned. The filaments she had seeded into her own arms because she believed, before she could prove it, that a body could be a bridge. Kaelin's voice on the link, counting her vitals aloud, steady as a metronome. The corridor she wanted the grazers to come back to. It was nothing like calm. It was presence — frightened, specific, entirely hers — offered to the wound whole, nothing held back and nothing performed.

The seam stopped widening.

Then, with the humility of tissue rather than the drama of machinery, it began to close against her — knitting along the line of her forearms as though her nervous system were the healthy edge it had been unable to find for a month, discord resolving toward her pulse a fraction of a tone at a time. She held. There was enormous load in it, more than any model would have cleared, her whole body conscripted as boundary; she held anyway, and the holding was not gripping but staying — the way you stay with a frightened patient, the way a jetty marker stays while the tide reads itself against it.

Kaelin's voice said nine minutes at some point, from very far away. It said other things after that. She lost the words and kept the metronome.

When the discord finally faded — not to silence, but to tune — the substrate released her so gently that she had no memory of the release at all, and none of crossing two hundred metres of open ground. That was the part no debrief would ever fully hold: she came back to herself on her hands and knees on the Empathy Flats, copper dust under her palms, the Basin's evening light going long and red, the distance between herself and the seam already behind her like a corridor the Preserve had quietly closed.

Boots, arriving fast. A field kit going down open beside her. Kaelin's hands at her collar, her pulse, her temples, moving through the assessment sequence with a fieldworker's economy while his breath came like a man who had run every metre of it.

"Eleven minutes," he said. His voice was wrecked and even. "Full integration under load, eleven minutes. Your filaments are running forty-one degrees. Can you feel your hands?"

"Yes." Her voice came out rusty, as though it had been away somewhere. Beneath her spread fingers the Flats' substrate lay faintly, unmistakably warm, and for half a breath — she would not put this in the notes either — the warmth had a quality of attention, the way a room has a different silence when someone in it knows your step. "The seam?"

"Closed. The margin readings went green four minutes ago, the whole length of it at once. The corridor—" He stopped, and she followed his gaze out across the plain, to where three grazers, unhurried, incurious, entirely ordinary, had walked back into the avoidance zone as though it had never existed and dropped their heads to feed.

Kaelin looked back at her, and she watched him arrive at the thing underneath the vitals, the thing his hands had been too disciplined to ask first.

"Do you know what you just did?" he said. "What you proved?"

Sarin sat back on her heels on the warm ground of the Empathy Flats, filaments cooling in her forearms, the first bio-technical lattice anchor in human history steadying her own breath in the red light, and gave him the only answer that was honest, and the only one either of their fields could build on.

"No," she said. "Help me find out."
