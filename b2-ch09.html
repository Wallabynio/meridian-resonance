The Narrow Gate · Chapter 09

Residual

The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

09

22 MarchRachelthe BatteryMars delay 18.0 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 18.0 min

The street hit thirty-four at ten past one, and the night lead put it on the wall.

She didn't announce it. She put the figure up in the corner of the map wall, white over the harbour, and went back to her clocks, and the whole floor turned to look at chair four.

Joaquín lay back in it with his eyes open, held over from the morning block. He had the camel coat on.

"It's March," he said.

"It's thirty-four," said Moss, from chair three.

"In March it doesn't count. In March it's an accident."

"Rule's the rule," said the night lead.

He sat up, sighed like a man at his own wake, and took the coat off.

"Three in a year," said the night lead. "He kept it on for twelve." The floor applauded. On the gallery behind the coffee machine seven children applauded too, because everyone else was, and one of them whistled through his fingers.

The schools had shut at noon. Every classroom in the city, on the heat rule. Last year it had been the seventeenth. Nobody on the floor called that late. Half the floor had nobody at home to leave a child with. Rachel had let them bring the children down on her name, and now there were crayons on the rail and seven faces against the flood glass, watching the silver fish hang in the green, all facing one way.

"Iverson." Moss had her eyes shut already, reading a sky. "Second front's in. Protons up two orders since breakfast. A sail on the ring just went dark."

Rachel was at the rail before the plot had changed colour. The floor smelled of coffee and crayon and the ozone off the chairs, and of seven children who had walked from the trains at noon.

The ring turned on the wall, a belt of tagged points round a blue disc. One tag had gone from white to amber. A power satellite, three kilometres of black sail, its controllers cooked by the storm at 12:52. It still caught the Sun. It had stopped knowing where the Sun was.

It was yawing. Slowly, one long arm of sail coming round like the hand of a clock, and in sixteen minutes the hand would cross a tether.

"Malé," said the night lead. "Tether station. Short cargo arm, hub crew of six."

"Where are they?"

"Behind their water. Since the watch went red at eleven."

"Can they cut loose?"

"From outside. Hands on the release." The night lead didn't look up from the clocks. "Go out in that and they're dosed out by tea. Two of them by the time they've found the bolt."

Six. Rachel took the pencil off its string and wrote MALÉ 6 on the inside of her wrist, under the tide times.

"One of its four thruster pods still answers," Dev said from chair two. He was counting before anyone had asked him to. "Pod C. Forty per cent. We fly it on that."

"Fly it," said Rachel.

The rota said the floor changed chairs on the hour. At one the night lead had held the change for the storm, and the morning block was still lying back. It was 13:14.

Aur'k stood at the head of the row with his feet set wide. He had no clock over him. Over each of the four chairs hung two numbers, the sail's time to the crossing and the operator's own bleed clock, counting down to the minute that took you out whatever you were holding. Over the reader, nothing. You're the clock. She had said it to him in February and meant it as a fact.

"Lin has attitude," he said. "Joaquín has the pod. Dev has the crossing. Moss has the air. The storm is swelling the air under the sail. It is a sail."

"Funny," said Moss, eyes shut. "I'm laughing inside."

Joaquín put the first burn in. "Pod C. Four seconds at thirteen sixteen. Cuatro." Flat operator's voice, with his street still in it.

On the plot the sail's yaw slowed by a hair. Lin held the nod out of it with her shoulders. The children had stopped drawing.

The band from Malé had gone to hiss at 12:58, with that whole arc of the ring. The storm was in the radio too. Six people behind their water, and nobody on the floor would hear a word from them until it passed.

"Crossing at thirteen thirty-one and eight seconds," said Dev. "Arm's over the tether at thirty-one and six. I need ten metres. I need a hundred."

"Burn two," said Joaquín. "Six seconds at thirteen twenty."

"Lin," said the night lead.

"I have it." Lin's clock went from thirty-one to twenty-four.

Aur'k's helm turned to chair one and held there. Rachel watched him read her: a man looking at a woman's shoulders and seeing where the weight sat. "Chair one, edge holding," he said. "Lift at thirteen forty."

"Moss," said Rachel. "What's the air doing?"

"Gusting. Air that wasn't up there at breakfast. Don't ask me how, I'm too busy being right." Moss's mouth tightened. "Next push at twenty-seven. It'll swing the sail back toward them by about what Joaquín just bought."

"Then buy it twice," Rachel said.

Joaquín bought it twice. Two burns, eight seconds, the pod at forty per cent shaking on its mounts, and Lin's clock went to seventeen, and Aur'k didn't lift her. At 13:27 the swollen air leaned on the sail and the sail leaned back toward Malé, and held, and didn't come any further.

"Arm's over the tether at thirty-one and nineteen," said Dev. "Crossing at eight. Eleven seconds. Four hundred metres." And again, as if it might change: "Four hundred."

After that it was waiting. At 13:30 the sail's tag dropped into the storm's noise, and the plot showed only the forecast: a thin grey line, coming round past where the tether should be. Nothing came back to say it had.

"Forecast clear," Moss said, and opened her eyes. "That's all I've got."

Nobody cheered. Somebody at the back started to, and stopped. The children looked at the grown-ups for what to do, and then went back to their crayons.

A girl of about seven had come down off the gallery to stand at Rachel's elbow. She looked at the plot, and then at Rachel.

"Were they real?"

"Six of them."

"Are they all right?"

"We'll know tonight," Rachel said.

The girl nodded as if that settled it and went back up to her crayons.

At 13:40 Aur'k lifted Lin out on the minute he had called, and the held block came out after her. Dev, then Moss, then Joaquín last, one hand on the chair's arm, asking the medic where his coat was. Aur'k stood at the head of the row while the new shift lay back, and set his feet again, and didn't sit down.

A hundred and thirty-three days. Rachel wrote it under MALÉ 6 and pulled her cuff over both.

· · ·

By dusk the Walk was full.

The heat had gone out of the stone by a degree or two and no further. People came up the stairs from the Polder with blankets and bottles and lay on the long park along the top of the wall for the sea wind, which came off the harbour warm and smelling of weed and warm stone and did nothing, and they lay in it anyway. Children ran on the grass in their school clothes. Below the parapet the harbour had gone the colour of old brass as the light went, and the pump house hummed up through the granite.

Kaelin Drake was on a bench beside it with a clinic stick across his knees.

She knew him from the post: the hot coast in the vowels, a man talking over someone else's breathing. She hadn't known he was this big, or that Earth had taken so much off him. He sat like old crew at the end of a shift, as if the bench had been put there for him to survive.

On the street below the wall a pod waited at the kerb. Its strip read DRAKE, K. over CIVIC — BATTERY.

"Director Iverson," he said.

"Mr Drake."

"Your pod's been keeping my pace since the vac." He nodded down at it. "I thought I'd save it the stairs."

She didn't sit. "You came to my wall."

"You come up most nights. Your floor says so. It's a chatty floor." He held out a handheld. "Look first. Then tell me to go."

On the screen was a photograph of a page. Pencil, square capitals, the page ruled by somebody who didn't trust the lines that came with it. Five lines.

LIN Y. 14. RAMAN D. 9. MOSS A. 11. REYES J. 8. AUR'K, READER. 40.

Under each, a stamp. The night of 14 March, logoff by logoff, against the floor's own rota.

"Minutes," Kaelin said. "After they go home. Their frames go on. Small corrections, each in its own shape. She can tell Lin's from Raman's, like handwriting." He touched the last line with a thick finger. "His runs forty. For a man who's at home making me tea."

Rachel looked at the page. The wind came off the harbour and didn't cool anything.

"This came off my floor."

"Off a ring relay. Your frames go up to the ring like everybody's."

"Elena Vázquez."

"I didn't say."

"You didn't need to." She gave him back the handheld. "It runs down."

"For forty minutes."

"It runs down."

"She said you'd say that." He turned the handheld face down on his knee. "She sent it to me for him. I promised him on a beach I'd stop asking what that floor is. So I'm not asking him."

"You're asking me."

"I'm asking you to take him off it."

"No."

He waited for the rest. She didn't give it to him.

"What's it for?" he said. "The floor. The contract that runs to a date."

"I can't tell you."

"So does he." He nearly smiled. "You two should get a sign made."

Up the Walk somebody had a speaker going, a song from before the wall, and a couple were dancing to it badly on the grass. Rachel watched them for two bars.

"She's against the Gate," she said. "She told me so in my own room. Against it, and against me."

"She told me too." Kaelin didn't raise his voice. "It doesn't change the minutes."

"It changes who's counting them."

"Then you count them." He shifted on the bench, and his knees complained, and he let them. "Your logs. Your people. If it's nothing, show me nothing, and I'll go home and eat his cooking and never say the word frame again."

"Where's the raw?"

"On a hundred roofs."

Of course it was. She had watched a city's worth of files come off a hundred roofs once, in pieces, faster than anyone could call them back. Cut small and passed along a valley. Nobody holding the whole of it.

And on her desk for a week, a line off a Continuity water authority's beacon outside São Paulo, from a town the relays don't serve. KEEPER: ROCHA, D. — DOOR OPENED 23:04. DOOR CLOSED 23:58. She had read it twice and not known what it was for. A reservoir controller's door opened on a keeper's key, one night, for fifty-four minutes. Its uplink followed the relays on its own. Now she knew what it was for.

"I'll audit the floor," she said. "Tonight. On our logs."

"Not hers?"

"Hers came off a water controller on a keeper's key. The minute it's in my record, somebody in a collar asks whose key." She looked at him. "So it's not in my record."

Kaelin looked back at her a long moment.

"You're keeping her out of it."

"I'm keeping my record clean."

He seemed to weigh whether those were the same thing, and to decide he wasn't going to get an answer.

"One more," he said. "Don't tell him I came."

"He won't hear it from me."

It came out easily. She heard how easily.

Above the harbour the brass had gone out of the sky, and high up, over the towers, the storm was showing: a faint green ribbon at the top of the dark, faint, coming and going. People on the grass pointed. Kaelin looked up at it, and his free hand went to his chest, to the place where a dosimeter used to clip.

"Still on," he said.

Her slate chimed. MALÉ. SIX OF SIX. FULL AIR. She read it twice.

"Six people," she said. "Behind their water since eleven. They're out."

"Good." He got the stick under him and stood in two stages. "That's good."

She was already calling the floor before he reached the stairs.

"Get me the Spire's engineer," she said. "And every chair's logs since November."

· · ·

On the glass by her door somebody had rubbed out 446 and written 452, and drawn a small sail under it, edge-on.

Her room was hotter than the floor. It always was. The cooling never reached the end of the glass, and tonight it had quit trying. The room smelled of warm paper and the coffee gone cold in her cup since six. Rachel sat at the desk in her shirtsleeves with her jacket on the door and the handheld in its pocket, face down, and the Spire's engineer sat across from her in his Spire black with his case at his feet, unopened.

He had kept the Lantern's clock on go-live night. Now he kept the Gate's. He was young enough that he still looked surprised when the numbers agreed with him.

"Every logoff since the fourteenth of November," he said. "Every chair. The reader too. Off the line's own clock."

"And?"

"And they persist." He turned the slate toward her. "After logoff the operator model goes on emitting. Small. Corrections at the edges. It decays."

It was a table. A row for each chair, the name, the peak after logoff in parts per thousand of the shift, and a last column headed CLASS.

LIN Y. 0.31. RESIDUAL. RAMAN D. 0.22. RESIDUAL. MOSS A. 0.27. RESIDUAL. REYES J. 0.19. RESIDUAL. AUR'K, READER. 0.04. RESIDUAL.

At the foot, a line: Threshold for RESIDUAL: 1.00 per thousand.

"Who drew the line at one?"

"The models." He said it as if it answered the question. "It's their noise floor. Below that, a model can't tell a pattern from the room it's in."

"So it decides what it can't hear."

"Every gauge does, Director. That's calibration."

She looked at the last row. Point oh four. The faintest on the floor by a distance. On Kaelin's handheld, in somebody's pencilled capitals, the same man had run forty minutes.

"This is height," she said. "How loud. Where's length?"

"There's no field for length. It's a level audit." He frowned, as if he had noticed it himself only now. "I could build one. A week. Ten days."

"The board sits at nine."

"Then not by nine."

"Drown it," she said, to the table, not to him.

She looked at the table. Four names she knew by their kids and their coffee and their bets in the pool, and a fifth who had no clock over him because she had said he didn't need one.

A week. In a week the board would have seen everything she saw, because she had promised it would. Wáng would have seen it. The floor would be steering the ring through a storm season with an audit open on its own chairs and no answer in it, and every chair in the row would know.

A thing under a line nobody would ever look at. Against six people behind their water, and a girl asking whether they were real.

"Print it," she said.

He printed it. She took a pen out of the drawer, not the pencil, and under the last row, in her own hand, she wrote RESIDUAL. Audited 22 March. R. Iverson, civic director.

It looked like every flood order she had ever put her name to.

When he had gone she sent one line to Kaelin Drake. It went out on the floor's routing, under her name, into her own log, and she saw that as it went, and let it. Audited. Residual. Iverson. Then she took the pencil off its string and wrote 40 on the inside of her wrist, under 133, and pulled her cuff down over it.

· · ·

The board sat in the old review room at nine, under the long glass, with the harbour black behind it and the wall's lamps laying a green stripe across the table.

Three seats. Hers, at the head of the table. One for the Mandate, which tonight was a screen at the far end: Wáng Sù in Beijing's morning, a white window behind him, the lamp off, a glass of water at his elbow, full. The third seat was empty. Nobody had moved its chair since February. Rachel had not asked anyone to.

"The Malé save," she said. "Six crew, a tether station, a power satellite flown off it on one pod. Four hundred metres. Six of six." She laid the printout on the table, though he was a screen. "And an audit. Every chair's logoff since November. Patterns persist after logoff, at low levels. All below the threshold. I've classed it residual."

"Thank you, Director." Wáng read it. She watched his eyes go down the page on his own screen, a line at a time, to the end, and back up to the threshold, and stop there. He moved his glass a centimetre, and didn't drink.

"Residual," he said. "It means what remains."

"It means below the line."

"Yes. And the line is the system's own." He folded his hands. "I do not say that as a criticism. A system that knows its own noise is a good system. I would rather a careful system than a confident one." He looked at the empty chair, then at her. "Six tonight. Four hundred and fifty-two since November, by your floor's count. I have counted them."

"I know you have."

"Then I will say what I came to say, while the count is in front of us." He let the room hear the end of that before he went on. "On the third of August the ring will still be over this city. So will the storms that take pieces off it, and the summer, and every summer after. The second of August is a day. The sky is not a day."

The pump under the floor hummed through the soles of her shoes. In the glass the harbour held the room's reflection: three chairs, one of them empty, and a woman in shirtsleeves with her cuff pulled down.

"I do not ask to open it wider, Director," said Wáng. "I ask that on the third of August, nobody closes it."
