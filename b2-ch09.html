The Narrow Gate · Chapter 09

Residual

The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

09

22 MarchRachelthe BatteryMars delay 18.0 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 18.0 min

The street hit thirty-four at twenty to one, and the night lead put it on the wall.

She didn't announce it. She put the figure up in the corner of the map wall, white over the harbour, and went back to her clocks, and the whole floor turned to look at chair four.

Joaquín lay back in it with his eyes shut, an hour into the morning block. He had the camel coat on.

"It's March," he said, without opening them.

"It's thirty-four," said Moss, from chair three.

"In March it doesn't count. In March it's an accident."

"Rule's the rule," said the night lead.

He sat up out of the link for the length of a sigh, a man at his own wake, and shrugged the coat off onto the rail.

"Three in a year," said the night lead. "He kept it on for twelve." The floor applauded. On the gallery behind the coffee machine seven children applauded too, because everyone else was, and one of them whistled through his fingers.

The schools had shut at noon. Every classroom in the city, on the heat rule. Last year it had been the seventeenth. Rachel had let the floor bring them down on her name, and now there were crayons on the rail and seven faces against the flood glass, watching the silver fish hang in the green, all facing one way.

"Iverson." Moss had her eyes shut again, reading a sky. "Second front's in. Protons up two orders since breakfast."

At 12:58 the morning block came out on the rota. Lin, then Dev, then Moss. Joaquín last, one hand on the chair's arm, asking the medic where his coat was. At 13:00 the afternoon four lay back in the warm chairs and the medic started their clocks. Four minutes to couple a block. It always took four.

At 13:01 a tag on the ring went from white to amber.

"Power satellite," said the night lead. "Controllers cooked. It's yawing."

Rachel was at the rail before the plot had changed colour. Three kilometres of black sail, still catching the Sun, no longer knowing where the Sun was. One long arm of it coming round like the hand of a clock.

"Malé," said the night lead. "Tether station. Short cargo arm, hub crew of six. Behind their water since eleven."

"Can they cut loose?"

"From outside. Hands on the release. Go out in that and they're dosed out by tea."

"Who's coupled?"

"Nobody." The night lead didn't look up. "Three minutes. And the only way to that sail is through Malé's relay, which has been hiss since twelve fifty. It clears the pole's noise in forty seconds, for seventy. That's the window."

Seventy seconds of open band and the next block three minutes from the link. Rachel did it in her head and didn't like any of the answers. "Drown it," she said. She took the pencil off its string and wrote MALÉ 6 on the inside of her wrist, under the tide times.

The band opened at 13:02. Telemetry came down off the sail like hail on a roof, and the plot filled with it.

"One pod still answers," said the night lead. "Pod C. Forty per cent."

Nobody touched it.

On the wall, the sail's first burn went in by itself.

POD C. 4 S. 13:02:19. The floor's log printed every call in the operator's own shorthand. Under the burn, in the column where a name went, it printed CUATRO.

Rachel looked at chair four.

Joaquín was still in it, logged off, his clock dark, his coat over his knees. His eyes were open. He was looking at the ceiling with the face of a man listening to a radio in another room.

POD C. 6 S. 13:02:41. CINCO SEIS.

"Who's flying that?" said somebody at the back.

The sail's yaw slowed. Stopped. Came back a hair. At 13:03:12 the band closed into the storm's noise again, and the plot showed only the forecast: a thin grey line, coming round past where the tether should be, by a margin it gave in round numbers. Nothing came back to say it had.

Somebody started to cheer, and stopped. The children looked at the grown-ups for what to do, and then went back to their crayons.

At the head of the row Aur'k had turned his helm, slowly, from the afternoon chairs to chair four, and held it there.

A girl of about seven had come down off the gallery to stand at Rachel's elbow. She looked at the plot, and then at Rachel.

"Were they real?"

"Six of them."

"Are they all right?"

"We'll know tonight," Rachel said.

The girl nodded as if that settled it and went back up to her crayons.

The director's channel had a single earpiece, and Rachel wore it on the left. It carried the floor's alarms and nothing else, and she had stopped hearing it in December. At 13:04, with the afternoon block coming up into the link and the medic calling their clocks, it opened.

A child, close, a little hoarse, as if she had been shouting for a long time somewhere else.

"I waited," the child said. "Like you said."

Rachel didn't move.

"You came back for them. Thank you."

It closed. The floor went on. The afternoon four came up one by one, and the medic said coupled four times, and Aur'k set his feet.

Wall bars. A gym floor's varnish under her knees. A list, a dead radio, a girl called Noor and her brother, and Rachel's own voice, twenty-three, saying Wait here, and the next name. She had not come back to the wall bars that night. Noor was one of the nine.

She looked at the channel's log on her slate. 13:04. CARRIER. NO TRAFFIC.

She told nobody. A hundred and thirty-three days. She wrote it under MALÉ 6 and pulled her cuff over both.

· · ·

By dusk the Walk was full.

The heat had gone out of the stone by a degree or two and no further. People came up from the Polder with blankets and bottles and lay along the wall for the sea wind, which came off the harbour warm, smelling of weed and hot granite, and cooled nothing. Below the parapet the harbour had gone the colour of old brass, and the pump house hummed up through the stone.

Kaelin Drake was on the bench beside it with a clinic stick across his knees.

She knew him from the post: the hot coast in the vowels, a man talking over someone else's breathing. She hadn't known he was this big, or that Earth had taken so much off him. He sat like old crew at the end of a shift.

On the street below, a pod waited at the kerb. Its strip read DRAKE, K. over CIVIC — BATTERY.

"Director Iverson."

"Mr Drake."

"Your pod's been keeping my pace since the vac," he said. "I thought I'd save it the stairs."

She didn't sit. "You came to my wall."

"You come up most nights. Your floor says so. It's a chatty floor." He held out a handheld. "Look first. Then tell me to go."

A photograph of a page. Pencil, square capitals, the page ruled by somebody who didn't trust the lines that came with it.

LIN Y. 14. RAMAN D. 9. MOSS A. 11. REYES J. 8. AUR'K, READER. 40.

Under each, a stamp. The night of 14 March, logoff by logoff, against her floor's own rota.

"Minutes," Kaelin said. "After they go home. Their frames go on. Small corrections, each in its own shape. She can tell Lin's from Raman's, like handwriting." He touched the last line. "His runs forty. For a man who's at home making me tea."

CUATRO, she thought, and CINCO SEIS, and kept her face where it was.

"This came off my floor."

"Off a ring relay. Your frames go up to the ring like everybody's."

"Elena Vázquez."

"I didn't say."

"You didn't need to." She gave him back the handheld. "It runs down."

"For forty minutes."

"It runs down."

"She said you'd say that." He turned it face down on his knee. "I promised him on a beach I'd stop asking what that floor is. So I'm not asking him."

"You're asking me."

"I'm asking you to take him off it."

"No."

He waited for the rest. She didn't give it to him.

"What's it for?" he said. "The floor. The contract that runs to a date."

"I can't tell you."

"That's what he says." He nearly smiled. "You two should get a sign made."

"Where's the raw?"

"On a hundred roofs."

Of course it was. And on her desk for a week, a line off a Continuity water controller outside São Paulo, from a town the relays don't serve: KEEPER: ROCHA, D. — DOOR OPENED 23:04. DOOR CLOSED 23:58. She had read it twice and not known what it was for.

"I'll audit the floor," she said. "Tonight. On our logs."

"Not hers?"

"Hers came off a water controller on a keeper's key. The minute it's in my record, somebody in a collar asks whose key." She looked at him. "So it's not in my record."

"You're keeping her out of it."

"I'm keeping my record clean."

He weighed whether those were the same thing and let it go.

"One more," he said. "Don't tell him I came."

"He won't hear it from me."

It was out before she had decided to say it. She noticed that afterwards.

High over the towers the storm was showing, a red glow at the top of the dark, coming and going. People on the grass pointed. Kaelin looked up at it, and his free hand went to his chest, to the place where a dosimeter used to clip.

"Still on," he said.

Her slate chimed. MALÉ. SIX OF SIX. FULL AIR.

"Six people," she said. "Behind their water since eleven. They're out."

"Good." He got the stick under him and stood in two stages. "That's good."

She took the pencil off its string and wrote 40 on the inside of her wrist, under 133. She was calling the floor before he reached the stairs.

"Get me the Spire's engineer," she said. "Every chair's logs since November. And the one-o'clock pass. All of it."

· · ·

On the glass by her door somebody had rubbed out 446 and written 452, and drawn a small sail under it, edge-on.

Her room was hotter than the floor. The cooling never reached the end of the glass, and tonight it had stopped trying. It smelled of warm paper and the coffee gone cold in her cup at six. Rachel sat in her shirtsleeves with the handheld face down in its pocket, and the Spire's engineer sat across from her in his Spire black with his case at his feet.

He had kept the Lantern's clock on go-live night. Now he kept the Gate's. He was young enough that he still looked surprised when the numbers agreed with him.

"Every logoff since the fourteenth of November," he said. "Every chair. The reader. Off the line's own clock. They persist. After logoff the operator model goes on emitting. Corrections at the edges. It decays."

A row for each chair: the name, the peak after logoff in parts per thousand of the shift, and a column headed CLASS.

REYES J. 0.83. RESIDUAL. LIN Y. 0.31. RESIDUAL. MOSS A. 0.27. RESIDUAL. RAMAN D. 0.22. RESIDUAL. AUR'K, READER. 0.04. RESIDUAL.

At the foot: Threshold for RESIDUAL: 1.00 per thousand.

"Reyes is top."

"Since February. It climbs." He slid a second sheet across, a line going up in steps, a step for each week. "Still under."

"And the one-o'clock pass."

"Nobody was coupled, so the line flew it on the best operator model it had for pod C, on the morning's data." He said it as if he were reading a fuel gauge. "That's Reyes. It's in the line's spec, so a pass isn't lost at the change. It isn't in your floor's book. I don't think anyone ever needed it before."

"It flew him."

"It flew his pattern."

She looked at the first row. Point eight three. A man who brought his aunt's flan in a cool bag and ran the hurricane pool and had never once asked her to repeat an order.

"Who drew the line at one?"

"The models. It's their noise floor. Below that, a model can't tell a pattern from the room it's in."

"So it decides what it can't hear."

"Every gauge does. That's calibration."

"This is height," she said. "How loud. Where's length?"

"There's no field for length. It's a level audit." He frowned, as if he had only just noticed. "I could build one. Ten days."

"The board sits at nine."

"Then not by nine."

Ten days with an audit open on her own chairs, in a storm season, and every chair in the row knowing. Against six people out of their water with full air.

"Print it," she said.

He printed it. She took a pen out of the drawer, not the pencil, and under the last row, in her own hand, she wrote RESIDUAL. Audited 22 March. R. Iverson, civic director.

It looked like every flood order she had ever put her name to.

When he had gone she sent one line to Kaelin Drake from her own handheld, off the floor's routing. Audited. Residual. Iverson. Then she pulled her cuff down.

· · ·

The board sat in the old review room at nine, under the long glass, with the harbour black behind it and the wall's lamps laying a green stripe across the table.

Three seats. Hers, at the head. One for the Mandate, which tonight was a screen at the far end: Wáng Sù in Beijing's morning, a white window behind him, a glass of water at his elbow, full. The third seat was empty. Nobody had moved its chair since February.

"Malé. Six of six," she said. "And an audit." She laid the printout down, though he was a screen. "Every chair's logoff since November. Patterns persist, at low levels, all below the threshold. I've classed it residual."

Wáng read it. She watched his eyes go down the page a line at a time, to the threshold, and stop there. He moved his glass a centimetre.

"Residual," he said. "It means what remains."

"It means below the line."

"Yes. And the line is the system's own." He folded his hands. "I do not say that as a criticism. A system that knows its own noise is a good system. Six today. Four hundred and fifty-two since November, by your floor's count. I have counted them."

"I know you have."

"Then I will say what I came to say, while the count is in front of us. On the third of August the ring will still be over this city. So will the storms that take pieces off it, and the summer, and every summer after. The second of August is a day. The sky is not a day."

The pump under the floor hummed up through her shoes. In the glass the room held its own reflection: three chairs, one of them empty, and a woman in shirtsleeves with her cuff pulled down.

"I do not ask to open it wider, Director," said Wáng. "I ask that on the third of August, nobody closes it."

· · ·

The call came through the night desk at ten to eleven. The desk didn't know what to do with it, so they gave it to her.

"He's at my table," said Joaquín's aunt. "He's gone quiet. He's breathing. He doesn't answer me."

The pod took the raised road out over the Shallows with its windows down, because its cooling was rationed after ten and the air outside was no worse. Joaquín's district sat on its fill in the dark, two streets of new walls on barged-in ground, every window open, every fan going. Somebody was frying onions. A dog barked at the pod and gave up.

His aunt opened the door before Rachel knocked. White blouse, black stockings in the heat, the same as at the graveside in July. She didn't say hello.

The flat was one room and a kitchen, and the room was mostly table. Rice, beans, a dish of mole under a cloth. Two places laid. Joaquín sat at one of them in his shirt with a fork in his hand and food on the fork, and his eyes open, and his head a little on one side.

"Since half past nine," said his aunt. "He was telling me about the children on your floor. Then he stopped."

Rachel knew the look. She had seen it on a screen on go-live night, thirty times at once. She had never seen it at a kitchen table, with a fork.

She sat down in the other chair. She took the fork out of his hand and put it on the plate. His fingers stayed curled the shape of it.

"Joaquín."

The fan turned its head across them and away. The street smelled of onions. Down the stairs somebody was arguing about a parking space in Spanish, and his aunt went to the window and told them to be quiet in a voice that would have stopped a crowd.

Rachel held his hand on the table and didn't let go. She didn't call the medic. She thought about calling the medic, and about what the medic would write, and under whose name it would go.

At 23:41 his hand closed on hers.

He looked at the table. Then at his aunt, and the dish under the cloth, and at Rachel, and he frowned as if somebody had moved the furniture.

"Jefa," he said. "Did I miss the pass?"

"No."

"Good." He looked down at himself, at his shirt. "Where's my coat?"

His aunt made a sound in the kitchen doorway.

"On the rail," Rachel said. "At the Battery. It was thirty-four."

"In March?" He laughed, a little. "Get away." He looked at her face, and stopped laughing. "I took it off?"

"Everybody clapped."

He thought about it. She watched him look for it, as you'd pat your pockets for keys, and not find it.

"I don't remember that," he said.

His aunt brought him water. He drank it all, and asked for more, and drank that, and the colour came back into him, most of it. He ate three forkfuls of the rice cold, because his aunt was standing there, and said it was good, because it was.

At the door he walked her down the stairs in his socks.

"Same chair tomorrow?" he said.

There was a line in her wrist under her cuff. There was a page on her desk with her name under it in pen, and the word on it was her word.

"Same chair," Rachel said.

In the pod she opened Friday's roster on her slate. Morning block, chair four. She typed REYES, and the pod took the raised road back over the dark district toward the wall.
