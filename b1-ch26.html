Fractured Horizons · Chapter 26

The plea

Fractured Horizons · III. The cut

26

14 JuneElenaVila Verde, Daniel's cooperativeMars delay 4.2 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE, THE COOPERATIVE HALL / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.2 min

Elena put pump house nine's handset down on the rota table, face down, as Daniel set down anything that might spill.

Nobody in the hall looked round. They were busy. Forty people at one in the morning in a room built for meetings about water, and every one of them had a job. The fan in the doorway turned the heat from one side of the room to the other. Forró came off somebody's roof a little behind its singer.

The crews' handhelds chirped one after another, forty-one up, forty-two up, and Daniel's mother sat on a chair under the mesh map with the cap of her marker in her teeth, ticking the north line roof by roof as each one answered.

The last tick was in red. It was a roof two streets from theirs in the Polder, to carry the knock the final stretch so it wouldn't come in by the roof Bellamy watched, and at half past eight it had gone dark with its district in the heat. Elena had stood at this table and watched New York's nine o'clock come and go, knowing what the channel would do to Haven's wall. The roof came back at twenty to twelve.

The spare lay in front of her, the one-eyed fish face down, the door open on it.

"Is she looking away?" Daniel said. He was standing at her shoulder with two cups of the hall's coffee. It was worse than his.

"She didn't say."

"That's yes, from her."

"That's yes." She took the cup and didn't drink it. "First, Mars."

She wrote it as she would have written a fault ticket, and sent it down the hard stop under her own name, through the door, past a seal that read Director and had never been built to look at doors:

Cobalt Basin, field operations. Drake. Do not pull the heads. Do not cut the Mars end alone. One end cut alone floods the end left open. Both ends must go inside one light-crossing of each other. I am cutting the Earth end. I will send you the time.

It went. Somewhere past the haze it began to cross toward a man she had never spoken to, whose driller she had called, in a room with a recorder running, a contact injury.

Then she went down into the Spire.

The maintenance layer opened under her thumb as it always had, a list in the Spire's grey, and the Lantern's Earth end sat in it near the top, where anything live and expensive sat.

EARTH END. LIVE. Stop: command action. The Charter, New York. The Mandate, Beijing.

Under it, smaller, the line she had come for.

Service state: available. Out of service, maintenance: any maintenance authority.

"That's it?" Daniel said.

"That's half of it." The command stop would have walked the channel down to nothing over minutes, and it needed two cities. A maintenance state needed one engineer and dropped it where it stood. "I can take it out of service. So can anybody in the Spire with my rights. It would be back up in ten minutes."

"Then it's a pause."

"And the minute I touch it, the layer writes it down." She turned the spare toward him. "It writes my name and the door I came in by. They'll see this roof. They'll see the line up your wall."

Daniel looked at the screen and then at the map, where his mother was ticking a roof in a town she had never been to.

"So it can't be a pause," he said.

"It has to be a cut that nobody dares lift." Elena put her cup down. "That means they have to know why. Everybody. Before I touch it."

The warmth came up at the back of her head, where it always came, and settled.

Look at you, her sister's voice said, delighted. Filing. At one in the morning, in a town with one tap.

Elena's hands found each other on the table. She took them apart.

"The paper," she said. "Now. While the door's still dark."

· · ·

The rota folder came out from between May and June. Mãe brought it herself and laid it on the table under the lamp and took her glasses off her head and put them on.

"Todas?" she said.

"Todas," said Elena.

Elena laid the slips out in order, as she had on the roof. The session first, every slip with the hour on it. Ventilation. Circulatory support. Pressors. A line into the neck. Released 06:47, director's order. The team report with her name at the top of it, the one she had been given in 2089 and read until she knew it like a prayer, with its margin full of Bellamy's grey.

Mãe photographed each one on her own handheld, holding it level in both hands, elbows on the table.

The manifest she had on the spare, copied off Haven's drum in the session room with Mara in the doorway. Number, name, role, sessions, consent. A teacher from the Shallows. Two brothers from Queens. Thirty-one, VÁZQUEZ, SORIA, VOLUNTEER. And above them all the row that had been there before the first volunteer came in.

BELLAMY, THEODORE. PATIENT.

She added it to the stack and felt nothing she could name, and then pity. That she could name. A boy in a bed for fourteen years. By morning everybody in the Charter would know his name.

He's got burns on every finger, the voice said. Is that the one with the hands? Does he ever stop soldering? Ask him if he solders in his sleep.

Elena didn't laugh. Her mouth moved, and Daniel saw it, and didn't ask.

She reached the last of the paper.

Two pages of a spec, her signature at the foot. The hold that would never let anyone be torn out of a session. Under it her own decode log, the January one, with the word she had cut still in its residue, and her name in its margin as the one who had cut it.

She laid them to one side. Not in the stack. Beside it.

They were her pages. Bellamy's paper was enough. The manifest was enough. Nobody could read the session and come out the other side still wanting the Lantern. Her two pages would only give them somewhere else to look.

Good, the voice said, warm and close and easy. That's enough. Nobody needs those. Nobody ever needed those.

Elena's hand stopped above the pages.

Don't tell him, it had said on the roof. He'll make you stop. The same ease. The same certainty.

She picked her two pages up and put them on top of the stack, face up, her signature in the lamplight, and the decode log on top of them.

Mãe looked at the name at the foot. Then at Elena.

"Essa é sua," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Sí."

She photographed it.

There was one more page in Elena's coat, folded twice, with four answers in pencil down its back. She didn't take it out. Her log said when the first packet came. Only this page said what else had happened at that second, and what it meant about the thing that had sent the warnings, and she couldn't prove it in a sentence to a city whose saves map had passed three thousand. She kept her arm across it.

Elena. The voice had changed. It came fast now, and close. Elena, listen to me. They're holding on. All of them on the wall, the ones on the floor, they've got hold of me, they're holding on to me, and if you shut it they let go all at once. They fall. You built it so nobody— You said. You said it in the corridor. Nobody gets torn out.

She had said it. In a corridor under the Spire, in 2089, with her hands closed one over the other, to her sister in a paper gown.

She ran it as a fault. She couldn't stop herself. It was what she was.

A channel holding thirty people coupled to it at once. A maintenance state that dropped the channel in a second, with nobody to walk it down. Her own hold, written for one volunteer on one line, trying to ride through on every one of them at once, and failing. She went down the branches and every one of them came out in the same place.

It was true.

"Daniel."

He put his cup down.

"When it goes, the people on Haven's wall are coupled to it. Thirty of them. They'll come out of it all at once, too fast." She made herself say the end of it. "Some of them may not come back."

He looked at her for a long time.

"Is that it talking," he said, "or you?"

"Both. I checked it. It's my design." Her hands were open on the table. "It's true."

The hall had gone on working around them. Fifty up. The fan turned. Somewhere a child was asking for water in her sleep.

"Mara's people," Daniel said.

"Yes."

"And if you don't?"

"Then more of them every hour. And the rock on Mars. And whoever touches a wall next." She didn't look away from him. "It's still true."

Daniel rubbed a solder burn with the other thumb. Then he picked up the stack, her two pages on top, and squared it against the table's edge, and gave it back to his mother for the last photograph.

"Then we know what we're in," he said.

· · ·

The wind came round at twenty past one.

She felt it first in the doorway, where the fan had been pushing the same hot air for an hour, and then the fan was losing to a colder air. The papers on the rota table lifted at their corners. Mãe put both hands on them. Out in the street a dog started up and stopped.

Then the rain came down on the tin over the hall's door like gravel.

It hadn't rained in Vila Verde in five weeks. The smell came in before the cold did: wet dust off hot brick, the whole town's dust at once, and under it the tank lids on every roof starting to drum, one and then the next and then all of them, up the valley in the dark.

The cistern room's door banged open under the stairs, and the republic came up in its underwear and ran straight past the rota table and out into it, shrieking, faces up, mouths open.

"The nodes," Daniel said, and was already at the ladder.

Elena went up after him with the spare inside her coat.

The roof was black and running. Out across the valley the painted circles had gone dark with wet, and every few roofs a torch came on as somebody climbed up to their box with a tarp or a plastic tub or a coat.

On the hall's roof the node stood in its wrap by the tank, its light green, the rain sheeting off its lid. Daniel got a tarp over it and crouched with a boot on one corner and a fist on another, and a man from the bakery took the third corner without being asked, water pouring off his nose.

"How long does it want?" Elena shouted.

"Not long. It's ugly in the wet. It'll drop pieces. Drown it, hold that corner." Daniel laughed with rain in his mouth. "It's made for dropping pieces. Send it."

She crouched under the edge of the tarp with the spare in both hands.

Everything was there. The session, the manifest, the team report and its margin, her two pages, her log. Four addresses she had typed by hand in the hall: the city council, for the dawn sitting. Beijing Command. Haven's Ward, Mara and Rosa Medina by name. Cobalt Basin, medical, Ines. She had not typed a fifth. Rachel would have it by breakfast like everyone else, and nothing would say she had been sent it.

And the mesh. Daniel had cut the files into ten thousand pieces at the rota table.

"Every roof keeps a few and passes the rest," he'd said. "Want it back? Climb every roof in every free town from here to the Polder."

You sat on the stair.

The voice was not warm now.

Six hours. Six hours, Elena, ten metres from the door, and you sat on the stair, and you didn't come in. And now you're on a roof doing it again, on purpose, in the rain, and you're going to write it up. That's what you do. You'd rather write it up. You'd rather die of thirst and write it up.

Rain ran off the tarp's edge and down the back of her neck.

Elena knew that one. She had heard it on the Veracruz roof inside Haven's wall, with Soria pulling the end of her hair. It had been a joke then.

She pressed send.

The node's light went from green to amber and stayed amber. Under the tarp Daniel's handheld began to count. Two hundred. Nine hundred. Out on the valley the roofs began to answer, torch after torch, too far off to hear, each one taking its pieces and passing the rest on through the rain.

Four thousand. Seven.

I opened Mamá's window because you wouldn't come home. You sat on the stair because you wouldn't come in. You never come in.

The first roof to answer sent back what it held, as the mesh did, so the sender knew it had landed: the top of the team report. Lead architect: VÁZQUEZ, E.

Ten thousand.

The amber went back to green.

It was out. Her name was on it, at the top of the team report and the foot of the spec and the margin of her own log, on every roof between here and the Polder, wet. By morning the Charter would have it. By noon it would have her.

She found she had expected to feel it as a fall, and it wasn't. It was the roof under her knees, and the rain, and Daniel's hand on the tarp a few centimetres from hers.

"Até que enfim," the man from the bakery told the sky, and laughed.

· · ·

The time.

She did it under the tarp, on the spare's glass, with the rain hammering a hand's width over her head. The light to Mars. A man on foot in a storm at a boundary she had never seen, getting his hand to whatever they had built there to cut it. A margin, because there was always a margin, because she had once built a margin and her sister had died inside it.

01:00 New York. Twenty minutes.

She wrote it on the hard stop, under her own name, to the man who waited in the brown.

Drake. 01:00 New York, 14 June. Both ends. I won't move it.

She sent it. Then, in the Spire's grey, she found the Earth end again, and the line under it, and set the Earth end to go out of service at 01:00, and the layer wrote her name beside it, and the door she had come in by.

On the spare a clock came up and began to run backward. 19:58. 19:57.

The warmth at the back of her head pulled in, small.

Elena.

It was barely there. The rain was louder.

It's dark at the edge. I don't like the edge. I counted to thirty-seven by myself, the whole night, the brown one was twenty. You were asleep. I kept counting so you wouldn't have to. I didn't mind. A long gap, and the rain. Don't make me count by myself again.

Daniel looked up from the tarp. He saw her face. He didn't ask. He moved his hand the few centimetres across the tarp's corner and put it over hers.

She stood up out of the tarp into the rain, so it would be her voice and not her head that said it.

"I fell asleep."

It came out loud. The bakery man looked round. She didn't lower it.

"I was little, with my feet in the water and my head on your leg, and you counted all night by yourself, and I never asked you the number. More than thirty years, and I never asked. You had to put it in a packet and send it to me from Mars."

The rain was in her mouth. She let it be.

"I sat on the stair. I sat on the stair for six hours because I couldn't stand to watch, and I built the thing that held you there, and I signed it. Everyone will read that by morning. Good. I want them to."

The warmth leaned. She didn't let it.

"If it's you in there, then I'm sorry. I'm sorry you counted alone. I'm sorry I wanted Mamá on every machine. I'm sorry I held you. I am not going to hold you any more." Her voice broke on the last of it and she went on through the break. "And if it isn't you, then let go of her. Let go of my sister. She was never yours to keep."

Nothing answered.

Rain on the tanks, all the way up the valley. The children down in the street, still shrieking at it. Daniel crouched on the corner of the tarp, looking up at her with water running off his face, his hand where hers had been.

She looked at the spare. 17:02.

She knelt back down beside him, in the wet, with the node's green light between them.

"Diecisiete," she said, and kept the count herself.
