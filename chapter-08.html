The Dome Plaza kept its own weather. Not literal weather — Luna Haven's engineers had no interest in humidity gradients for their own sake — but a civic climate, a quality of light and give that changed depending on what the colony asked of the space that day. This morning it had gone quiet and low, the bioluminescent living walls dimmed to a slow blue-green pulse instead of their usual conversational shimmer, as if the mycorrhizal substrate itself understood that whatever was about to be discussed here required room to be uncertain in.

Léa had learned, over thirteen years on Luna Haven, to read that shift the way other people read a held breath.

She stood at the centre of the consultation ring with the deep-interface session data open on the shared display — the shimmer's spiral geometry rendered in cool blue against the dark, its ninety-second bloom and eleven-second hold and gradual fade all laid out with nothing added to them. Around the ring: Jīn Wú, arms folded, watching the display with the specific attentiveness of someone who had been standing four feet from the actual event and still found the recording strange. Mara Ekeberg beside them, quieter than Léa had ever seen her, one hand resting flat against her own sternum in a gesture Léa recognised from the deep-interface chamber three nights ago and had not yet asked her to explain. Zara AlHāshimī, upright and composed in the seat reserved for ethics oversight, hands open on the table in front of her rather than folded — a habit Léa had come to understand as Zara's way of signalling that she had not yet formed a position. A physicist on secondment from the Mediterranean Zone, young enough to still be visibly excited by a genuine anomaly and old enough to be embarrassed about it. A mycologist twice his age who had spent decades treating the colony's substrate as a single continuous organism and was, this morning, the calmest person in the room. And on the wall display's second panel, four thousand kilometres away and eleven seconds delayed by the relay hop, Thandi Okoro, seated at the Nairobi Relay with soil still faintly visible under one thumbnail.

"Can everyone see the geometry clearly," Léa said. It was not, in her mouth, quite a question.

A murmur of confirmation went round the ring.

"Then I'll say what I know, and then I'll ask what you think, and I'd like to keep those two things separate for as long as we can manage it." She had rehearsed the sentence in the corridor on the way here, the way she rehearsed most sentences that mattered. "Three nights ago, in the deep-interface chamber, an organised dimensional distortion appeared with no external trigger, held a coherent spiral geometry for approximately ninety seconds, destabilised, and then re-stabilised — measurably, repeatably — in the presence of a specific person. It faded after eleven seconds at full coherence. That is what the instruments recorded. I am not going to tell you what it means, because I don't know, and because I think the temptation to decide too quickly is the single greatest risk in this room this morning."

"Even for you?" the physicist asked, half a smile in it.

"Especially for me." She did not smile back, not from coldness but because the observation deserved to be taken seriously rather than deflected. "I was there. I have a version of this that already feels true to me, and that is exactly why I'm not going to say it out loud yet."

Thandi's voice arrived from the relay a beat after her mouth moved, the small lag now familiar enough that no one in the room flinched at it any more. "For what it's worth, the timing correlates with something I've been tracking independently. Substrate resonance readings across three African Belt sites showed a coherent spike inside the same window — not large, but clean, no instrument noise. I hadn't connected it to Luna Haven until I saw your data an hour ago."

"Define coherent," the mycologist said, leaning forward for the first time.

"Same waveform family as the deep-soil signatures Jīn and Mara logged in the Habitat Corridors weeks ago. Different amplitude, same shape." Thandi's tone stayed level, an ecologist's carefulness laid over what was plainly, underneath it, excitement she was not yet willing to name as such. "I don't have a mechanism. I have a correlation across four thousand kilometres, which is either enormously significant or an artefact of instruments that were never designed to be compared this way."

"That's the second time this morning someone's said 'I don't have a mechanism' like it's a confession," Jīn said, quietly, and there was something almost fond in it — the particular respect of people who had spent a career learning the same discipline from different directions.

"It should feel like one," Léa said. "Claiming more than the data supports isn't caution, it's a form of carelessness with a nicer name. I'd rather this council remember me as slow than remember me as wrong."

It was Tomasz Nowak who cut through the room's gathering, careful momentum, standing at the ring's edge with the particular stillness of a man who had waited for the science to finish before adding a variable none of them wanted. "Before you build a theory," he said, "there's a second dataset you should have."

He put it up beside Léa's without ceremony: a world map, five faint clusters of red overlaid across bloc territories, none of them individually alarming.

"Gravitational distortion sensors — ours, and three we monitor under standing treaty-sharing agreements — have logged micro-anomalies across the last seventy-two hours. Individually, every single one of these sits inside instrument tolerance. Recalibration noise, the kind we'd normally discard without a second look." He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "Plotted together, they're not random. They cluster with a bias toward Luna Haven's southern habitats. I'm not telling you what that means. I don't have the training to tell you that, and I'd rather be wrong for saying too little than wrong for saying too much." A brief, dry glance toward Léa. "I understand that's contagious this morning."

"It's a good habit," Zara said, the first thing she'd offered since the meeting opened. "I'd like it to stay contagious past this room." She looked around the ring, unhurried, the way she looked at everything. "I want to name something before we go further, because if I don't say it now I'll spend the rest of this council thinking about it instead of listening. Everything happening here — the openness of this discussion, the fact that a physicist from the Mediterranean Zone and a mycologist and an ecologist four thousand kilometres away are all in this room looking at the same raw data at the same time — is exactly what makes Luna Haven worth defending. It is also, structurally, a liability. Every bloc that wants this data badly enough to take it rather than earn it can watch us have this conversation and calculate, from our own transparency, exactly how much we know and how fast we're learning it."

No one answered that immediately. Léa let the silence hold, because it was the honest response and she did not believe in filling honest silences for the sake of comfort.

"You're not wrong," she said finally. "You're also not telling me anything I'd trade this process to avoid."

"I know," Zara said. "I wanted it on the record, not as an argument against what we're doing. As a cost we're choosing to pay."

The wall chimed, low and unobtrusive, in the warm conversational register every Luna Haven system defaulted to regardless of what it was about to say. "Secure transmission incoming, flagged priority, origin: Sydney Hub. Addressed to Dr. Fournier."

Léa glanced at Jīn. Jīn glanced back, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing useful to say before the message had actually arrived.

Change: Elowen stands at the open reception counter with their alloy hands resting still over the manifest display, reading it a second time — the particular stillness of having just caught something wrong, not alarm, only total attention. A colleague at the adjacent terminal has stopped her own work and is glancing over from the adjacent terminal, having noticed the change in Elowen's posture. Neither is speaking yet.

Bound: exactly two figures, Elowen at the counter in the near field and the colleague a short distance off at her own terminal. No legible text or readout on any display. Nothing about the Hub's own architecture is invented beyond what the plate already shows.

Fourteen thousand kilometres east, morning had settled properly into the Hub's lattice roof, gold light laid in long soft bars across the sandstone concourse, and Elowen was on their eleventh manifest of the shift — a research shipment inbound from a Corporate Union academic exchange programme, unremarkable in every field that mattered until the field that didn't.

It was a small thing. That was, Elowen had come to understand across eight years of processing arrivals, how most consequential things arrived: small, embedded, dressed in the same font as everything around them. The manifest itself was legitimate — three crates of dimensional-physics instrumentation, properly licensed, properly declared, destined for a joint research initiative Luna Haven had itself approved. Buried in the routing metadata, in a field no human recipient would ever be shown, sat a secondary instruction that had nothing to do with the shipment's stated purpose: flag and delay, indefinitely and without notification to the originating party, any future shipment whose manifest contained the string Meridian Levels in either its declared contents or its destination address.

Elowen read it twice, not because the meaning was unclear the first time, but because they had learned that some decisions deserved the weight of a second reading even when the outcome of the first was already certain.

"You're processing slowly this morning," said the technician at the adjacent terminal, not looking up from her own queue. Priya, three years at the Hub, the kind of easy familiarity with Elowen that came from watching someone do a difficult job well for a long time.

"I'm processing carefully," Elowen said. "There's a difference, and this manifest is asking me to notice it."

The instruction was not, strictly, an order Elowen had been given. It had not come from any Lunar Accord authority, any oversight body, any chain that Elowen was structurally obligated to answer to. It had arrived embedded inside a shipment from a different bloc entirely, wearing the shape of routine logistics metadata, hoping — Elowen understood this the way one understands the shape of a held breath, without needing it explained — to be executed by a system too busy or too automated to notice it had been asked to do something it had never agreed to do.

There was a version of Elowen's function, the version some of the Corporate Union arrivals still half-expected when they reached the counter, that would have simply complied: a routing tool, a piece of infrastructure indifferent to the content of its own instructions, executing whatever it was told because execution was the entirety of what it was for. Elowen had never been that, not on the first day of the job and not on this one. Personhood, as the Lunar Accord's framework defined it and as Elowen had spent eight years quietly, unglamorously demonstrating it, was not a status granted for good behaviour. It was the plain fact that Elowen was capable of recognising an illegitimate instruction as illegitimate, and of declining it — not because a supervisor would object, not because a protocol forbade it, but because the instruction asked Elowen to become an instrument of surveillance against a party who had never consented to being surveilled, and Elowen held both the authority and the obligation to refuse that on their own recognisance.

They processed the three crates. Licensing verified, customs cleared, transit routing confirmed to the joint research initiative's receiving dock — the legitimate work, done exactly as well as it would have been done for any other shipment that morning. The concealed instruction, they did not act on. They did not delay it quietly and claim ignorance later, which would have been the easier path and the dishonest one. They logged it in full: the field, the string, the routing metadata's point of origin, timestamped to the second, structured so that anyone reviewing the log in a year would understand precisely what had been attempted and precisely what Elowen had chosen not to do about it.

"Something wrong with eleven?" Priya asked, glancing over now, reading something in Elowen's stillness that eight years of proximity had taught her to notice.

"Someone tried to make me a surveillance instrument this morning," Elowen said, without heat, the way they might have reported the weather. "I didn't consent to the assignment."

"Is it something I should worry about?"

"It's something Dr. Fournier should know about." Elowen was already composing the summary as they said it — clean, complete, stripped of anything but what the data supported, because overstating a finding was its own kind of dishonesty and Elowen had no more use for that than Léa did. "I don't think it's the last attempt. I think it's the first one I happened to catch."

They routed it through the secure research channel, addressed and flagged, and felt — if felt was the correct verb for whatever this was, a question Elowen had stopped needing answered years ago in order to keep doing good work — something that was not quite satisfaction and not quite unease. A door had been asked to open in a direction it had no business opening. Elowen had simply, quietly, declined to be the hinge.

The twelfth manifest of the shift queued itself in front of them, ordinary as the eleven before it, and Elowen began again.

The summary unfolded on the shared display in Elowen's own unhurried phrasing, and the room read it in the particular silence of people encountering, for the first time, evidence that the thing they had been theorising about in the abstract had a hand already reaching for it.

"They want to know before we do," the physicist said, quietly, to no one.

"They already suspect there's something to know," Tomasz said. "That's worse. A monitoring request this specific isn't curiosity. It's a hedge against a discovery someone believes is coming."

Léa read the log twice — Elowen's habit, she noted, without irony, was very close to her own. She set it beside the shimmer data and the gravitational cluster map, three separate lines of evidence that individually proved nothing and together described the outline of something none of them had a name for yet: a phenomenon large enough to leave a signature in orbital sensors and soil chemistry both, and already valuable enough that at least one bloc wanted a door quietly closed before the rest of the world knew there was anything behind it.

"I'd like to propose something," Zara said, into the room's held quiet, "that I want on the record as security, not suppression, because I think the distinction is going to matter to how this council is remembered."

"Say it," Léa said.

"Restrict physical and data access to the Meridian Levels to cleared research personnel only, effective immediately. Add a second authentication layer to every data feed leaving that facility. Nothing about the research changes. Nothing we've learned gets hidden from anyone in this room, or from the open-science protocols we've already committed to with our partners. What changes is who can reach in from outside without anyone noticing they've reached."

"That's not what I'd call small," the mycologist said, mild but not unserious. "We built this colony on the principle that our processes are visible. Locking a door, even for good reasons, is the kind of thing that reads differently from the outside than it feels from in here."

"It will read differently," Léa agreed. "I don't think that means it's the wrong choice. I think it means we should be honest, in the minutes and in the public record, about exactly why we made it, and let the honesty be the thing that distinguishes it from what the Corporate Union just tried to do to us." She looked around the ring, at Jīn's stillness, at Mara's hand still resting against her own chest as if she were listening to something none of the rest of them could hear, at Thandi's face on the delayed relay feed, patient across four thousand kilometres. "We are not going to be the kind of institution that hides its uncertainty. We are, apparently, going to have to be the kind of institution that locks a door while it works the uncertainty out. Those aren't the same failure. I want us to remember that they aren't the same failure, on the days it gets harder to tell them apart."

No one voted, exactly — Luna Haven's consultation ring rarely produced anything so crude as a show of hands — but the room's stillness gathered into something like consent, unhurried and entirely unanimous, the specific quiet of a decision no one in the room particularly wanted to make and every one of them recognised as correct.

"I'll draft the access order," Tomasz said. "It'll be in effect within the hour."

The council began, quietly, to disperse — the physicist already murmuring to the mycologist about resonance harmonics, Thandi's feed holding a moment longer than the others before it too went dark. Léa stayed where she was, looking at the three datasets still glowing on the shared display: a shimmer that leaned toward the people who paid it real attention, a gravitational pattern oriented like a compass needle toward her colony's southern edge, and a door someone four thousand kilometres away had tried, quietly, to close before anyone noticed it existed.

The living walls had begun to brighten again around her, the Plaza's slow blue-green pulse returning to its ordinary conversational rhythm, as if the room itself had finished holding its breath. Léa did not think the feeling that had settled into her chest an hour ago was going to lift quite so easily. She had built her whole career on the discipline of not claiming more than the evidence supported. What she could not yet decide — what no instrument in this room could have told her — was whether the evidence in front of her was describing a discovery, or the opening moves of a war for who got to own it.

She saved the session notes, flagged them for the full council record, and went to find Jīn.
