The threshold

Fractured Horizons · I. The voice

01

12 January 2095ElenaNew York, the SpireMars delay 13.2 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE SPIRE / Elena Vázquez — Mars 13.2 min

The threshold fired at 05:14, and Elena's first thought was that she had finally broken it.

It had never fired. She had spent most of a year tuning it to hear one wrong signal across every relay the Charter owned, and for years since it had sat at the foot of the amber boards like a smoke alarm in a house that never burned. Now a thin white line stood up from it, the only white on the floor, and the floor's voice said, "Threshold event. Class unassigned. Owner: Vázquez."

"Acknowledged," she said, and stood.

The anomaly floor of the Spire was a dim room shaped like a lens, built so that whoever stood at its centre could see every board without turning her head. At this hour it held Elena, two cleaning machines and the boards, ranked from the top down by insured value: a cooling-grid stutter in Singapore worth more than some countries, a pressure fault in a Gulf Crescent desalination plant, a pump house on the wall drawing too much power.

In the bottom corner, where nobody looked, sat the week's surge forecast for the harbour. It had been red for so long that red had stopped meaning anything.

Rain ran down the glass behind the boards. Below, the streets of the Polder shone black under the solar canopies, and at the end of every eastward street stood the wall. January rain, on streets the old people at the night markets still remembered white. Elena had seen snow twice. Both times it was gone by noon.

She had less than three hours until the 08:00 shift review, and she meant to walk into it with a closed ticket: threshold event, cause found, fault corrected, owner's apology attached. An alarm that rang for nothing was worse than no alarm. She had said so herself, in that room, to people who had wanted a louder one.

She started at the bottom. Sensor, loopback, clock.

The threshold's diagnostics came back green. She fed it a clean sample from last spring and it stayed silent. She fed it a dirty one and it fired, crisply, and reset itself. She checked the Spire's clock against the Battery's and the Battery's against the orbital ring's and found them agreeing to the nanosecond. Clocks did that. This once, she had wanted them not to.

Then she looked at the signal.

It sat on the Mars relay's calibration band, where the relays tuned themselves against the rock and where nothing lived but hiss. Knowing that hiss on every night of the year was the threshold's whole purpose. Tonight a pulse lay in it, small and clean, too regular to be weather and too faint to be anyone's equipment. She marked it. Then she found the one before it, under the line, and the one before that.

Her lace chimed behind her ear. Daniel.

She let it chime twice and took it, because he would only call again. His face came up at the edge of her left eye: hair wrecked, the kitchen behind him, a mug held in both hands. He held every mug like a man warming his hands at a fire, even in August.

"You're up," she said.

"Vila Verde's up. They're two hours ahead and they think it makes them right about everything." He drank. "Also there's a bucket under the skylight, and it's winning."

"I fixed that skylight."

"You fixed it in theory. In practice it's raining in the kitchen." He tipped the camera down. Rain rang into a blue bucket. "I've named it. I talk to it more than I talk to you this week."

She was smiling. She noticed, and kept doing it. "What did you name it?"

"Elena." He looked at her more closely. "You've got your fault face on."

"I've got a fault."

"Your fault, or its fault?"

It was the question she had been walking around for ten minutes. She looked at the pulses laid out in her overlay, at the gaps between them.

"I'll tell you at breakfast," she said.

"You won't, but I'll make it anyway." The call closed. His mug went with it, and the bucket, and the rain in the kitchen, and she was alone with the gaps.

The same gap from each pulse to the next, to the second. Not wandering as a failing part wandered, warming and cooling with the building. She set the interval beside the one number Foresight never had to look up, because half its forecasts lived and died by it: how long light took, this morning, to come from Mars.

They agreed to a tenth of a minute.

She checked it twice. Then she checked the relay's own logs and found the pulses coming down the Mars link with no station handshake, no Cobalt Basin routing, no sender at all. A fault on Earth could not know how far away Mars was today. Whatever had made these did, and it was keeping time with her.

Protocol said a confirmed anomaly went to the duty chain unopened. She had written that protocol.

She opened it.

· · ·

The pulses were frames. It took her eleven minutes to see it and four to prove it. Someone had taken the format the relays used to calibrate themselves and filled it with data instead of hiss, a few hundred bytes a pulse, stitched end to end across the last hour.

She wanted gibberish. Gibberish would have been noise with a regular habit, and she could have written that up by eight.

It parsed on the first pass.

"Decoded content," said the floor. "Class unassigned."

"Hold."

It held.

Two tracks unrolled across her overlay in orbital shorthand she had not read since training. The first was a Charter shuttle on its approach to Cobalt Basin, its slot and burn sequence laid out in numbers the Charter did not publish. She knew which shuttle. Everyone in the Spire knew: the director's relay survey team, forty people sent to Mars to measure the rock for a new relay nobody had yet been allowed to build.

The second track was an ice tug in a low parking orbit. The packet put them in the same place at 14:52, New York time, nine hours out, and gave a reason. The tug had a thruster fault its own crew had not yet seen. Its emergency burn would carry it across the shuttle's corridor.

Under that, a correction. Move the burn. A time, a vector, a duration, written out in full. One answer, and no alternatives.

There was more beneath it, a second layer packed tighter than the first. She left it for the review. The prediction was the part with a clock on it.

She ran the conjunction against the Charter's public tracks and found nothing, because the public tracks did not know about the fault. She could not check the fault. Nobody on Earth could, not with every question taking the long way to Mars and back.

In the header sat an epoch field, the moment the data had been taken. It read three days ago. She frowned at it, put it down to a clock error in a sender whose clock was clearly the least of its problems, and moved on. The collision was nine hours out. The burn that prevented it was not: the correction set it for 06:31, New York time, and the correction still had to cross to Mars.

She logged the prediction as a threat, and the board did what she had helped teach it to do. It weighed the shuttle, the tug, the ice and forty lives at the Charter's rate for off-world contractors, and ranked the whole thing eleventh, below a cracked sea gate in the Shallows and a late shipment of cooling units for Singapore's schools. Cobalt Basin barely made the first screen.

She would have to walk it up by hand. Fine. That was what people were for.

She was assembling the report when she saw the residue.

The frames stopped short of the end of the last pulse. The tail was ordinary hiss, the rock's own noise, and the threshold had flagged one shape in it anyway, a few bytes it could not account for. She decoded them out of habit, expecting nothing.

The overlay showed her a word.

Bichito.

She was sitting down. Afterwards she could not remember deciding to.

Nobody else had ever called her that. Not their mother, not Daniel, not one record she had ever been in, and she had been in all of them; she had built half of them.

It came from Soria. From the top bunk in a hot room when Elena was six and couldn't sleep, duérmete ya, o me siento encima. From the door of a sealed room, the last time, with Soria already in the gown: Don't look like that. It's a scan with extra steps.

Elena clasped her hands on the desk and made herself treat it as a signal.

Seven letters. The hiss on that band was as close to random as anything she measured. A word in Spanish, spelled right, in the one stretch of the packet that held no data, was not chance. So it had been put there. Someone had known a word that existed in no file, about a woman six years dead, and had sent it to the one alarm on Earth tuned fine enough to hear it.

It was a good argument. She noticed her hands were shaking and pressed them flat.

The board's clock read 05:47. Every minute she held the report was a minute the correction spent not travelling.

She knew how the file would sit on the director's desk: prediction, method, residue. She knew what the residue would become in a review room with eight people and a recorder running. Someone would search the word. Someone would find nothing, and then someone would find her, and ask why her dead sister's name for her was arriving from Mars. They would take it off her for her own good. She would have done the same.

She opened the decode log.

She had built that log so that nobody could change it without leaving a mark, and she had been proud of it. She cut the residue out. The log took the edit and wrote in its margin, in small grey letters, Altered 05:48 — E. Vázquez. She looked at her own name there. Then she cut the word from the report too, typed residual noise, unclassified where it had been, and sent the prediction up the duty chain marked urgent.

The raw capture she could not touch. The board had sealed it the instant it arrived, and the word was still in there, a few bytes in the hiss.

Her name sat in the margin of her own log, telling anyone who read it where to dig. She left it there. Only someone who already knew the word would know what they had found.

· · ·

Bellamy came down at 05:53.

She saw him first in the glass, because she always stood where the glass showed her the doors. The lift had not come up from the street. It had come down, from the director's floor. He stepped out already dressed, the high collar of his coat closed to the throat.

"Elena." He said it as if they had arranged to meet. He crossed the floor and stopped with his back to the boards, so the amber light ran along the lines of his face and showed her nothing in them. "You've had a night."

"It has to go to Cobalt Basin now," she said. "And to orbital traffic. The correction only works if they burn before—"

"It's gone."

She stopped.

"Both," he said. "Four minutes ago, on my authority. We don't wait for a review when there's a shuttle in it. Nobody would want us to."

Four minutes ago she had sent it. She walked the building in her head: her flag up the duty chain, the chain to the night officer, the officer to the director's queue, the director reading, deciding, sending. Four minutes. Nothing in the Spire had ever moved that fast. She had seen budget lines take a season.

"You read it," she said.

"I read enough." He held out his hand, palm up, and after a second she understood he meant the file. "We'll take custody from here. It came in on a Charter relay and it touches a Charter vehicle, and there will be questions from people whose questions we have to answer. It's better if they come to me."

"It came in on my threshold."

"And we're grateful it did. It's a beautiful piece of work, and it was always going to be you who heard it. We won't forget that."

She passed him the file. She watched the board lift the raw capture out of her clearance and into his, one line of amber going grey on her side of the room.

"How did it get onto the calibration band?" she said. "No routing. No sender. Somebody on Mars built a message out of our own calibration frames, and they knew the delay to the tenth of a minute—"

"Then someone on Mars is very good at their job," Bellamy said, "and we'll find them." He glanced at the board, where the warning sat eleventh, under the cracked sea gate, and a line in his face moved and was put away. "Was there anything else in it?"

He asked it gently, looking at the board and not at her.

Elena held her hands in front of her, one closed over the other.

"Noise," she said.

He looked at her for the length of a breath. Then he nodded, as if she had told him what he had come down to hear.

"Go home," he said. "Sleep. We'll be in the review at eight, and we'll want to keep it to the method. Only the method. Then we can all go home." He turned for the lifts, and turned back. "Elena. Thank you."

The doors closed. The lift went up.

She stood at the centre of the lens with the boards around her. The surge forecast burned red in its corner, unread. Rain ran down the glass. Out past the dark, a correction she had found was crossing toward forty people, and nothing would come back from Mars before the review.

She had told him noise. It was the first thing she had ever kept from the Spire, and he had carried it out in his hand.
