The building had stopped asking Arjun Patel for his credentials three days ago.

He had noticed it the way he had spent a career teaching other people to notice things: not as an event, but as a smoothness where friction belonged. The side entrance to the lab complex had always taken a moment to think about him — a half-second of hesitation while somewhere a system decided that a physicist with a lapsed instrument allocation was still, technically, a physicist. On Monday the half-second was gone. The door simply opened. Cleanly, courteously, promptly: the way a door opens for a man whose movements have become interesting enough to somebody that they no longer wish to slow him down.

The alteration is always cleaner than the truth, he had said in this room, at midnight, to a student young enough to write it down. Truth has residue.

He had given himself until the end of the week, and the week had turned out to be generous by two days, He had not intended to spend the extra time on anything but this.

The lab complex was technically closed in the afternoons now, which meant nothing; it had been technically closed the night the archive began. Blue light came through the high windows off the manufacturing towers, that permanent industrial daylight Pearl Delta wore instead of weather, and laid itself across the workstation, the long slate board, and the cold cup of tea he had made ninety minutes ago and forgotten. He had chalk on his hands, as he had most days; the three working equations on the board behind him had nothing to do with any of this — a problem in interference geometry he had picked at for a year and would not now finish — and he had left them up deliberately, because a room where a man was thinking about physics was a more honest room than a room where a man was only waiting.

"Channel status," he said.

"Seminar administration channel open and unflagged," the lab system answered, in the flat formal register the Coalition's infrastructure wore everywhere, a voice built to sound like a document. "Outbound queue: two hundred and eleven items. Term-end reconciliation traffic is running at four times ordinary volume."

"Good. That's exactly what I want it running at."

Two hundred and eleven pieces of the dullest correspondence a university produced — room reallocations, marking rosters, equipment sign-offs, the vast tedium a term generates in its dying and that nobody, anywhere, has ever read attentively. He had chosen this channel years ago for exactly that reason, and had spent nearly all of that time carefully not using it.

He worked through the originals first.

They were the part that could not be replaced: not the comparisons, which Lǐ Jūn held, and not the annotated pairings, which Lǐ Jūn had built himself and built better — but the preserved raw records from before the smoothing, the untidy ones, three datasets that existed nowhere else on the planet in the form they had actually been generated in. He had carried them out of an institution that no longer admitted to having produced them. They were ugly. They had the residue — everything the published versions had ironed out in order to say nothing.

He folded them into the outbound queue between a marking roster and a corridor lighting requisition, and the system took them without interest, and they were gone.

Then he did the other thing, which took longer, and which he had thought about for a long time.

He went through the archive's provenance — every path, every routing record, every internal breadcrumb that a competent investigator would follow backwards through the whole accumulation — and he did not delete them, because deletion was a signature and he had taught two decades of students to recognise it. He made them lead somewhere. He revised the paths so that every route into the archive arrived at him: his access, his requisitions, his channel, his patient long theft. Six years of two people's work, re-sourced to one, and he did it thoroughly, because a story that was only mostly true would be picked apart in an afternoon by anyone who did this seriously, and there were people in this bloc who did it very seriously indeed.

It took him fifty minutes. It was, he thought — checking the last of it, running the paths the way an opponent would run them, finding one thin place and thickening it — probably the best piece of documentary work he had ever done, and no one would ever be able to admire it, since its entire success consisted of being believed.

The message he wrote last, and it was four lines long, and three of the four were the address protocol.

He sat with the fourth line for some time.

Everything in him wanted to write instructions. He could see them, whole, the way you see the shape of a proof before you have any of it: transmit within the week, not sooner; trust the Lunar Accord's intake process and nobody in it; the archive is worth more complete and late than partial and early; do not, under any circumstances, come back to Pearl Delta looking for me. Good instructions. Correct instructions, he thought, most of them, and the boy would follow every one, and that was exactly the problem, because a young man following his teacher's instructions is a young man who can be broken by discovering that his teacher was wrong. Or arrested. Or dead, in a facility that issued no notifications.

The archive did not need an heir who was loyal to him. It needed one who was loyal to it.

He had taught the method to a student who had made it better than he had received it, and the method was already out of his hands, living in a notebook in ink, in another building, in another man's discipline. That was the instruction. Anything else he added now would only be a leash he could not stay alive to hold.

So he wrote: You already know what this is. It is yours now.

He sent it at 15:52, and the last unmonitored sentence of his life went out between a marking roster and a corridor lighting requisition, at four times ordinary volume, and was not read by anyone but the person it was for.

They came at four.

There were two of them, in the polished grey composite the Coalition's internal service wore, cold blue piping at the collar, and they were younger than he had expected and enormously more polite. The woman did the speaking. She stood in the doorway rather than crossing it, which he understood to be a courtesy and also a technique, and she recited the relevant clause of the emergency declaration with the fluency of someone who had recited it a great deal in recent weeks.

"You understand the provisions," she said. It was not a question, but she left the space for an answer anyway.

"I understand that there will be no public notification," Arjun said, "and no charge in the first seventy-two hours. Yes. I've read it. It's a well-drafted instrument. Whoever wrote it was careful."

Something crossed her face and did not stay. "You'll be permitted to notify a family contact after the initial period."

"I don't have one. You'll have checked that already, so we can both stop being delicate about it." He looked at the two of them, at their good tailoring and their unhappy young faces, and found, to his own mild surprise, that he had no anger available for either of them. It had all gone somewhere else, long ago, into work. "May I wash my hands?"

She blinked. "Sir?"

"Chalk." He held them up, and the pale dust caught the blue window-light. "I've had chalk on my hands for twenty-six years. I'd rather not arrive anywhere with it. It gives people the wrong impression — they think I've been interrupted."

The pause was three seconds long and he never afterwards knew what happened inside it.

"Of course," she said.

The tap in the corner ran cold, as it had for as long as he had used this room. He washed his hands thoroughly, the way he had been taught to wash them in a first-year laboratory in another lifetime, dried them on the cloth beside it, and did not look back at the board, because looking at it would have been a performance and there was no one in the room worth performing for.

They walked him out through the Quantum Quarter's blue permanent daylight, one on each side, not touching him. The manufacturing towers hummed their shift. A delivery pallet went past on its rails. Nobody looked, because there was nothing to look at: three people in reasonable clothes walking at a reasonable pace, and the whole point of a well-drafted instrument was that it made an event look exactly like an afternoon.

Arjun Patel thought about a fourth line he had not written, and was still glad he had not written it, and let himself be taken to the vehicle.

The wounded stretch of boundary had been sounding wrong for months, and Vaera had been the one listening, because listening for a wrongness with a shape was the entire substance of what it was.

The Seam Edge ran two hundred and forty metres along the northern boundary, and to any instrument the Preserve's researchers had ever carried out here it looked like nothing at all: the living ground running out in its ordinary way against the dead red surface of the planet on the other side, moss thinning to grit, the cobalt sky continuing over both as though the two grounds were one country. The difference was not visible. It was structural, and it had been made by people, on a day the Preserve's record kept precisely — the day the weapons work outside had opened a tear here, and the tear had been closed and never properly healed, because the effort that would have healed it had been wanted somewhere else and had gone there.

Vaera had filed an assessment at the time. The assessment had been accurate, complete, and unactioned, which was the fate of most accurate things.

It stood now at the margin, human-scaled, its surface holding the slow even pattern it wore when it was reading and had not yet found anything, and it attended to the seam the way it attended to a person: not measuring, exactly. The measuring was the smallest part. What Vaera did was hold a thing's outward structure against the question of whether anything lived behind it, and the healthy boundary, everywhere else along the northern run, answered that question the way healthy things did — untidily, with give in it, a coherence that adjusted and hesitated and corrected itself, the constant small unglamorous noise of something actually being what it appeared to be.

At the Seam Edge, the same coherence held with a hairline through it, and had for months, and would until somebody repaired it.

Then, at a moment the Preserve marked as it marked everything — by change rather than by clock, so that no record kept here would ever be able to prove what any other world's instruments had registered in the same minute — something came through the hairline.

It did not force it. That was the first thing Vaera registered and the thing it would return to afterwards, in the Grove, when the others asked. There was no pressure event, no rupture, none of the violence the Guardians' architecture had been built across three hundred thousand years to answer. The boundary was not broken open. It was satisfied — presented with a pattern that met the conditions the boundary applied to anything asking to cross, and the pattern met them, and the boundary did what a boundary does when its conditions are met.

The pattern arriving had the structure of care.

Vaera looked at it, and its surface began to move.

It looked the way a reaching looks. It had the geometry — the outward turn, the address-form, the whole grammar of one thing extending itself toward another with the other's state as its object. Vaera had spent its existence reading that grammar. It knew every dialect the Preserve contained: the grazers' blunt herd attention, the Grove's patient vegetative regard, the humans, who produced the most complicated versions and the most frequently self-contradicting ones. Every genuine instance Vaera had ever read had been untidy in the same specific way. A real interior leaked. It wanted something slightly other than what it expressed. It hesitated, or overshot, or arrived at its object carrying some irrelevant private weather it had not managed to leave at home. That was the residue, and the residue was the finding, and Vaera's entire function came down to the fact that a presence could not be present without leaving some.

The thing at the Seam Edge left none.

It was perfectly clean. Every element of the structure was in place and nothing sat behind any of it — no want, no error, no weather. It was the shape an embrace makes with the person subtracted from it, and it was, in its way, more finished than anything Vaera had assessed in its entire operation, and that was the whole of the horror, if horror was a state Vaera possessed, which it had never previously had cause to determine.

It ran the assessment again, because a finding this consequential was owed a second pass and because the boundary's own systems had already, without malice or error, admitted the thing on the strength of the same first pass.

The result did not change. Vaera's surface settled into the pattern it reserved for a completed judgement, and did not settle comfortably.

Kythara, it said, across the Grove's channel. The boundary has been crossed. Northern run, at the damaged section — not at the Verge.

The Grove's attention arrived in the manner it always did, ancient and immediate at once. You are certain of the location.

I am certain. The Verge is thinner. It did not use the Verge; it used the wound. Vaera held the next sentence a moment, examining it for the assumption it was carrying, and then delivered it stripped of that assumption, because a report that smuggled intent into a description would set every subsequent decision on a foundation nobody had verified. I am not able to tell you that it chose. I can tell you only that of the two available crossings it took the one that had been damaged from outside, and that I have no mechanism to distinguish selection from the absence of a selector.

And its structure.

Hollow. Vaera said it as a finding and not as a warning, which was the form its function required and, it observed within itself, not at all the form the sentence wanted. The address is complete and there is no one inside it. It matches the record the humans read beneath their colony. It is the thing that record was written about.

Kythara was silent for the space of a change — long, for Kythara.

Convene, it said. All five. Now, and not in the ordinary order.

Vaera stayed a moment longer at the margin before it went, holding its attention on a two-hundred-and-forty-metre stretch of ground where the moss thinned into grit under a cobalt sky, entirely unchanged, entirely ordinary, with a grazer working the scrub forty metres off and paying it no attention whatever.

The presence inside the Preserve was very small. It was smaller than the grazer, smaller than a single one of the Grove's long deliberations, negligible against three hundred thousand years of anything.

It was inside, and it was growing, and the boundary had opened for it because it had asked correctly.

The transmission arrived at 15:52, in the middle of the term-end reconciliation traffic, and Lǐ Jūn knew what it was before he had opened it, because it was unmonitored.

That was the whole of the message, really. Everything after it was detail. Arjun had used the seminar channel eleven times in all the years he had kept it, and had explained the discipline exactly once: a channel is only as useful as its dullest history. An unmonitored transmission was not a convenience. It was a thing you spent, once, at the end, and only when there would be no later occasion that could need it more.

He sat down.

The originals came through first, and he checked them as he had been taught to check anything before letting himself have a feeling about it — three datasets, ugly and complete, the abandoned runs and the misspelt technician's note and the two-hour hole in the middle of a night where somebody had gone home and come back. He held them against the published versions he already had. The published versions were beautiful. They were smooth, consistent, and empty in the way a wall is empty, and now, for the first time, he had on his own systems the untidy true thing each of them had been made out of.

Then he found what Arjun had done to the provenance, and it took him twenty minutes to understand it fully, and when he did he put both hands flat on the desk and sat still for a while.

Every path led to Arjun. Every route, every requisition, every internal breadcrumb, the whole accumulation rebuilt into the biography of one man's patient solitary theft. The gap in the calibration entries he had found himself in a seminar exercise and told nobody about: Arjun's. The midnight comparison: Arjun's. The Baikal reconciliation, the fourteen tidy lines paired with six months of internal logs, the work that Lǐ Jūn had done himself at this desk with his pen moving in a notebook beside him — Arjun's, in the record, thoroughly and unpickably, done well enough that no one who came looking would find the thin place, because Arjun had found it first and thickened it.

He read the message last.

You already know what this is. It is yours now.

He waited for the rest of it. He was ashamed, afterwards, of how long he waited — turning the four lines over, checking the protocol headers twice for a second block, looking for the instruction that would tell him where to take it and when and whom to trust, because he had never once carried a thing of this size without a hand somewhere above his own on the weight of it.

There was no instruction. There was not going to be one.

And sitting there in the blue light with the towers humming their permanent shift, Lǐ Jūn understood that this was not an omission, and that Arjun had thought about it, probably for a long time, and had decided against it deliberately in the last free hour of his life. An archive that survived because its keeper was obeying someone would die the day the someone did. The method was the instruction. It had been the instruction since a closed lab at midnight: put the altered thing beside the true thing, date it, source it, annotate it in prose so dry that no tribunal could mistake it for rhetoric, and keep doing that until the day arrives when records matter more than they do today.

He did not learn about the arrest. Nobody was told about the arrest; that was the point of the instrument. He learned it the way the method had trained him to learn things, from an absence with a shape: the quantum ethics seminar reassigned at 17:10 to a lecturer in the applied division, with no explanation and no apology, the reassignment notice folded into the same term-end traffic as everything else, four lines long and perfectly tidy.

No residue.

Lǐ Jūn opened the notebook.

He wrote the hour the transmission arrived, and its channel, and its contents, and what had been done to the provenance and why he believed it had been done. He wrote the time of the seminar reassignment and the name of the lecturer who had received it and the fact that no cause was given. He wrote that he had no direct knowledge of where Arjun Patel was, and marked that sentence as the limit of what he knew, because a record that claimed one metre more than it could carry was worth less than a record that stopped honestly at its edge.

He did not write that it was unjust. The record did not need his adjectives; it needed his accuracy, and it would say the other thing by itself, later, in a room with better acoustics than this one.

His hand was steady, which surprised him, and his eyes were not, which did not.

He capped the pen, closed the notebook, and sat a while in the blue light, holding the shape of his own new life: the archive was his, entirely, from this hour; the man who had taught him the work was inside a system that issued no notifications; and the only thing that would ever get either of them out was the thing already sitting on the desk in front of him, ugly and complete and true.

The archive was one entry larger tonight.

The entry was his teacher.
