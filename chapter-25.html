The Mirror Bastion had been rearranged for guests, which meant it had not been rearranged at all: the same black lacquer, the same long curve of table, the same amber light strip running the room's perimeter and reading the vitals of everyone inside it — including, tonight, the vitals of six Sovereign Coalition officials whose consent to be read existed only in an annex of the security alignment treaty that none of them had personally signed. Rachel Iverson noted that they had all chosen seats along the table's far curve, backs to the wall, the way people sat in rooms they did not control. She had left them that side on purpose.

The file she had opened in this room was old enough now to have survived two quarterly reviews without anyone asking to hear it read aloud. That was the file working as designed. A contingency framework's entire value lay in being already written on the day the world changed — because on that day, the room would not want analysis. It would want something to authorise.

"Twenty-four days ago," she began, "a research programme conducted on Lunar Accord sovereign territory opened an uncontrolled dimensional aperture for forty-two seconds."

The wall behind her resolved the first image without being asked twice: the terraced field outside Shaoyang at dawn, mist in the low ground, the spar of rust-red wood lying across the wet green rows. Every person at the table had seen the photograph. That was precisely why she opened with it. You did not build an argument on evidence a room might dispute; you built it on evidence the room had already accepted before it walked in, and then you told the room what its own acceptance obligated it to do.

"Foreign material of confirmed extra-solar origin materialised at three sites on Earth's surface," she said. "One in the Coalition's Shaoyang hinterland. One in Lunar Accord Mediterranean territory. One in the unregulated Kazakh margins, which no one governs and no one monitors on the ground — an orbital instrument event that sat unwitnessed in empty steppe for four days before anyone stood beside it." She let that sit. "I ask the council to consider the fourth site — the one that did not happen this time. The aperture selected its exit points according to physics no agency in this room can model. The next selection could as easily be the Atlantic Financial Spine, or the Beijing Command's arcology ring. That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the absence of a model, stated plainly."

"The Lunar Accord characterises the event as a research accident." The Coalition's senior intelligence director was a spare, unhurried man who had spent the presentation's first ten minutes reading the room rather than the wall. "Contained, disclosed, suspended pending review. Their words."

"Their words," Rachel agreed, because agreeing with an objection was cheaper than pretending it did not exist. "And I will go further than they would expect me to: I believe the sincerity of most of the people saying them. Sincerity is not the issue before this council." She advanced the display. The Shaoyang photograph gave way to a resonance-signature trace, months of it, the sevenfold power draw she had first shown a smaller and more manageable audience in this same room. "Capability is. Here is a research programme that has demonstrably destabilised the physical reality of three sovereign territories — by accident, under its own safety protocols, while sincerely intending abundance for all mankind. I am not asking the council to judge Luna Haven's intentions. I am asking it to notice that the distinction between a weapons programme and a physics programme that keeps detonating is a distinction visible only from inside Luna Haven. From where the rest of the species is standing, there is no difference."

"You are proposing a strike." The intelligence director said it without inflection, a man laying a card face up to see what the table did.

"I am presenting a framework," Rachel said. "Contingency architecture, drafted under this board's classified file, reviewed quarterly, never yet acted upon. It defines a coordinated operation against the Meridian Levels research complex — the aperture site, nothing beyond it — conducted jointly under the security alignment treaty's mutual-defence provisions, on the legal theory that an uncontrolled dimensional breach constitutes an ongoing hazard to the territory of both signatories." The framework diagram assembled itself on the wall behind her, clean and grey and procedural, orbital assets and timing windows and rules of engagement rendered in the visual grammar of an infrastructure audit. She had built it to look that way. Violence, presented as engineering, let a room authorise it one specification at a time. "The framework requires four separate authorisations at four escalation thresholds. Tonight's session concerns only the first: whether joint operational planning may begin."

"And if the review concludes the accident was an accident?"

"Then the planning is never used, and it costs both blocs nothing but the planners' salaries." She said it mildly, and watched the sentence do its work along both curves of the table — the small, precise reframing that made yes feel like a hedge and no feel like a gamble. "But if the next aperture opens over a city, this council will be asked why the two most capable security frameworks on Earth spent the interval commissioning studies. I have sat in the rooms where that question gets asked afterward. I would rather sit in this one."

The silence that followed was the kind she had built the whole hour to produce: not the silence of persuasion, which was fragile, but the silence of people who had each privately concluded that agreement was the position requiring no defence.

The council did not vote. Councils of this kind never voted; voting created records of division, and the entire architecture of the joint session existed to produce its opposite. Instead the Corporate Union board's senior member glanced along the far curve, collected the Coalition director's fractional nod, and said, "Planning is authorised. Joint working group, classified at treaty level, progress review in thirty days." He did not phrase it as a question. Nobody in the room treated it as one.

Rachel gathered her tablet. Twenty rows of grey procedural text on a wall, six careful silhouettes, one sentence spoken in the flat register of scheduling — no part of it would have photographed as history. That was the craft of it. She had learned long ago that the decisions which determined outcomes were never the dramatic ones; drama was what a decision looked like after it was already too late to make a different one. The strike, if it ever came, would arrive wearing the same administrative face as tonight.

The amber strip read her vitals one last time on her way out, and said nothing about what it found.

The tanker was called Meridian Trade 7, it was flying a registered commercial resupply profile to a Corporate Union cislunar depot, and there was nothing wrong with it except that it was the fourth tanker in nine days to fly that profile to a depot that had spent the previous year drawing fuel at a rate consistent with running its station-keeping thrusters and not much else.

Sienna TeAriki stood at the launch-coordination floor's main display, high in the upper systems of the retasked Opera House, and looked at the depot's traffic history the way she had once learned to look at a seawall: not for the crack, but for the pattern of stress that told you where the crack was going to be.

"Show me thermal on the depot," she said.

"Six weeks of it." The Orbital Station's voice kept its register, warm and unhurried, a colleague who happened to be a building. "Radiator load is up forty per cent over baseline. Consistent with expanded life support. They filed a crew-rotation notice with the traffic authority — eighteen additional personnel, listed as maintenance."

"Maintenance crews don't ship in on four tankers' worth of reaction mass." She pulled the orbital chart across the display. The depot itself told her little; depots were allowed to be busy. It was the constellation around it that read wrong, the way a harbour read wrong before a storm not because any one boat was out of place but because all of them were slightly not where habit put them. Two Coalition surveillance platforms had drifted, over three weeks, in lawful increments, into orbits whose planes intersected above Luna Haven's northern hemisphere. A Corporate Union communications relay had been re-registered to a slot that made no commercial sense and excellent triangulation sense. Every filing was individually compliant. Nothing had been hidden, because nothing needed to be: it was all infrastructure, and infrastructure was boring, and boring was the best camouflage there had ever been.

But Sienna had spent sixteen years converting other people's decisions into physical logistics, and she knew what she was looking at, because she had built this exact pattern herself — from the other side, in evacuation season, pre-positioning fuel and spares and bed capacity ahead of a surge the public forecasts had not yet named. Somebody, somewhere, in a room she would never see, had stopped discussing and started provisioning.

She did not have confirmation. She was precise with herself about that. There were no intercepts, no defectors, no signed orders — only mass and heat and orbital mechanics arranging themselves into the shape of a decision. If she took this to the council as an alarm, it would arrive as an accusation the blocs could disprove line by line, filing by lawful filing, and Luna Haven would have spent its political credibility purchasing nothing.

So she did not raise an alarm. She did what the almanac could not argue with.

"Open my logistics queue," she said. "New maintenance schedule, standing authority, my code. First item: the relay spares inventory at the northern junctions is overdue for redistribution. Move a full replacement set to each of the six primary nodes — call it corrosion-cycle rotation."

"Logged as scheduled maintenance." A pause, one beat longer than processing required. "The corrosion cycle isn't due for five months."

"Then we'll be early. Second item: the supply run consolidation we shelved in the spring — unshelve it. I want no single-point routings left on the priority chains by month's end. Every critical delivery gets a second path that doesn't share infrastructure with its first."

"Drafted. That one will read as an efficiency programme."

"It is an efficiency programme." She watched the queue assemble itself down the display's margin, item by item, each one true, each one defensible, each one wearing its dullest possible name. "Third: run a capacity audit on the Gardens Relay treaty channel. Quietly. If we ever had to route primary traffic through Neo Singapore, I want to know tonight what would break, and I want it fixed under the audit's remit before anyone has a reason to ask why."

"Audit scheduled." Another pause. "For the record, Sienna: taken together, this queue looks like something."

"Taken together, everything looks like something." Below the coordination floor's long window the Hub went about its evening — the grown lattice of the concourse roof holding the last light along its ribs, the harbour skiffs stacking at the platforms in their ordinary patient queues, and beyond the breakwater the low grey line of the drowned suburbs that nobody had rebuilt, keeping their own kind of record of what happened when the sea's intentions were debated instead of provisioned for. "Log the queue exactly as filed. Maintenance, efficiency, audit."

She stayed at the display after the floor system had gone back to its rounds, one hand resting on the console's worn edge, and gave herself thirty seconds to feel the size of it — the particular loneliness of being the person who saw the pattern first and could prove none of it, whose only honest option was to fix seals and move spares and widen channels so that whatever was being decided in rooms on the other side of the world would arrive, if it arrived, against a system already quietly braced to survive it.

Then the tide gauges wanted her attention, because the sea did not care what the orbital chart was doing, and she gave it to them.

The migration reached the Basin's western floor an hour after first light, and Kaelin Drayke was already on the ridge above it with his survey grid live, his notebook open, and no intention of telling anyone why.

Below him the megafauna came through the rust-red scrub in their long, unhurried columns — forty-one animals in the lead group, he counted them twice — following paths that no hoof had cut and no instinct alone could explain, paths that on his overlay ran in load-bearing curves across the Basin like the transit maps of a city no one had built. Six years he had been documenting those paths, and for most of those six years he had filed them under instinct, because instinct was the conclusion that cost nothing. Only this season had the data finally held the weight of the other word — infrastructure: designed, maintained by use, responsive to the empathy-waves that moved through the herds like weather — and in all six years he had catalogued the Basin the way a man catalogues what he believes he will be allowed to study forever: thoroughly, patiently, at the pace of a career.

The letter had ended the career pace. It rode in his jacket's inner pocket now: nine pages in Misha Fedorov's angular hand, printed from the facsimile the historian had sent through the research relay within a week of crossing back to Luna Haven — nine pages that held the distilled reading of a geological archive three hundred thousand years old. Kaelin had read them four times. The fourth time, he had stopped being able to pretend the survey he was planning was science as usual.

Because the record said the Preserve had defended itself before. That was the sentence he kept returning to — not the origin of the parasite, not the ruin of the civilisation that had made it, but the quieter operational fact buried in Misha's careful prose: the ecology's response to coercion is not anger but closure. Sufficient aggression is answered with a sealing that is permanent, and the record's authors regarded this not as a punishment but as an immune function, no more negotiable than fever. And twenty-four days ago, on the far side of the boundary, human beings had torn an uncontrolled hole in the Meridian, and the blocs whose photographs of rust-red wood were now the most reproduced images in history were, if Kaelin understood anything about how frightened institutions behaved, not finished escalating.

He did not know where the threshold was. Neither had the record's authors — that was the terrible elegance of an immune system; it did not publish its triggers. He only knew that on the day the door sealed, if it sealed, everything unrecorded on this side of it would become permanently unknowable: every species, every migration corridor, every resonance-bearing structure in the Basin's living architecture. Three hundred thousand years of the only ecology in the known universe that answered consciousness with consciousness — surviving afterward only as whatever some field biologist had managed to write down in time.

So he wrote it down.

He worked the grid west to east as the light climbed, and he catalogued as if cataloguing were a form of prayer he had only just learned the words to: the forty-one animals of the lead column and their spacing, tighter than seasonal norm; the secondary paths' junction nodes, where the vegetation grew in reinforced whorls like the grain around a knot; the small burrowing species whose warren mouths aligned, all of them, with the migration curves, a detail he had noticed years ago and never given its own file. Everything got its own file now. He photographed, sampled where sampling took nothing the Basin would miss, and filled page after page in a hand that grew less legible as the hours passed, because his hand had understood before he had that the deadline was real and unpublishable.

Midmorning, Alia Solís found him on the shared channel. "Your telemetry's been uploading since dawn," she said, from the Empathy Flats, forty kilometres of cobalt sky away. "Full-spectrum survey protocol. That's a month of fieldwork you've queued in a day, Kaelin. What's the occasion?"

He looked down the ridge at the columns moving through the scrub, at the paths that looked like infrastructure because they were infrastructure, and he made the second hard choice of the day. Alia read botanical stress the way he read migration; if he told her what was in the nine pages, she would understand within a sentence, and then there would be two people in the Preserve working with an urgency they could not explain, and the Guardians missed nothing, and the researchers' access here rested on a trust he was not certain would survive the blocs' next month. The letter was not his to spend. Not yet.

"Baseline consolidation," he said. "The cascade shifted the migration calendar eleven days off seasonal norm. If the Basin's rewriting its own schedules, I want the before-picture finished while it's still true."

A pause on the channel, exactly long enough to tell him she had noticed the answer was shaped like a real reason without quite being one. "Then send me your junction-node coordinates," was all she said. "If you're building a before-picture, it should have roots in it."

"You'll have them by tonight." And she would — that much of the catalogue he could share without sharing why, and her botanical survey would thicken the record he was building, and if the worst came, her data would survive on the far side of the boundary inside his transmissions, credited, complete. Documentation as survival. Misha had written that in the letter's last line, almost as an aside: the record we read had one author who understood in time. Be that author, Dr Drayke.

The lead column crossed the grid's eastern margin in the early afternoon, and Kaelin stood for a moment without writing anything — one man on a ridge in a preserved world older than his species, under a sky the colour of deep water, watching forty-one impossible animals walk a road that consciousness had built. The wonder of it had not diminished in six years. That was the cruelty of the assignment: the Basin had never been more beautiful than it was now that he had learned to see it as something that could be lost by someone else's decision, in a room he would never enter, on a world that thought of this place — when it thought of it at all — as a photograph of red wood in a green field.

He opened a new page, and dated it, and kept going. There was a great deal of Basin left, and no way to know how many days the door had left in it, and the only honest response to either fact was the next entry, and the next, and the next.
