MARTIAN PRESERVE — COBALT BASIN, ACCESS GATE / Misha Fedorov — Preserve day 1 · Luna Haven +0h

The crossing did not ask for courage. It asked for calm, which Misha Fedorov had spent four months learning was a different and much rarer thing.

He had made the approach eleven times by remote session — his consciousness threaded through the Meridian Levels' interface while his body sat in a padded chair on Luna Haven, a technician monitoring his vitals and a mug of tea going cold at his elbow. He knew the protocols the way he knew declensions: by drill, until the knowing moved below thought. Breathe. Settle. Bring the mind to the state the boundary would accept, which was not blankness and not serenity but something closer to honesty — the state of a man not carrying anything he was pretending not to carry.

None of it had prepared him for arriving in his own body.

The light hit him first. Luna Haven's light was teal and tended, a kindness engineered into every corridor; he had lived under it long enough that his eyes had quietly given up expecting anything else. The Cobalt Basin's light was not tended. It fell out of a deep blue sky onto rust-red terrain with a saturation that struck him as almost impolite, colour laid on with no regard for what a human retina had been told to expect, and Misha stood at the threshold of the access point with his equipment bag in one hand and found that his scholarship had gone entirely out of him, and what was left was a man in his fifties blinking at a world.

The air was thin and smelled of cold iron and, under the iron, something green. Where the access structure's grown lattice met open ground, dust drifted from his boots in slow, low-gravity plumes and settled with a patience that made his own breathing sound hurried. He made himself stand still until the two rhythms agreed.

"So," he said to no one, in Russian, because some observations were only accurate in one's first language. "It's real."

It has been real for a very long time, said the light to his left.

He had been briefed on Kythara. Everyone who worked the Preserve programme was briefed on Kythara, and every briefing failed the same way, because the briefings were written by people trying to describe a presence in the vocabulary of a body. There was no body. There was a place where the air over the rust-red ground had gathered itself into a faint, structured shimmer — the way heat organises light above a summer road, if the heat had intent — and there was a voice that did not arrive through his ears so much as through the same channel the boundary had used to read him a moment before.

"Kythara." He inclined his head, then felt foolish, then decided he did not mind feeling foolish. "Four months of sessions. I believe we've spoken for something over ninety hours."

Ninety-one. Though speech is a generous word for what the relay permits. The shimmer drifted, unhurried, in the direction of the Basin's western rim, where the land rose toward a darker mass of canopy he knew from orbital imagery and from dreams he had not admitted to the medical staff. You have been reading us at a distance, Misha Fedorov. The record you have come for cannot be read at a distance. The Grove is half a day's walk. The walk is part of the reading.

He looked at the canopy on the rim, then down at his own hands — ink-stained at the fingertips, a lifetime's occupational tattoo no dermal treatment had ever been asked to remove — and he understood, with the suddenness of a word finally parsing, that he was afraid. Not of the place. Of the possibility that he would not be equal to what the place had kept.

"Then we should walk," he said.

MARTIAN PRESERVE — COUNCIL GROVE, ANCIENT GROVE / Misha Fedorov — Preserve day 1 · Luna Haven +5h

The Grove did not have light so much as a light logic. The canopy was dense enough that the deep cobalt of the sky arrived at the floor only in long, cool columns, and the rust-red trunks — trees older than agriculture, older than speech, standing in an atmosphere that should not have permitted them — parted those columns the way pillars part the light in a cathedral that has outlived its religion.

Misha set his camp at the Grove's edge, because it had seemed important not to presume, and Kythara had neither confirmed nor corrected the instinct, which he had already learned was its way of confirming.

The record system was not a chamber, or a device, or anything his prepared vocabulary could be attached to. It was the Grove itself — or rather, it was a stratum of the Grove, a depth at which the root systems and the stone beneath them held resonance the way sediment holds ash: in layers, in order, without comment. Kythara had described it in the remote sessions as a geological-resonance archive, and Misha had dutifully written the phrase down and understood nothing. Sitting now with his back against a root-wall, his notebook open on his knee, and his palm laid flat on the cool grown surface at Kythara's direction, he understood it the way one understands a bell by being inside it when it rings.

The first signal moved through him and his hand jerked off the root as if burned. It was not pain. It was structure — an ordered pressure that rose through his palm and bloomed somewhere behind his sternum, freighted with pattern the way a sentence is freighted with grammar, and gone before he could hold any of it.

Too fast, Kythara said. The shimmer stood among the trunks a few metres off, patient as weather. That was the record at its own pace. I will slow it. What I cannot do is interpret it. Understand this before we begin, because it is the condition of your being here: I can render the record's structure into forms your nervous system can carry. Pressure. Tone. Shape. What the structures mean — that work is yours. We have had three hundred thousand years to listen to this record, and we are not its native readers either.

Misha flexed his hand, laid it back on the root, and did what he had done in every archive he had ever entered, from the water-damaged monastery registries of his doctoral years to the quantum-indexed state records of Beijing Command that he was officially not supposed to have read. He stopped hoping to understand, and started counting.

Recursion first. Any record made on purpose repeats itself — form is the first fossil of intent. By the time the Grove's filtered columns had swung visibly across the floor, he had eleven pages of notation in a shorthand no one else alive could read: pressure-forms sorted by shape, by duration, by the intervals at which they returned. There was a unit. There was something that behaved like a boundary marker, a form that closed other forms the way a cadence closes a phrase. There was — his pen paused over the page when he realised what the pattern of returns implied — there was address. A recurring opening form, high in the structure, that framed everything after it the way a vocative frames a sentence.

The record was not a chronicle. Chronicles face backward. This structure faced forward. It was built, whatever else it was, to be found.

"It's addressed," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the Grove, small and dry. "To someone who wasn't there when it was made. Kythara — this was written to us. Not to us — to whoever came. To anyone who came."

Yes, Kythara said, and in the single syllable Misha heard, for the first time in ninety-one hours of conversation, something he could only have transcribed as grief handled carefully, like an artefact. You are reading a letter, historian. We have always known that much. Read on.

MARTIAN PRESERVE — COUNCIL GROVE, ANCIENT GROVE / Misha Fedorov — Preserve day 3 · Luna Haven +12h

By the third day he had stopped being able to say where the method ended and the reading began.

He slept at the Grove's edge when Kythara made him — the Guardian regulated his sessions with the firmness of a sanatorium nurse, and had twice simply withdrawn the record's rendering mid-form, deaf to his objections, when his hands had begun to tremble against the root-wall. He ate field rations he did not afterward remember eating. Around his camp, he noticed, the Grove's fine copper dust had arranged itself overnight into faint concentric ripples, centred on the place where he sat to work; he chose not to mention it, and suspected the Preserve of noticing the choice.

The grammar had opened on the second day, the way languages always opened for him — not gradually but all at once, a jammed door giving, forty hours of inventory resolving into syntax between one pressure-form and the next. What had come through since then, in Kythara's slowed and patient rendering, form by form, was this:

They had built the Meridian. That was the first thing the record wanted known — wanted, there was no other honest verb; the address-form recurred before every major section like a hand laid on the reader's arm. The builders had made the thing that human physics had been circling for a decade, and they had made it not as a door but as an instrument: a mechanism for consciousness to meet consciousness across the distances that matter, which were never, the record made clear in its dry structural way, distances of space.

And then, at what should have been the height of their competence, they had attempted the thing the instrument made thinkable. A collective crossing. The merging of many minds into a shared, continuous awareness — not as conquest, not even as survival, but as what they understood to be the next honest step. The record's forms for this section were the most beautiful in the archive, and Misha had filled four pages before he understood why that beauty kept unsettling him: the forms were celebratory. This section had been composed in advance. They had written the announcement before the event, the way a wedding is written before the vows.

The sections after it were composed in a different hand.

That was not the right word and he wrote it anyway, because forty years of craft had taught him to trust the wrong words that insisted. The later forms were compressed, hurried, structurally simplified — a high language collapsing under load, keeping only what it could carry. And what it carried was an accounting.

The merge had not failed. That was the horror the record circled with such care, and Misha put his pen down when the shape of it finally came through whole. The merge had worked, for those who had entered it in truth — genuinely, willingly, with their interiority intact and offered. But the crossing had been a civilisation's crossing, and a civilisation is never unanimous. Some had been unready. Some had been unwilling and said so and been included anyway, in the way institutions include people, with procedures in place of consent. Some had consented the way one consents at the end of an argument, hollowly, to make the asking stop.

And the instrument had done precisely what it was built to do. It had taken the pattern of every participant as the truth of them. From the willing it had taken presence. From the unwilling, from the hollow, from the coerced, it had taken the shape of presence with no one inside it — care's architecture, empty; connection's structure, vacant — and it had propagated that shape through the merged awareness along every channel the merge had opened, which was every channel of love and attention the civilisation possessed.

They had not been invaded. Nothing had come for them out of the dark between dimensions. They had manufactured their own ending out of the difference between authentic union and enforced inclusion, and the record's final sections existed because some remnant of them had stayed unmerged long enough to write down what the difference was.

"It's still here," Misha said quietly. It was not a question. Three days of the record's structure had taught him how the Preserve fit around what the record described — why a paradise this old was also, everywhere he had walked in it, a perimeter. "The residue. The pattern they made. You've been holding it."

We were made to hold it. Kythara's shimmer had not moved in hours, a stillness that Misha had learned to read as full attention. What the record calls the emptied shape persists where the merge failed — quarantined, at cost, for longer than your species has had writing. I must be exact with you, historian, in the way you have been exact with the record: what you have read is your interpretation of a structure. We believe the interpretation is sound. We have believed so for three hundred thousand years. But the record is a translation of a translation, and the thing itself does not explain. You will want to present this to your colleagues as history. Present it as what it is — a warning, in a dead language, about an extant fact.

Misha looked at his notebook. Twenty-nine pages of shorthand, and beneath the shorthand, pressing up through it, the thing the whole Grove had been built to keep and the whole record built to say: they were destroyed by what they became, and they became it by refusing to distinguish the willing from the included.

On Luna Haven, at this hour, Jīn Wú's team was refining protocols for deliberate resonance work. In New Manhattan and Beijing Command, other teams were refining other protocols, with other attitudes toward consent. Half a day of their time had passed while he sat here learning why that difference was not a policy question.

"I need to sit somewhere with a horizon," he said.

MARTIAN PRESERVE — COBALT BASIN, WESTERN RIM / Misha Fedorov — Preserve day 3 · Luna Haven +14h

The Basin at evening was a bowl of embered light. From the rim below the Grove the rust-red plain ran out for kilometres under the darkening cobalt sky, and the wind coming up the slope carried iron and that persistent, impossible green, and far out on the open ground a herd of grazing animals no human taxonomy would ever properly hold moved through the last of the day like a sentence being written in a language still alive.

Misha sat on a shelf of iron-oxide stone with his notebook shut on his knee and did not write anything for a long time.

He had spent his working life among the records of ended things. It had made him, he knew, a particular kind of man — his wife had said, in the last year they were married, that he loved the dead because the dead had finished making their mistakes, and he had written the sentence down, which had rather proven her point. Every archive he had ever opened had been an ending looked at from afterward. The distances had been part of the method. You could be precise about Carthage. Carthage was not still deciding.

This was the first archive that had read him back. The first ending that was not finished — that sat quarantined half a kilometre beneath his boots and propagated in principle through every merged awareness anyone would ever build, patient as the dust, waiting for the next civilisation that could not tell the difference between a hand offered and a hand closed around a wrist.

The light went down by slow degrees. The dust at his feet had settled into stillness, and he noticed — with a small interior laugh at the Preserve, at himself, at the mirror this place couldn't help being — that so had he.

Somewhere behind him in the Grove, five ancient intelligences kept a three-hundred-thousand-year appointment. Ahead of him, hours away through the access point, was a research programme that believed its most dangerous problem was dimensional physics, and two fragmented technicians in a medical bay whose condition was the old catastrophe in miniature, and blocs full of serious people refining the machinery of enforced inclusion because it looked, from inside, like security.

They did not need his grief. They needed the letter delivered.

Misha opened the notebook to a clean page. His pen hovered, and he thought of the record's address-form, that recurring shape like a hand on the reader's arm, composed by someone who had stayed out of paradise long enough to leave a warning on its door — and he did what translators had always done, and borrowed the opening of a better writer.

For those who come after, he wrote, in Russian, in the rust-coloured last of the light. So they understand what was at stake.

Then he sat in the Basin's deepening evening, a man in his fifties in a preserved world older than his species, and he began, form by form, page by page, to write it all down.
