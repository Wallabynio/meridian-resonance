Fractured Horizons · Chapter 05

The first quiet

Fractured Horizons · I. The voice

05

3 MarchKaelinMars, the Preserve's boundary at Cobalt BasinMars delay 9.0 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Kaelin Drake — Earth 9.0 min

Petra said the drill was cheating.

"It's a drill," said Kaelin.

"It's a drill that knows it's being counted." She had her helmet lamp on the board by the core rack, where the morning's cores were chalked in two columns: the ones rig three brought up on its own, and the ones she'd had to go into the collar and free by hand. Hers was longer. "Four jams before ten. It's doing it on purpose. It wants me to look bad in front of the Charter."

"It wants a new core lifter. So do I."

"Then it can ask nicely."

The wind came over the basin rim thin as breath and laid a skin of red dust on anything that held still. It kept coming, all day, the finest grit on Mars settling into seals and sensor heads and the creases of everyone's suits.

Every ten minutes somebody walked the rigs with a soft brush and wiped the lenses clean by hand.

Three rigs. Six crew. Two bores already cased to depth and capped with the survey team's coupling heads, squat grey drums with cables running back to the array frame. The third bore was at two hundred and ten metres and going down.

Kaelin looked at Petra's left glove.

The seal tape over the second knuckle had a patch on it, and the patch had a patch, and the third had been laid half over the second, the tape gone the colour of the dust.

"Your glove."

"Third patch. It's outlasted two ships." She flexed it. "Store's got four seals and a lock on it till the window. Ines signs them out for blood, not knuckles."

"I'll ask her."

"She'll say no. She'll say it nicely, and then she'll take your chair." Petra tipped her helmet toward the berm road. "Your guests."

The survey team's rovers came down off the ridge in a line, lamps on in daylight because the dust made a brown dusk of everything past a kilometre. Kaelin watched the ramps drop and counted them off without meaning to. Eight from the first. Eight. Six. Nine. Nine. Forty, in Charter grey with the Foresight mark on every shoulder, stepping down onto the basin floor and looking about them as new people did, as if the ground might move.

Forty. He had counted them once before, as a number on an ops screen. He hadn't thought he would ever have to count them as faces.

One came straight to him. A tall woman, her name stencilled across her chest plate: FERRAND.

"Mr Drake." Her voice on the band was crisp and warm and a little out of breath. "Anouk Ferrand. Survey lead." She put out a hand, then remembered the gloves and left it there anyway, and he shook it. "I was on the Alexandria. Seat fourteen. I've wanted to say this since January."

"You don't have to."

"I do. Thank you." She said it plainly and meant it. Then her helmet turned to rig three. "Two hundred and ten?"

"And eleven, if she stops jamming."

"We need three at depth by the twelfth for the heads, or the calibration slides and everything behind it slides. Then we're out of your air." She almost laughed. "I've seen your shower log. I know what forty of us cost."

Seat fourteen. Funmi on her knees at a hand wheel, forty turns, twenty, ten. He put it back where he kept it.

"You'll have your bore," he said.

· · ·

Aur'k came out at eleven in the boundary rover, alone, and walked across the site without stopping at the rack or the frame or anyone. He came to a halt at Kaelin's shoulder, the rust-red plating of his chassis furred with dust, and turned his whole helm toward rig three.

"Stop that bore."

Petra looked up from the collar. Ferrand, at the array frame, looked over.

"Why?"

"Because each time the bit goes in, the boundary answers." Aur'k held up a forearm and laid a trace across Kaelin's visor: the rock's ordinary hiss, and in it, a row of small clean rises, evenly spaced. "Those are this morning's runs. The substrate moves when the bit moves. In the same second."

"Rock moves when you drill it."

"Not like this. This rock is alive, Kaelin."

"We know it's biologically active," Ferrand said, walking over. She sounded pleased to be asked. "That's the point. The Lantern calibrates against a living boundary, or it can't hear anything. The protocol accounts for it."

"The protocol was written in New York," Aur'k said, "by people who have never stood on it."

"With respect, it was written by the people who are standing on it now."

Kaelin watched the trace. He didn't know how to read it the way Aur'k did, and he knew he didn't. What he could read was the date by the twelfth, and forty faces, and a shower log, and a supply line that had already brought half what it promised once this year.

"Tell me what happens if we keep going," he said. "Not what might. What will."

Aur'k was silent.

It went on long enough that Ferrand shifted her weight. Kaelin had stood in a narrow blue room in January and waited out a silence like it, and he knew this one. Aur'k had the number. He was deciding how much of it to say.

"I can't tell you that," Aur'k said.

"Then I can't stop a bore on it."

Aur'k's helm stayed on him a moment longer. Then he keyed the open band, so that every suit on the site heard the click.

"Boundary lead, Cobalt Basin, to the Signatories, by post. Formal objection to boring at the Lantern site, bore three, on substrate response. Trace attached. Reply when you can."

The band clicked shut. Nobody on the site said anything. The wind laid dust on all of them.

"File it," Kaelin said, a few seconds after there was any point. Then, to the collar: "Petra. Keep it turning."

· · ·

The answers came back after three, two posts a minute apart, one for each bloc.

Kaelin read them on the rig board, out in the dust, with Aur'k beside him reading the same screen. The Continuity's came first, from the Signatories' desk, in a font that looked like a stamp.

Objection received and noted. Schedule confirmed. Proceed.

The Compact's came second, and it was longer, because it was trying to be kind.

The Compact thanks its citizen for his diligence and care for the Preserve. Having reviewed the trace with Foresight's engineers, we concur that the schedule should stand. Reply when you can.

Aur'k read it twice. Kaelin watched him do it, the small stillness at the end of each pass.

"They were quick," Aur'k said.

"Plenty of time to agree."

"They had time to agree, and to thank me." He closed the screen. "That's the Compact. It always says thank you on the way past."

Then the rig alarm went.

Petra was already moving. Bore three had stopped dead, the drive whining at the top of its note, and the inner tube had come up on the wireline and jammed in the casing head, a metre down, with the core still in it. She killed the drive. In the silence Kaelin could hear the wind ticking grit against his helmet.

"Brine," Petra said. "It's coming up round the tube. Lifter's caught on the shoe." She was on her knees at the collar already, lamp down the throat of it. "If that freezes round the tube we lose the bore."

"How long?"

"Ten minutes. Less. It's eighty below and the brine's warm." She glanced up at him. "Warmer."

Six on the rig, forty survey staff on the site, and only one of them had cleared that shoe four times since breakfast. Ferrand was standing at his elbow with the date in her eyes, not saying it. Aur'k had gone completely still.

Petra's left hand was resting on the rim of the collar, three patches on the knuckle.

"I'll go in," Kaelin said.

"You've got hands like shovels. You'll jam it worse." She was already reaching for the release bar. "I know where the teeth are. Two minutes."

He looked at the glove. He looked at the frost already starting to feather round the collar's lip where the brine breathed out. Nine days to the twelfth. Forty faces.

He pulled the tape off his thigh and wound it round her knuckle, three turns over the three patches. It was the best he had.

"Pole," he said. "Two minutes. Pole."

She went in to the shoulder.

He counted for her. Fifteen. Thirty. He heard the tube knock and give, and her grunt, and the lifter teeth scraping, and then she said "Got it," and then she said nothing at all.

Her suit sang a breach tone on the band, thin and high.

"Petra."

She came up out of the collar in one low bounce with her left hand held out in front of her, and Kaelin had the tape round her glove again before he had finished thinking. The patches had split on the lifter's teeth, all of them, his tape too. Through the gap, for the second it took him to close it, he saw the tip of her middle finger, white with frost, and on it, wet and faintly shining, a smear of blue.

The core had come up with the tube. It lay in the catcher, a cylinder of dark wet rock with veins through it the blue of a gas flame turned down low, glowing in the brown light. The lattice. He had never seen it this close in daylight.

He wound the tape twice more. The tone stopped.

"That's going to bruise," he said. "Your fingertip's going to come up in little red spots tomorrow, and Ines is going to laugh at you."

Petra didn't answer. She was kneeling very still, her hand in his, her helmet tipped a little to one side.

"Petra."

"Oh," she said.

Then she laughed, surprised, like someone a stranger in a crowd has just called by name.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's eighty below."

"I know." She took her hand back and looked at it. "I know it is." She looked at the core in the catcher, and then up at him, and grinned. "Two minutes. Mark it on my side."

He marked it. Her column was longer by one. Behind him, Ferrand laughed on the band, shaky, and said thank you to the sky, and Aur'k said nothing.

· · ·

A week later the station was full of the smell of other people's soap.

The queue for the shower ran round the corner of the passage, and the log had grown a second page. The galley at evening meal was standing room, and Kaelin stood, his back to the hatch, eating greenhouse greens out of the pan because the plates were all in use.

The screen above the slate was showing Lagos. Earth's news, late as always: the lagoon flat and white under a white sky, crowds under the misting lines on the lagoon bridges, a figure in the corner of the frame that kept climbing. Somebody at the survey table said it was the highest ever measured in a city that size. Somebody else said they'd been saying that every year since they were born.

The Earth-night slate was mostly Charter now, forty new names in neat Spire hands, all of them betting on Lagos as if it were a sport. Funmi's column was still there at the left edge, her last bet underlined twice. Nobody had crossed it out. Nobody had written over it either. Even the survey team had worked that out by themselves.

Petra had a marker and an opinion.

"Funmi read the Battery's gauges," she was telling a young engineer who had made the mistake of asking. "Everybody knew. She had them in her overlay, I watched her eyes go, she used to sit right there and—"

She stopped.

She hadn't lost her place. Her head tilted, as if someone had spoken in the next room and she wanted to hear it. The engineer waited, then looked at Kaelin.

Kaelin put the pan down.

"Petra."

"Sorry." She came back with a little shake, and smiled. "Sorry. Where was I. Funmi had the—" She lifted her hand with the marker in it and looked at it, and her face went puzzled and then, slowly, amused. "Pass me the. The. Thing I'm holding."

She put it down in her lap, and her hands stayed there with it, still.

Ines was already beside her. She laid a hand flat on Petra's shoulder and crouched to her eye line, knees cracking like dry sticks.

"Hear that? That's my knees filing a complaint. Look at me, sweetheart." She shone a light in one eye and then the other. "What's my name?"

"Ines. Your knees are older than the rest of you."

It was the wrong way round. Ines's mouth tightened. "Close enough," she said.

Petra laughed. "I'm fine. Honestly. It's like when you're falling asleep and someone's talking low in the next bunk, and you can't hear the words, but you know it's about you, and it's nice." She frowned a little. "It's nice."

Ines looked at Kaelin over Petra's head. She had looked at him like that across a lot of bunks.

"I've had her on the scanner three times this week," she said. "Bloods, lace diagnostics, the lot. There is nothing wrong with this girl that I can find."

"Then keep looking."

"I am going to keep looking. I'm telling you what I've found so far. Nothing. So you don't stand there thinking I'm hiding one."

She glanced at Petra's left hand, the fingertip still mottled with small dark spots. "I said no to a seal for her last week. Ask me now."

Ferrand had come over from the survey table with her tray. She looked at Petra and her voice was gentle. "I'm sorry. I didn't know she was unwell." Then: "Mr Drake. Three's at four hundred and ninety. Two more days to depth and the heads go in. The sweeps are clean on one and two. Two days and we're out of your air."

Aur'k had been standing at the hatch. Kaelin hadn't heard him come. He came forward now into the light.

"Stop drilling," he said. He wasn't talking to Ferrand. "Stop the sweeps, and stop the drilling, and send to Earth that you did."

The galley went still around them. Forty people from New York, and the station's own.

Kaelin looked at Petra. She was looking at the marker in her lap as if it might tell her its name.

He could stop it. He had the authority on the surface, and nobody on Earth could argue with him until the lag came round.

And when it did they would, and in a day they would put someone in his place who wouldn't, and the forty would be here another month instead of two days, breathing the station's air and drinking its water and sleeping in its bunks, every one of them alive because Funmi had stayed at a wheel. He didn't know what was wrong with Petra. Nobody did. Aur'k might, and Aur'k wouldn't say.

"The drilling goes on," he said. "Petra's off the rig. As of now."

Aur'k didn't move.

Petra looked up. "Who's going to clear the jams?"

"Me."

"You've got hands like shovels."

"Then I'll jam it worse, and you can mark it on your side." He crouched in front of her, beside Ines. "Listen to me. I don't know what this is. Ines doesn't. That's all of it. That's the truth."

She thought about it.

"That's a rubbish bet," she said.

"I know."

Behind him, Aur'k turned and went out through the hatch, and the galley started talking again, a little too loudly, about Lagos.

· · ·

Ines called him at two, station time, six hours after the galley, and said, "Come and look at this," and closed the line.

Medical was dim. Five bunks, four empty. Petra lay in the fifth with a scanner cap over her scraped-up hair and the thread down her neck lit faintly where the cap read it, asleep, one hand open on the blanket. Ines had carried the good chair down from the galley, and she sat beside her in it with one hand flat on Petra's shin.

"I couldn't find anything," Ines said, without looking round. "So I stopped looking at the bits and left it running. Six hours." She lifted her other hand and the overlay came up between them.

It was a trace of Petra's brain through the night. Most of it was the ordinary weather of a sleeping mind, rising and falling without pattern. And through it, under it, ran a slow swell. It came up, and held, and went down. Then nothing. Then it came up again.

"There's your nothing," Ines said. "Every thirty-one minutes. Regular as a pump."

Kaelin didn't need to count it. He had built a week around that number. Thirty-one minutes between sweeps at the array, every crew cleared from the collars before each one, the schedule chalked at the top of the rig board in his own hand. He looked at his wrist. The next sweep was forty seconds out.

Neither of them said anything.

At the array, six kilometres away, a coupling head sent its signal down into the living rock. On the overlay, Petra's trace began to rise.

In the bunk, Petra smiled in her sleep. Her lips moved. It wasn't a word Kaelin knew.

"Tell me that's a coincidence," Ines said.

He couldn't. He didn't.

His hand had gone to the band at his collar. One call to ops, and the sweeps would stop, and bore three, and the forty would stay.

The swell held, and went down. Petra's hand lay open on the blanket.

He took his hand away from his collar.

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she got up out of the good chair and pushed it toward him with her foot. It was the closest she had ever come to giving it to anyone.

He sat down beside Petra, and put his hand flat on the blanket next to hers, and started counting to the next one.
