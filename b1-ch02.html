Fractured Horizons · Chapter 02

The Ice-Works

Fractured Horizons · I. The voice

02

12–13 JanuaryKaelinMars, Cobalt Basin Station and the Ice-WorksMars delay 13.2 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Kaelin Drake — Earth 13.2 min

Funmi wrote her bet on the slate before the feed had settled, and Petra said she was cheating.

"I'm reading the sky," Funmi said.

"You're reading the Battery's tide gauges. You've got them in your overlay. I watched your eyes go."

"I've got eyes. That's allowed." Funmi capped the marker and stood back. Her column ran down the cargo panel beside the galley hatch, past the stencilled load ratings and the dent where somebody had once dropped a crate, longer than anyone's by a hand. Rain till dawn. Wind off the harbour by six. Surge over the Polder sill by seven. She underlined the last one twice. She had taken Earth night seven times in eight, and she wanted the eighth.

On the screen above the slate, New York was minutes old and wet. A street camera on the Polder showed solar canopies streaming, a pod lane shining black, the wall at the end of the street with its work lights on. January rain, on a city that had once had snow.

Kaelin had grown up on the Reef Coast, where January meant cyclone shutters and the sea coming in under the door, and he still couldn't watch weather without counting who was out in it.

Two. A woman under an awning. A man walking a dog that had opinions about it.

"Kaelin is burning it," said Aur'k.

"I'm searing it."

"You are carbonising it. Searing has a stopping point." Aur'k turned his whole helm toward the pan, as he did toward anything he meant to correct. "That is the third error. The first was the oil. The second was the hot sauce."

"The hot sauce was the point."

"There are four bottles of hot sauce on this planet, and you put a third of one into a pan with nothing to protect it."

Kaelin scraped. The potatoes were the station's own, grown under lights, small and knobbly and worth defending. Most of them came up. Some had joined the pan for good.

When he turned round, Ines was in his chair.

It was the only chair in the galley with arms, salvaged from a lander seat in some year before any of them, and it had been the good chair ever since. Ines sat in it with her knees drawn up and her sleeves rolled, eating off his plate.

"I was in that," he said.

"You were at the stove. You can't hold a chair from a stove. That's medicine." She tried a potato and considered it. "My knees are forty-seven and the rest of me is older. Respect your elders. Also, this is burnt."

"Seared."

"Funmi, put me down for no surge," Ines said. "The wall holds. Walls hold. That's the whole job of a wall."

"Sucker's bet," Funmi said, and wrote it in Ines's column, where it looked lonely.

Petra was up with the other marker. She was the youngest face at the station, her hair scraped up, the thin line of her thread showing down her neck, and her hands never stopped. "I want it on the record that Funmi has won six of the last eight—"

"Seven."

"—seven of the last eight, and nobody wins seven of eight on New York weather. New York doesn't win seven of eight on New York weather."

"Funmi wins because she listens," Kaelin said, and sat on a step-crate with what was left. "I lose because I'm an optimist."

"You lose because you bet on sun in January," said Aur'k.

"Somebody has to."

Funmi laughed low in her chest and pressed her thumb beside the underlined surge as if she were sealing it. Her braids were tied back for work, and there was Ice-Works grit in the creases of her knuckles that never came all the way out. Nine years on her knees out east. She said it was the only honest way to talk to a drill.

The galley speaker clicked.

"Field operations." Command's duty voice, clipped and flat. "Priority post, Earthside, Charter routing. Field-ops lead and boundary lead to ops. Now."

For half a second nobody moved. Then Petra put her marker down.

Kaelin was already counting. Funmi at the slate. Ines in his chair. Petra. Aur'k by the stove, gone completely still. Him. Five in the galley. Eight out at the Ice-Works on the night shift. Forty on the survey shuttle, somewhere over their heads on approach. He did the sum without meaning to and set the plate on the crate.

"Save me the chair," he said.

"No," said Ines.

· · ·

Ops was a buried module with three screens and a coffee ring on every flat surface. Earth's warning sat open on the main screen: a Charter header, a director's authority code, two tracks in orbital shorthand, and a correction.

Command's duty officer stood as Continuity officers always stood, as if someone might be filming. "Earth says the Tarawa has a thruster fault her crew hasn't found," he said. "Earth says her scheduled burn puts her across the Alexandria's corridor this afternoon, New York time. Earth says move the burn."

"Who on Earth?"

"Foresight. The director's office, on his own authority." He straightened his collar. "We've asked the Tarawa to run a diagnostic. Forty minutes."

"And the burn?"

"Twenty-nine."

Aur'k had stopped at the second screen and laid the correction beside the tug's mass sheet. He touched nothing. He went still again, and Kaelin, who had lived beside that stillness for six years, waited for the number it was making.

"The duration is wrong," Aur'k said.

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong for her mass. Ninety-four seconds will not give the Tarawa that vector with two hundred tonnes of ice on her frame. Not on her good thrusters. Without the load, it does." His helm came round. "Earth's numbers only close if she sheds."

"So she sheds."

"And shed cargo goes down," said the officer. "Procedure. Nothing loose in a shuttle corridor." He touched the screen and the plot drew itself: the tug's new track, the ice frame parting from it, the frame's fall bending into the thin air and breaking up, a fan of debris opening across the basin floor. The broad end of the fan lay over a cluster of grey markers eleven kilometres east.

Kaelin didn't need the labels. Rig one. Rig two. The melt hall. The works shed. Rig four, out on the eastern lobe, where the ice was best and the walk back was longest.

"Thirty-four minutes after the burn," Aur'k said. "The frame comes apart first, in pieces. The ice comes down after it, all at once."

"Move the burn a minute."

"The window is eleven seconds wide, and Earth put the burn in the middle of it. Earth sent one answer." Aur'k said it gently, and that was worse. "I looked for a second. There isn't one."

"Then we hold it until they're out, and—"

"And the Alexandria meets the Tarawa this afternoon with forty on board," said the officer, "if the fault is real. If it isn't, Earth is wrong and we've dropped two hundred tonnes of ice on our own water for nothing." He looked at Kaelin and not at the screen. "A burn over an inhabited footprint needs surface sign-off. That's you."

Kaelin looked at Aur'k.

Aur'k's helm did not move. "I won't authorise this for you."

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

He had been. He stood with both hands on the back of the ops chair. Eight at the Ice-Works. Forty on the shuttle. A sender on Earth he had never heard of, whose numbers were too clean to have been argued over by anyone. Twenty-seven minutes. He thought of a checklist on a wet clipboard on the Reef Coast, twenty years gone, and put it back where he kept it.

"Signed," he said, and laid his palm on the plate. The screen wrote his name under the director's.

"Get me the works."

"Shed handset, one channel," said the officer. "They're not on the net out there."

"I know where they are."

The line to the Ice-Works ran out across the basin on poles, copper and old fibre, older than anyone on the station. Three budgets had promised to replace it. Three budgets had cut it. It rang six times before anyone picked up.

"Shed." A man's voice, thick with a nap at the pump panel.

"Sami, it's Kaelin. Listen and don't talk yet. Where is everyone?"

"Three in the melt hall. Three on rig two. Tuvalu's out on four, checking the casing. Me." A pause. "Why?"

Kaelin told him: the tug, the ice, the burn he had just signed, the fan with its wide end over them.

"Sixty-one minutes," he said. "The crawler takes four. Melt hall goes in the crawler. I'm bringing a rover for rig two and you. Rig four is two kilometres on foot in the dark, and the far lobe is where the fan's widest. Call Tuvalu in now. If he walks fast and doesn't stop, he's got a chance. Not a good one." He made himself say the rest. "I'm not going to tell you everyone gets out. Not everyone, probably. Run anyway."

The line hissed. Somewhere behind Sami a pump was knocking.

"You traded us," Sami said.

"Yes."

The handset clicked dead in his hand. Kaelin set it down. When he turned, Funmi was in the hatch, already sealed into her skinsuit, her helmet under one arm. Ines stood behind her with the med kit.

"My crew," Funmi said. It wasn't a question, and he didn't treat it as one.

· · ·

The rover took the berm road east with its lamps on, and the night came up to meet it at eighty below.

Kaelin drove. The suit pressed on every part of him at once, the tight layers hugging his ribs and thighs like a held breath, and he knew without looking that he'd be purple under it by morning. Ines sat behind him with the kit between her boots. Funmi rode up front with her hand on the dash, watching the poles go by, one every hundred metres, the old line sagging between them.

"Tuvalu Mensah walks slow," she said. "He stops to look at things."

"Then he'd better stop looking."

At the burn's minute a small new star crossed the sky from west to east, fast and bright, and went dim. Petra's voice came over the band from the station, thin. "Tarawa reports burn complete. Shed confirmed." Then, smaller: "Shuttle's track is clear."

Nobody in the rover said anything. Kaelin counted poles. Thirty. Thirty-one.

The Ice-Works came up out of the dark as a scatter of lit towers and a long low hall under its berm, and above them, high in the west, the sky began to scratch.

Thin white lines. Dozens, then more. They came down fast and silent and very beautiful, like frost cracking across glass, and Kaelin had never seen anything on Mars so lovely or so plainly meant for him.

"The frame," Ines said.

The first one hit before they reached the shed. It struck beyond rig one with no sound at all, only a spray of red dust that hung lit in the lamps and a shudder up through the wheels. The second hit closer. On the suit band somebody started shouting about the melt hall.

The crawler was at the shed with its hatch open and Sami Brandt beside it on one knee, his left leg wrong below the knee. The three from rig two came running in long, low-gravity bounds, dust kicking off their boots. Kaelin got them into the rover. Three.

"Melt hall," Funmi said on the band. She was already out of her seat. "The line's gone in the crew passage. They can't get through."

He saw it when he swung the lamps: white steam pouring from the passage hatch and flashing into ice crystals in the cold, a glittering column that leaned with the wind.

"The melt line's split in the passage," Funmi said. "Wellhead valve's at the hall end. Hand wheel. I'll close it."

"I'll close it."

"You don't know which one." She was already moving. "Get Sami in."

Sami would not get in.

He was on his feet, more or less, holding the crawler's hatch frame, his helmet turned east toward rig four, where a single suit beacon blinked, still a long way out. "Tuvalu," he said. "Go and get him. You've got wheels."

Kaelin had already looked. The lobe road ran straight into the broad end of the fan, and out there the ground was jumping in small red bursts where the frame was coming down.

"Aur'k," he said on the band. "Rig four, by rover."

"You would reach him as the ice arrives," said Aur'k from ops. "Not before. I am sorry."

Kaelin switched to the open channel. "Tuvalu. I can't come for you. Walk to the hall and don't stop. Funmi's opening you the passage." He made it plain. "You probably won't make it before the fall. Walk anyway."

The beacon kept blinking. It started to move faster.

"You don't get to say that," Sami said. "You said it already. You signed it." His voice cracked on the band. "I didn't choose this. You hear me? Nobody asked me. I didn't choose this—"

Kaelin took him under the arms and lifted. Sami weighed about what a sack of potatoes weighed at home, and fought like three.

He hit Kaelin's helmet with a gloved fist, twice, and screamed it again, that he didn't choose this, as Kaelin got a shoulder under his hips and carried him to the rover with his good leg kicking. Ines caught his head as he went through the hatch and laid one hand flat on his chest and held him there.

"I've got you," she said. "I know. I've got you."

Four.

On the band, Funmi was breathing hard and steady. "Wheel's stiff. Forty turns on this thing. Twenty." The steam column thinned. It broke. Three suits came out of the passage hatch at a run, went for the crawler, and the crawler pulled away west with its hatch still closing. "They're through," Funmi said. "Go."

"Come out."

"Tuvalu comes in through this passage. If I leave the wheel it opens again and he walks into it." A grunt. "Ten turns. Go on. I've got the longest column."

"Main fall in ninety seconds," said Aur'k. "Kaelin. Ninety seconds."

He was out of his seat. He didn't remember deciding to be.

Ines had him by the harness ring. She was smaller than him and forty-seven, she had braced one boot on the hatch frame, and she did not let go.

"Six in this rover," she said. "Count them."

He counted them. Sami, sobbing against the bulkhead. The three from rig two. Ines. Himself. Two empty seats.

East, Tuvalu's beacon was still moving, still a kilometre short of the hall.

"Funmi," he said.

"Go, Kaelin."

He drove.

The main fall came down behind them in a long white sheet, and the ground jumped under the wheels. When he looked in the mirror, the lit towers of the Ice-Works were going out one by one, east to west. The beacon on the lobe had gone out before any of them. Funmi didn't answer the band again.

· · ·

It was near midnight, station time, when he went looking for Aur'k.

Medical had Sami asleep with his leg pinned and the others in a row of bunks, and Ines walking the row with a hand flat on each of them in turn. Kaelin's suit had left him striped like bruised fruit from neck to knee. He had stopped feeling it somewhere on the berm road.

Aur'k wasn't in ops. He was at the boundary, alone, in the narrow module where the station's rock met the Preserve's and the monitors watched the seam between them. The light down here was low and blue. Kaelin had never liked it here, and he came in anyway.

Aur'k stood in front of a single trace, and for once he didn't turn when Kaelin came in. The line on the screen was flat, the rock's ordinary hiss, except for one place where it stood up clean and narrow and fell away.

Then Aur'k closed it, and the screen went back to flat lines.

"What was that?"

Aur'k turned his helm toward him, the whole of it, and said nothing. He said nothing for long enough that it became an answer of its own.

In six years Aur'k had never once given him a comfort he couldn't back. He wasn't giving one now. He wasn't giving anything.

Kaelin could have asked again. He stood with his hand on the hatch frame and thought about it, and about the man on his shoulder screaming that nobody had asked him, and about how tired he was of being the one who made people answer.

"Get some sleep," he said instead.

"I don't."

"Then pretend. For me."

On the way back he passed the galley. The screen still showed the Polder, dawn coming up grey over the wall. The rain had stopped. The harbour lay flat behind the sill. The surge had never come.

Funmi's column stood down the slate with her last bet underlined twice. He picked up the marker to cross it out, as they always did once the sky had answered, and held it there.

Then he capped it, and left her column standing, and did not go back down to the boundary.
