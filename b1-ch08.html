Fractured Horizons · Chapter 08

Come and see

Fractured Horizons · I. The voice

08

4 AprilElenaNew York, homeMars delay 6.5 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE POLDER / Elena Vázquez — Mars 6.5 min

The answer came at 04:10, while the kettle was still making up its mind.

Elena had the review's register open at the kitchen table, as she had every night since the seventeenth of March: a pale column at the edge of her eye, and under it the calibration band, laid out in hiss. She slept in pieces now. Two hours in the bed, then the table, then the band. She had taken to setting the kettle going so that there would be a sound in the room besides the cooler.

Packet received. Mars relay, calibration band. Raw copy: safety review.

She switched the kettle off.

One pulse train, shorter than the third. She worked it from the bottom. Clock. Frames. Header. At the header she stopped.

The third packet had been addressed to no one. This one had a destination, and she knew it, because she had built it by hand in an empty room. The review's hard-stop channel. The field with the red line round it.

It was addressed to her.

It parsed on the first pass.

Ven a ver.

Three words, in a pulse train cut to fit them. At the tail sat a run of padding in the same pattern she had put on her own frames, out of a habit from training that nobody else in the Spire had kept.

She read it twice. Ven a ver was what Soria had called down from the top of every ladder in Veracruz, never looking back, because her sister always came. She never once said what was up there. That was the game.

It had not answered the question. She had asked who. It had told her where.

And it had taken eighteen days. She could not make that number mean anything yet, and she set it aside.

Raw copy: safety review. Every packet raw, Rachel's rule, and this one sat in the register under her question, in Spanish, for the chair or anyone else to open in the morning. Eighteen days, and nobody on the review had said one word about the red line. She had stopped waiting for someone to. She left it where it was.

She opened the channel and began to build frames for a second question.

She got as far as the first frame. ¿Dónde? A few hundred bytes a pulse, and the answer would come back the same way, in its own time or never: a word, a riddle, another door. You could not see down a band. You could only be told.

Seeing was what a lattice was for. She had helped build the only one on Earth that could reach this far, and had watched it closed.

She ran the doors in order, as she would have run a fault.

The Spire line. Booked like an operating theatre when it ran, sealed now under one man's name, and he was told whenever anyone so much as asked, and he had already come down once to tell her, gently, what grief built.

Cobalt Basin. Lattices, and a season away by any ship that would take her.

The third door was already in the register, two lines above the packet. Luna Haven, bounded test. Agreement signed, 2 April. Chair: R. Iverson. She had read Rachel's scope on Sunday and been proud of her. One sweep. Twenty minutes. Four keepers. A lever on the wall that nothing could argue with. The only living lattice licensed for use, a day away, and now part of the Lantern. Which put it on the review. Which put it on her.

"You haven't slept since they shut the schools."

Daniel was in the doorway, barefoot, in the cooperative's old water-rota shirt, his hair standing up on one side.

The seventeenth of March. The heat closure. He had it right to the day, and he did not know what the day was.

"I've slept."

"In that chair. Sitting up, with your lace on. Like a horse."

"Horses sleep standing up."

"Then you're doing worse than a horse." He looked at the kettle. "My mother makes tea at four in the morning when somebody in the family's pregnant or dead."

"Neither."

"Good. Come to bed."

The frame sat half built in front of her, one question's worth of space and nothing in it. Discard? said the console.

She discarded it. She shut the register and went to bed, and lay on her back beside him with his arm across her and her lace off, and three words where the ceiling should have been, until it got light.

· · ·

The storm came over the wall at 18:40, half an hour before the forecast said it would.

Elena heard it first on the canopies. A patter, then a drumming, then a roar over the whole street. The sky outside the window went from white to bruise in the time it took her to cross the kitchen. Down at the corner the misting heads, still running for the afternoon's heat, sprayed on into the downpour with great seriousness, like a man watering his garden in the rain.

Her lace spoke, low and close. "Elena Vázquez. Storm advisory. Remain above ground floor. Flood gates closing at the bridges."

On her overlay, the train lines of the Polder went grey.

Not one. All of them, in a fan from the wall. Line held. Service will resume. No time given.

She looked at the grey for a long moment. The trains ran on the forecast. Nobody she knew had ever seen one stop. She had not. Not once in twenty years.

The forecast had been late. Foresight's forecast, fed by every sensor the Charter owned, had missed a spring storm by half an hour, in the same season that the band had given the Battery the Jamaica Bay pumps to the minute. Hers, in the sense that every number in that building was a little hers.

A message from Daniel. Train stopped. Whole carriage staring at the ceiling like it owes them money. Walking. Save me a towel.

She had been cooking for an hour.

She had not decided to. She had come home early and found herself at the stove, doing the beans his family's way, with too much garlic and a bay leaf, as she had watched him do them a hundred times and never once done herself. The pot ticked on the hob. Under the skylight, the bucket had started to ring.

She stood with her back to the pot and opened Foresight's secondment form.

Secondment, external. She filled it in at the speed of someone who had designed forms. Lantern safety review. Luna Haven bounded test. Role: on-site safety observer, test and after-reports. Duration: to the review's close.

Authority code.

She knew it by heart. Everyone on the review did. It went on every bill, and in March it had gone on hers. She typed it.

The form changed colour.

Review code accepted. Priority: review business. Estimated approval: within the hour.

An hour. Foresight lost ordinary requests for a season.

Under the estimate sat one more line.

Off-world posting. On approval, your household contact will be notified: D. Rocha.

She touched the field. Household contact cannot be changed while a request is open.

She could close the form. She could stand at the stove until he came in and tell him all of it, the word in January and the drawer and the roof and the three words at four in the morning, and watch his face while she did, and file in the morning, when there was nothing left to tell. The beans ticked. Rain went over the skylight in a sheet, and the bucket rang under it.

She filed. Submitted 18:52.

· · ·

He came in at quarter past seven, like a man the harbour had sent back.

The olive jacket was black with water. His hair was flat for once in his life, plastered to his forehead, and he stood on the mat and dripped and laughed, the laugh going on longer than anything needed.

"They stopped," he said. "The trains. The actual trains. In a tunnel, two stops from the wall, with all the lights on. Forty people staring at the ceiling. A man in a very good suit asked his lace for a pod. In a tunnel." He wrung out a sleeve onto the mat. "They walked us along the track to the portal. A woman in front of me took photographs of the rails the whole way, as if she'd met somebody famous."

"It's never happened."

"I know. I was there." He grinned at her, water running off the end of his nose. "Your forecast was late."

"It isn't mine."

"It's a bit yours."

Then he smelled the kitchen, and stopped.

He looked at the pot. He looked at the bay leaf on the saucer by the hob, set ready for the rim of his plate. He came across the kitchen, still dripping, and crouched by the hob and looked at the flame, as if the fault might be in the burner.

"Tea at four," he said, "and the beans at seven. Somebody's pregnant or dead."

"Neither."

"Then what?" He stood up, and looked straight at her. "Elena. What's happened?"

She went to the window, because it was a thing to do with her body. The rain hit the glass in handfuls. Down in the street a pod sat at the kerb with its lights on and nobody in it, waiting for someone who had not called it yet. In the glass she could see the kitchen behind her, the door, him.

"The review's sending me to Haven," she said.

He did not say anything. She heard him take his jacket off.

"The Lantern's test. They've signed it. One sweep through Haven's lattice, twenty minutes, four of Haven's keepers in it, and the keeper on a lever outside it, a mechanical one, steel. If she pulls it, the coupling breaks and the test is over and it's never run again. Rachel wrote that. It's a good scope." She heard herself and did not stop. "After a session there are reports. On the four. Fog, sleep, what they see after. Somebody from the review has to read them there, raw, the same day, not a summary a week later on Earth. Somebody who's read the protocol. So."

"So they asked for you."

"Yes."

The rain came in another handful. Her hands had found each other in front of her. She left them there.

"Did you want to go?" he said.

"No."

It came out easily. She had been building it since four in the morning, and it was well made.

He came and stood beside her at the window, close enough that she could feel the wet coming through his shirt. For a while he watched the empty pod with her.

"I'll come up for a week," he said. "I've never seen the Moon. I'll bring the iron. Somebody on Haven must have a box that needs a disgrace."

"It's Charter work. They won't clear you."

"Then I'll wave from the roof. You'll see me. I'll be the one waving." He found her eyes in the glass. "And you'll sleep up there. In a bed. Lying down, like a person."

"I'll sleep."

"Promise me that, and I'll stay here and mind the bucket."

"I promise."

That one was easier than the first.

Her lace chimed.

Secondment approved. Lantern safety review. Departure: 21 April.

Three seconds later his chimed too, behind his ear, and she watched his eyes go to the corner where the notice was.

"Household notice," he read. "E. Vázquez. Off-world posting, Luna Haven, from 21 April." He laughed. "The city's behind you, for once."

He turned to her, and his face was so plainly glad that she made herself look at it, all of it, the flattened hair and the water on his eyelashes and the gladness, because she had put it there herself in under an hour, with a form, a code and one word at a window. "They asked for you. Of course they asked for you. You're the only one of them who reads the whole thing."

He kissed her temple, wet, and smelling of the tunnel.

Out past the glass, the line on the avenue lit up again, car by car, from the wall inward. Service resumed, said her overlay. The pod at the kerb pulled away for whoever had finally called it.

On the table his own handheld chimed. The old one, the cooperative's, with the cracked corner. He picked it up on his way to the stove and read it with a spoon in his other hand.

"Audit," he said. "Wonderful."

She read it over his shoulder. He did not tilt it away. He never had.

Continuity network office. Integrity audit: Vila Verde mesh cooperative, repair contract. Unregistered route from the cooperative's New York node into a Charter maintenance layer, 16 January, 00:49 to 01:12. Referred by: the Charter, network compliance.

The Charter's network compliance had one office, and it was in the Spire.

"The sixteenth of January." He frowned at it. "A Charter maintenance layer. From my box." He laughed again, shorter. "My box can barely reach the school roof. They see a box they can't read and they call it a crime. It's the contract they want. Somebody with a logo wants our contract." He tasted the beans, and closed his eyes. "Oh, that's good. That's very good. Who taught you?"

"You did."

"I'm wonderful." He put the handheld face down by the fruit bowl. "I'll pull the logs off the roof in the morning and send them the lot. They can read every packet that box has ever carried. It's mostly the school."

Above them, on its bracket by the parapet, his node sat in the rain in its taped wrap, with the seam turned to the wall, as she had left it. In the morning he would go up and open it.

There was still time to tell him, before the roof did.

She took down two plates, and put the bay leaf on the rim of his.
