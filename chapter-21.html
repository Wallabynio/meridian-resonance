LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, MEDICAL FACILITY / Rina Matsuda

The readings above the two beds were the most beautiful thing Rina Matsuda had seen in nine days, and she hated them for it.

She stood at the foot of the isolation bay with a diagnostic tablet she had stopped consulting twenty minutes ago, because the wall display rendered the problem better than any summary could: two neural-lace telemetry fields, side by side, each one a slow bloom of pattern activity that was not the flat line of death and not the banked, orderly dimness of coma and not anything else that had ever been given a name. Fragments of signal surfaced and faded across both fields like phosphorescence in dark water — here a motor sequence, here a language cluster, here something the analysis software labelled, with the helpless honesty of a system out of its depth, coherent, unclassified.

In the beds beneath the readings lay Noor Behzadi and Anders Halvorsen, who had walked out of the deep-interface chamber on their own feet nine days ago, and who had not woken since the fourth day, and who were — the medical lead had now said it three times in the course of one briefing, each time more carefully — neither asleep nor gone.

"Triage read it as lace-shock." The medical lead stood on the far side of Noor's bed with her hands folded, a compact woman in her fifties who had run this facility through two depressurisation drills and one actual breach and had never once, Rina suspected, given a briefing like this. "Both of them were lucid that first night. Disoriented, headached, asking the right questions about their own condition — everything we'd expect from proximity to a field event. Standard protocol is rest and observation. They rested. And somewhere in the resting, they started to — " she paused, and Rina watched her discard two words before choosing the plainest one — "scatter."

"Progressively?" Léa Fournier asked. She stood at the head of the bay where she had stood for the whole briefing, very still, her session tablet dark at her side. She had not taken a single note. Rina understood that to mean she was recording everything.

"Progressively. Noor first, Anders eighteen hours behind her, the same curve in the same order. Global coherence dropping in steps, like — " the medical lead's hands unfolded, sketched a helpless shape, refolded — "like watching a building let go of its floor plan one room at a time. Nothing I gave them touched it. By day six I stopped giving them things and started asking your people what I was looking at. Because it is not in my nosology, and I have been doing this thirty years, and I can keep their bodies alive for another thirty without ever once knowing whether anyone is home."

The bay's monitoring system chimed softly, logged something, said nothing. Even the walls in this room had the sense to keep to procedure.

"Rina." Léa's voice, quiet. "Tell her what you told me."

Rina brought the telemetry comparison up on the wall with two keystrokes, because she had built the comparison at four in the morning and had known while building it that she would be asked to show it, and had dreaded that, and had built it anyway.

"Three fields," she said. "Left is Mara Ekeberg, day one after the cascade. Middle and right are Noor and Anders, current." The displays settled. Even the medical lead, who did not read lace telemetry, leaned forward, because the resemblance did not need training to see: all three fields showed consciousness distributed, pattern activity spread across nodes instead of gathered in one place. "Same event. Same mechanism, as far as the physics goes — the cascade reached all three of them, and all three of them dispersed. The difference is here." She isolated the connective traces, and Mara's field lit with them: a fine, branching web binding every scattered fragment back toward every other, thread after thread after thread. "Mara was in the Habitat Corridors, embedded in the mycorrhizal network she's maintained for three years. When she scattered, the network's geometry gave her reference points. Something to be coherent across. Her pattern is dispersed but indexed — every fragment knows where every other fragment is."

She switched to Noor's field, and did not say anything for a moment, and let the absence say it.

"They were in the chamber," the medical lead said slowly. "Closest to the aperture. And the chamber gave them — "

"Instrument racks. Deck plating. Nothing alive, nothing patterned, nothing that could hold an index." Rina heard her own voice doing the thing it did under load, going flat and exact, itemising. "Their fragments are internally coherent. Watch long enough and you'll see whole sequences fire in order — Anders ran a full calibration gesture with his right hand on day five, textbook, eleven seconds, the same motion he's performed six thousand times two levels down. Noor's audio cortex loops four notes, the same four, a few times a day." Something she was humming that morning, Rina did not say. She had cross-referenced the chamber's ambient recordings and found it, Noor humming at her station in the minutes before the aperture opened, and that finding was in the report, and she was not going to be able to say it aloud in this room. "The pieces are all still firing. What's gone is the thread between them. It's not erasure. It's a library with the catalogue burned — every book still on some shelf, and no way for any hand to find them."

"Can it be rebuilt?" the medical lead asked. "The catalogue?"

"Mara's is the only case where a dispersal held together, and hers held because the anchor was already grown before the event. Nine days after — " Rina looked at the two beds, at Noor's hands lying open and unmarked on the coverlet, hands she had last seen wrapped around a coffee bulb in the Levels' commissary, arguing cheerfully about resonator maintenance schedules. "I don't have a pathway. I'm not saying there isn't one. I'm saying that what I know how to restore is systems, and systems can be restored because somewhere there's a record of what they looked like when they were whole."

The sentence stopped. Rina had not planned for it to stop there; it stopped the way a corridor stops at an unpressurised door.

"And there is no record," Léa said, into the quiet, "of what a person looked like when they were whole."

It was not a question, and no one treated it as one. On the wall, in Noor Behzadi's scattered field, four notes fired in sequence, faded, and did not return.

LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, HABITAT CORRIDORS / Rina Matsuda

The Corridors kept their night register in the hours after the briefing, the bioluminescence banked low and blue-green along the walls, and Rina and Sarin Yelena walked through it side by side and said nothing for a very long way.

It was not an awkward silence. Rina had spent enough of her life in machine rooms to know the difference between silence that was absence and silence that was load — two systems running at capacity, no cycles to spare for handshake protocols — and this was the second kind, and they both knew it, and so neither of them spent anything on relieving it.

Sarin walked close to the wall. Rina had noticed it three junctions back: not touching, mostly, but near, the way a person walks near a railing above a long drop, and every so often her fingers drifted out and trailed along the living surface for a few paces before returning to her side. The filament lines down her forearms caught the wall-light when they moved, fine silver threads laid under the skin, and where they crossed the inside of her wrists the skin was still faintly inflamed, nine days and one crossing back from the Preserve not yet enough to settle what eleven minutes as a dimensional boundary had asked of it. She had sat through the entire briefing without a word — the only person on Luna Haven whose nervous system had done, deliberately and survivably, a version of what had been done to the two people in those beds — and the medical lead had glanced at her forearms twice, and had had the grace not to ask.

Rina thought about patterns.

It was what she was for; she had known that about herself since she was nine years old, disassembling her grandmother's antique circuit boards on a kitchen table in a city she had since watched become a Corporate Union asset ledger. Systems declared themselves to her. Structure was legible the way faces were legible to other people. And what her own structure was doing now, in the blue-green dark, with the sound of the medical bay's soft chime still somewhere in her ears, was itemising — helplessly, the way it always itemised — everything she knew.

She knew the architecture of her own neural lace to the register level; she had rebuilt half of it herself. She knew, provably, more about consciousness-pattern telemetry than anyone in the colony's active roster. She could write down what she knew — had, in fact, spent years writing it down, documentation being the discipline that separated engineering from improvisation — and every line of it would survive her, and none of it would be her. That was the distinction her mind kept walking around, the way a survey drone circles a structure it has been told to map: what she knew was recorded in a hundred systems, and what she was — the pattern doing the knowing, the specific weave that itemised in this order and dreaded in this key and had loved, quietly and to no purpose now, the sound of Noor Behzadi humming in a machine room — existed in exactly one substrate, and the substrate was mortal, and nine days ago the distance between those two facts had stopped being philosophy.

A library with the catalogue burned. She had said it, so it was hers to have thought.

"You're doing it too," Sarin said. First words in eleven junctions. Her voice was low and unhurried and slightly rusty, the way it had been since she came back through. "Whatever it is. I can hear it."

"Thinking."

"Mm." Sarin's fingers drifted out, touched the wall, came back. "About them?"

"About what would have had to already exist," Rina said, "for them to be findable."

Sarin nodded slowly and did not ask the follow-up question, which was how Rina knew she had already walked the same corridor herself and found the same unpressurised door at the end of it.

They stopped where the residential branch split from the research spine, the junction light pooling pale around them. Down one corridor, Rina's quarters, her console, the telemetry she would sit with until she fell asleep in front of it. Down the other, the lifts to the Levels, and below them, wrapped in restored quiet, the Archive.

"Eleven minutes," Rina said. "At the Threshold. What held?"

Sarin looked at her forearms, at the silver under the skin, and considered the question with the seriousness of someone who had been debriefed nine times and never once asked that.

"Everything did," she said. "That's the part I can't get the reports to hold. It read me — all of it, the fear included, especially the fear — and what it needed wasn't strength, and it wasn't calm. It needed nothing left out. Whole, or nothing." She flexed her hands once, slowly, watching them answer. "I keep thinking about how close I came to going out there with less."

Rina said nothing. Somewhere beneath their feet, two levels down and nine days back, four notes went on firing in the dark with no thread to find their way home along, and Rina Matsuda stood in the junction light a moment longer than she needed to, and then went down the corridor toward her console, walking the way she walked when she had begun, without yet admitting it, to design something.

LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, ARCHIVE CHAMBER / Léa Fournier

The Archive Chamber kept a different silence from the rest of the colony, and Léa Fournier had always been able to feel the threshold in the soles of her feet: the point where the research levels' living hum gave way to something older, drier, patient — a room that had been rebuilt twice in the colony's short history and had both times been rebuilt first, before the labs, before the commissary, because a colony that lost its records lost the ability to know what it had survived.

The record cores rose in their quiet ranks under conservation light, and between them the Archive's technical lead was doing what the Archive's staff were always doing, which was tending: a diagnostic here, an index verification there, the unhurried husbandry of the colony's memory. Births. Deaths. Session notes. Eleven weeks of shimmer data. The whole honest sediment of a small civilisation, kept.

"Léa." The technical lead set down his verification wand. "You're filing tonight? The duty system could have taken it."

"I'm not filing." She had thought, on the way down, about how to say this, and had decided — it was the decision that had taken longest — to say it formally, in person, on the record, because the alternative was to have the conversation casually, deniably, in a corridor, and some questions were corrupted by being asked deniably. "I'm here to request a consultation. A research-planning consultation, on the record. I'd like the Archive's assessment of a hypothetical."

Something in the technical lead's posture adjusted — the small, complete attention of a records specialist being handed, for the first time, a question about the future instead of the past.

"Go ahead."

"If we were to consider the preservation of a consciousness pattern as a contingency measure," Léa said, "a record — inert, non-functional, a description of a mind and in no sense a continuation of one — what would the ethical requirements be? What would consent even have to look like, for something no one has ever consented to before? And what would this facility, specifically, need to become in order to hold such a record without betraying what it's for?"

The silence that followed had texture. Above them the conservation lights held steady; somewhere in the ranked cores, a verification cycle completed and logged itself, a sound like a page turning in another room.

"You're asking because of the two in medical," the technical lead said at last. It was not procedure to say it. It was, Léa thought, the necessary act of a careful man refusing to let the question stay comfortably abstract.

And there it was — the fork she had stood at, on and off, all evening. The clean version of this consultation named no one. The clean version was a planning exercise, defensible in any review, adjacent to no particular grief. She had written the clean version in her head in the lift, and it had been well-constructed, and delivering it would have been, in the precise sense she had spent twenty years learning to detect in herself, a way of leaving something out.

Whole, or nothing, Sarin had said in her debrief, of what the boundary would accept.

"I'm asking because of Noor Behzadi and Anders Halvorsen," Léa said. "Who scattered nine days ago with no record anywhere in the world of the pattern that should be reassembled, and who are teaching us the cost of that absence one day at a time. Put their names in the consultation request. A hypothetical with names in it stays honest."

The technical lead entered the request as she spoke it, formally, field by field, and the Archive received it the way the Archive received everything — without alarm, without ceremony, one more entry in a ledger that had outlasted every crisis the colony had thought unrecordable. Consultation request 1: ethical requirements for consciousness-pattern preservation as contingency measure. Requestor: Fournier, L. Occasioned by: Behzadi, N.; Halvorsen, A. Status: open.

"I'll tell you now," the technical lead said, "the Archive can't answer most of this. We keep things. What a kept thing is for — whether keeping a mind's shape honours the mind or trespasses on it — that's not a records question. You'll need ethicists. Law. Probably the council."

"I know," Léa said. "But every one of those conversations will need a place to stand, and the place is here. You're the only infrastructure on this world built passive on purpose — built to hold without using. If it can be done at all without betraying someone, it starts in this room."

She stayed a while after the request was filed, longer than the filing needed, standing in the dry patient light among the ranked memory of everyone who had ever lived here. Then she took out her session tablet and wrote the night's last note, and for once did not cross any of it out.

We have been keeping records of everything except the thing that keeps the records. Consultation opened. Requirements unknown. The question is now, itself, on file — which means that whatever we decide, we will not be able to pretend we never asked it.

She filed the note, and the Archive kept it, and two levels up, in the medical facility's small hours, four notes surfaced in the dark of a scattered mind — patient, coherent, unfindable — like a signal from a ship that did not know yet that someone had finally begun, on the far shore, to build the instrument that listens.
