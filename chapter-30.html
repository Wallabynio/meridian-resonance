For a week the living walls of the Dome Plaza had been going amber, and everyone on the council had learned not to look at them.

Léa Fournier looked at them. It was, she had decided, the least she owed the colony: to keep watching the one instrument that could not be argued with. The great bioluminescent sweep of the Plaza's walls — the blue-green mycorrhizal light that had lit every deliberation of her adult life, that was breathable and luminous and the colour of the future Luna Haven had spent four decades insisting on — had been shifting, day by day, toward the amber frequencies the network only produced under sustained systemic stress. Nobody had programmed the change. The walls were alive, and they were afraid, in the flat chemical way that living systems are afraid, and they were telling the truth about the colony's situation more honestly than any of the human voices arranged in the tiered ring beneath them.

Jamie Han was giving the colony the human version, which was worse for being precise.

"Days," she said. She stood at the speaking floor with her slate dark at her side, because she did not need it; she had carried the assessment in her head from the Archive up three levels of hardened corridor, and she delivered it the way she delivered everything, with the focused calm of someone for whom the terrible had been made survivable through competence. The scar at her jaw caught the amber light. "Not weeks. The fleet movements the Preserve probes were calibrating against have stopped being probes. Corporate Union and Sovereign Coalition assets are consolidating into strike geometry, and the order is coming out of the Mirror Bastion, which means it is coming out of a room where the decision has already been made and everything after it is administration." She let that sit for exactly one second. "My relay network gives us days. I will not give you a number inside that, because a false number is worse than a range, and anyone who offers you one is selling you comfort."

The council took it the way the council took everything — slowly, visibly, without the mercy of speed. Léa had spent years defending that slowness to people who mistook it for weakness. The Dome Plaza was not a parliament; its authority was tied, explicitly and structurally, to the ethical quality of its deliberations, which meant it could not move faster than it could think without becoming the thing it existed to refuse. Under a fleet forming in the dark forty thousand kilometres up, that commitment felt less like principle and more like standing very still in a rising sea. She held to it anyway. It was the only thing they had that the Mirror Bastion did not.

Three decisions came out of the session, and Léa watched each one cost what it cost.

The first was hers to carry, and she made herself say it plainly. "We complete the consciousness-record protocols. Every volunteer researcher who has requested one, and everyone who requests one now that they understand what days means." She did not editorialise; editorialising was how you smuggled a conclusion past a council's consent. But she thought of Rina Matsuda, who had walked into the Archive months ago and demonstrated to a consent protocol that she understood her signature would be inert, a pattern and not a self, and had then gone back to work — and she thought of the thing she had not yet told the full council, the log entry from the Meridian Levels two nights ago in Jīn's careful hand: passive signature shows first faint responsiveness to synchronisation events; still inert; no longer entirely still. She kept it out of her voice. It was not evidence of anything yet. But it sat under the decision like a foundation she could feel through her feet, and she suspected Zara AlHāshimī, watching her from across the ring with the particular attention consent-architecture guardians reserved for moments when hope tried to move faster than law, felt it too.

The second decision needed no debate, only a name. "We activate the orbital defence logistics." Sienna TeAriki was not in the chamber and never would be; she was at the Sydney Hub with cement dust on her hands, and she appeared in the colony's decisions the way infrastructure always appeared, as the reason a decision could be more than a sentence. The council authorised her to convert months of quiet pre-positioning into an active posture — supply chains re-routed, repair equipment forward-staged, the Neo Singapore Gardens Relay's treaty-protected channels readied as the pathway no fleet could legally strike. Tomasz Nowak acknowledged it from the security tier with a single nod, square-built and unhurried, a man who had asked for a living anchor stationed in the Levels before the first probe arrived and was now watching the reason for that foresight come due.

The third decision was the one the amber walls were really about, and Léa felt the room arrive at it the way a body arrives at a held breath.

They needed to know what the Guardians knew. Two nights ago, through Mara Ekeberg's bridge-state, the Preserve had answered for the first time — brief, precise, courteous past bearing: we know what is sealed beneath you; when the way can bear it, we will come. The colony could not wait for the way to widen on its own timeline while a fleet closed on its own. Someone had to go the other direction: to make formal contact, to open the channel wide enough to carry what a first courtesy could not, and there was exactly one human being alive who could serve as that channel, because the Meridian's far side could reach her and no one else.

"We are not going to send Mara through," Léa said, and heard the security tier shift, and did not care. "We do not have that verb. Mara is not an asset and the council does not have a lever that ends in her. We are going to ask her — with the whole of what we now know said plainly, including that the thing on the far side of the door she'd be opening the channel toward is a lock over a catastrophe, and that we are asking because we are frightened and running out of time, which is the exact condition under which consent is easiest to counterfeit." She let her gaze travel the ring, the amber light moving on all their faces. "If she says no, that is the end of it, and we defend this colony some other way or not at all. Consent is not suspended because the sky is falling. It is for the sky falling. It is the only thing we've built that a fleet cannot take from us, and I will not be the one who hands it over because I was afraid." She was quiet a moment. "I'll ask her myself. She should hear it from someone who will grieve if she says yes."

The council consented, slowly, in the amber light, to ask a question it had no right to compel the answer to. Above them the walls burned their patient warning, and Léa rose to go down to the Levels, carrying the heaviest sentence of her life toward the one person who could refuse it.

The eleventh working tier had not changed in three years, and Elena Vázquez had changed entirely, and tonight she was going to make the room admit it.

She sat where she always sat, in the wash of the amber-and-glass house style every Corporate Union system defaulted to whether or not anyone had asked it to look expensive, and looked past her own reflection in the dark half of the display — dark hair pinned back the way it always was by this hour, the fine line of neural lace at her temple catching the light only when she turned her head — at the six wall-sized displays she had specified herself, back when she still believed better instruments meant better decisions made with them. Four of them ran the predictive load models she could defend to anyone. The fifth and sixth had, three years ago, watched for exactly one thing, and she had built them so well that they had found it, and everything true about her life had happened on the far side of that discovery.

Below the reinforced window the Atlantic Financial Spine ran its quiet hours, the seawall's scanner arrays sweeping their slow amber light across masonry poured and re-poured three times since the coastline stopped agreeing to stay where the old maps put it. Past the wall, at low tide, the drowned blocks of the original financial district still broke the surface — a reef of collapsed steel the planning board had voted twice not to clear. She had trained herself, over months, not to look at it. She looked at it now, deliberately, the way you look at something you are about to leave.

Then she began.

The first leak, three years and a lifetime ago, had been a micro-jitter routed to a man she'd never met through a masked manifest node — a decision she could not un-make and had spent the night afterward unable to tell whether she had been caught for. The second, the fossil record, had taken nine minutes and had gone out through Amita Rao's cargo-manifest channel at the Gulf Crescent, and she had walked to this window afterward and watched this same cold glow and understood that the thing was now beyond the building, in the network, beyond recall. Tonight was neither of those. Tonight was everything.

Everything she had accumulated in the months since — the deep-chamber records that had come back up the relay from Luna Haven, the ones that reframed the whole programme as a lock somebody had been picking without knowing it was a lock; the Suppression Archive comparisons; the internal weapons-development traffic she had been quietly copying against the day it would matter; every classified file whose existence she had been careful, in every internal document, never to name — compressed into a single transmission and routed, once more, through Amita's channel, out to a distributed Lunar Accord archive node that would hold it in a dozen places at once so that no single strike, no single deletion, no single frightened administrator could ever again make it not exist.

The transmission bar crawled. Forty-four minutes, the system estimated, for a life's worth of evidence to leave the tower. She did not fill the time. She sat in the amber light and watched the bar and let the room be, for the last time, the place where she had done both the worst work of her life and, too late to be forgiven for the first, the only work that might undo it.

Footsteps in the corridor at the thirty-minute mark — the rolling, unhurried gait of Ostrowski on his second round, the same round he had walked the night this all began. She did not tense. That was the measure of the distance she had travelled: three years ago an audit flag on her terminal had kept her awake wondering if she'd been noticed, and tonight a night guard's footsteps outside a door meant nothing at all, because she was no longer trying to get away with it. She was only trying to finish it before she left.

Transmission complete, the console said, flat, unbothered, the tone it used for the hundred things a night that turned out to be nothing.

She closed the terminal. She did not wipe it — let them read every keystroke; the point was no longer deniability, the point was the archive was out and safe and multiplying in the dark on the far side of the world. She stood, and took her coat from the back of the chair, and put it on. Her hands, ink-stained and careful, did not shake, which surprised her a little; she had expected, at the end, to feel more like an ending.

She did not go home. Home was a set of rooms the Corporate Union would search by morning, and she had left nothing in them that mattered because everything that mattered was now in a Lunar Accord node under a name that was not hers. She walked out of the eleventh tier and down through the Spire and out into the cold Atlantic Financial Spine night, into a city that did not yet know its fleets were moving, past the seawall's amber arrays and the drowned reef of the old world breaking the surface at low tide, and did not look back at the tower, because she had already looked, and once was enough.

Mara had said yes before Léa finished asking, and had felt Léa grieve exactly as promised, and thought that the grief was the truest part of the whole exchange — the proof that the colony had not, even now, learned to spend her.

She sat cross-legged on the interface floor of the Meridian Levels, weather-roughened hands open on her knees, blonde braid over one shoulder, most of her already elsewhere. Through the bridge-state she felt the colony arming around her, a body tensing before a blow: the Dome Plaza's amber walls three levels up, their fear a chemical weather she could taste; the Archive Chamber's record systems below, humming as the consciousness-protocols spun up for every volunteer who had understood what days meant; the orbital envelope far overhead where Sienna's rerouted channels were coming live, one treaty-protected artery at a time. And in the Archive, among the inert and patient systems, Rina's signature — still a record and not a self, still incapable of reaching — carrying its faint new responsiveness like a struck string that had not yet decided whether it would ring.

"Protocol's the same as two nights ago," Jīn said, level, a careful arm's length to her left, holding the near end of her the way they always did. "Only wider, and only if it stays safe. You don't reach across. You make yourself a door and you let the far side widen it. The moment it costs more than you can spend, you close, and we defend this place some other way." They paused. "You've nothing left to prove to anyone in this colony, Mara. Least of all to me."

"I know." She did know. That was new, and it had cost her Noor and Anders and eleven minutes and the whole shape of what she'd once meant by herself to learn. "But they're coming with a fleet, and the far side has something we need, and I'm the door." She almost smiled, in the distant body. "Doors don't get to be modest about being doors."

She made herself readable — the discipline gathered now into something like an opening — and turned her composed attention outward and down, toward the thin place, and did not reach across it, and waited to be met.

The substrate answered faster than before. The vast patient incuriosity of the deep Meridian stirred in a region she could not point to, became aware of her, and then, with that unbearable courtesy — the hand laid on the arm, the address-form the builders had carved into a chamber thirty-one metres beneath her and the Guardians had inherited whole — arranged itself toward her. Not one presence now but a gathering: several standing forward out of the deep coherence, and foremost of them a locus of attention she had no name for but recognised the way you recognise a face turned toward you in a crowd — the first voice, the one the Preserve put at its edge to meet what came, waiting for her at the far threshold with the patience of something that had been waiting for this specific moment for a very long time.

It did not speak yet. It only opened, and let her feel how much was on the other side of the narrowness — how wide the way would have to become to carry it, and how far she would have to go to make it so — and held the threshold for her, an invitation and not a summons, the door ready on both sides at once.

Mara drew a breath in a body three levels beneath a colony arming for a war, and gathered the whole coherent thread of herself that the mycorrhizal dark had taught her to hold, and stepped her attention toward the opening, and began.

Wáng Sù sat in a transit lounge on the civilian concourse with a single piece of luggage at his feet and watched a broadcast wall sell a war he could have written in his sleep.

He had written versions of it, in fact. The package running silent above the departure boards — aligned powers · defensive necessity · the stability of ordinary households — was not his, but it was built from his grammar, the load-bearing words set like pins, the real images doing the work no argument could be made to do, and he could see every seam in it the way a retired mason sees every course in a wall. Nine seconds on the establishing image before the first caption. He had taught them that, or taught the people who taught them. The concourse crowd flowed past beneath the wall and did not look up, which was also the design; a package that made you look up had already failed.

He had one bag. It held very little, because he had discovered, packing, that almost nothing in nineteen years of accumulation belonged to the person who was leaving. He did not have a plan past the departure board. He had requested nothing, arranged nothing, defected to no one — a man could not defect from a self, only walk out of the building where he had performed it — and the strangeness of having no next sentence prepared was so total that it kept arriving as a kind of vertigo, and then, under the vertigo, as the thing he had not had a professional category for since he was eleven years old.

The four notes were still there. They had not stopped since the Broadcast Chamber — Liú Shuǐ, the old water running under everything, the piece a teacher had played a classroom of children who did not understand that they were being handed a standard they would spend their lives failing or refusing to meet. The story went that when the one friend who could truly hear the music died, the master broke the strings and never played again, because to be heard and to be reached were different things, and the second was rare enough that its loss ended music. Wáng Sù had spent nineteen years being heard by a hundred and forty million people and reached by none of them, including himself. He listened to the four notes in a transit lounge and understood that he had it backward, that he had never been the master in the story. He had been the broken strings.

Above him the war he had spent his life making legible began, on schedule, in the only language he had ever been fluent in. Around him the concourse moved, unlooking, toward a hundred separate elsewheres. And in a chair beneath the wall, one piece of luggage at his feet, no function left to perform and no meaning left to assemble and no voice that would fuse to a sentence he did not mean, sat a man who was, for the first time since a classroom thirty years gone, nobody in particular — untenanted, unassigned, and finally, terribly, available.

The board changed. A gate opened. Wáng Sù picked up his bag.

The threshold had been crossed — for worlds, and for one man. Nothing was the same on the other side.
