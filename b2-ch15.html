The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

16 AprilElenaVila Verde; the launch fieldMars delay 19.2 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE AND THE LAUNCH FIELD / Elena Vázquez — Mars 19.2 min

The bag could weigh eleven kilos, and Elena's weighed eleven and two hundred grams.

She had it open on the rota table under the one lamp, at twenty to four in the morning. The hall slept around her in its corners, and the fan turned its face across the floor and away.

The two hundred grams were the notebook. A footballer on the cover, gone soft at the corners. Roofs and faults in pencil, the free towns' re-entry lines upside down from the back, and the page in the middle headed TWO with one line through it.

Mara's post had come down the roofs to Daniel's handheld at eleven the night before, with Sunday's vote at the top of it.

Carried. Four votes. Halla counted on her fingers and came out four short, the first time I've seen it. The margin is the thinnest Haven sells: if your shuttle holds on the pad, the ship lights without you, and there is no fuel for another window before the planets close. So don't hold. Full air. M.

Elena tore out the middle page, the one headed TWO, and folded it twice into her shirt pocket. She put the rest of the notebook on the rota table, square to the edge, for Daniel's mother to find.

Eleven kilos. She closed the bag.

On the screen inside the door the bulletin had run all night on the old man's feed. SÃO PAULO. NO DEATHS. White tracks turned aside over this city, and then Rachel.

They had her file photograph and then they had her moving: on the Battery's steps in the dark, a dark suit, platinum hair blunt at the jaw, walking fast with a light held at her. Somebody shouted a question. She stopped for as long as the answer took and not a second more.

"The floor did it. Ask them." And went in.

NEW YORK'S CIVIC DIRECTOR, said the strip under her. THE WOMAN WHO TURNED THE WAVE.

The bakery man had fallen asleep in front of it on a mat with his cup on his chest. Every time the clip came round he woke enough to raise the cup to it.

The floor. Elena knew what was under the floor, and whose name was on its rota.

"She looks tired," said Daniel's mother.

She had come out of the cistern room in her old cardigan with the jug. She poured a cup off the jug, the morning share an hour early, and ticked it in the hall's column with the thick marker, and put the cup in front of Elena, and didn't sit.

"She's always tired," Elena said.

"So are you." Mãe looked at the notebook on the table's edge and didn't touch it. "Volta," she said. "E come."

"Vou." Elena drank the water. It was warm already and tasted of the tank, and she drank all of it, because it was ticked.

Daniel came down the stairs with the van keys on their string and his hair flat on one side. Elena had a sentence ready for him. She had had it since February. She opened her mouth.

"Van's out front," he said. "The old man says if we break it he'll know."

She closed the bag's strap instead, and stood up.

· · ·

The van went down the ridge road in the dark with the hill asleep behind it, every roof a blue tank and a painted circle and a node with its little light. Then the main road and the towns the relays reached, blue-white windows coming on for the morning cooling. Then the dry plain.

Daniel drove with both hands. Twice she felt him about to speak, and twice the road needed him.

The rectenna field went by on the right, kilometres of fenced mesh under the stars, humming at a pitch she felt in her back teeth. Beyond it the sky was still black to the ground.

"There," Daniel said.

The launch field came up out of the plain as a fence and a long white shed and lights on poles. Far out past all of it, alone on its pad in a ring of floodlight, stood the shuttle. It was smaller than she had thought it would be, and older.

Its belly was scorched black in a long feathered stain from nose to tail, and it stood in a white cloud that rolled off the pad and thinned away over the ground.

It was hot already, at half past four. The air through the window smelt of dust and of a burnt chemical sweetness off the pad. Out on the field the first dust devil was walking, a thin brown thread turning on itself, crossing the floodlight and going out.

Over the shed the ring was crossing in its thread of beads, and one of them was not white. It burned a dull steady red, low in the east.

"That's it," she said. "That's the ship. Those are its radiators."

Daniel looked at it through the windscreen. "It's very small."

"It's a long way off."

"I know." He turned the key and the van stopped humming. "I know it's a long way off, Elena."

· · ·

The gate was a white room with a queue in it and a machine at the end.

The shed smelled of floor polish and hot plastic and too many people up too early. Most of them had come to see somebody off: families in their best, children asleep on shoulders, and screens on every wall, and on every screen Rachel stopping on the steps. The floor did it. Ask them. A man in the queue clapped every time, out of habit.

Some of the families had synthetics among them.

Elena saw the first at the head of the queue: a city chassis, slim, pale grey, in a good linen jacket, holding a small girl by the hand. Where a helm would be it wore a display face, a soft oval. It was showing the girl a smile, gentle and wide, and the girl was smiling back up into it, and when it bent to her the smile bent too.

Down the shed another wore a face that was laughing with an old man at his own joke.

Then the doors at the far end opened on the heat, and a field chassis came in out of the dark.

Rust-red plating, scoured, ticking as it cooled. A smooth helm with nothing on it. Nothing on any of it that wasn't for work. He walked with his feet set wide in a body built for a third of this weight, and the crowd made room for him without knowing it had.

He stopped in front of her and turned his whole helm to her.

"Elena Vázquez," he said.

"Aur'k."

"You sent me a roof," he said. "Twice."

"It was the same roof."

"It was better the second time. There were people on it." He took her bag out of her hand before she could keep it, and held it out level and still, and she watched his helm tip a degree. "Eleven. You left something behind."

"A notebook."

"Good," he said. "I've been priced for this trip down to the gram. I'd hate to be outweighed."

Daniel laughed, once, and covered his mouth. Aur'k turned his helm to him.

"Rocha," Daniel said, and held out his burnt hand, and Aur'k took it carefully, as he took everything in a body too heavy for him.

The machine at the end of the queue was a plain grey post with a reader in its top. Every passenger touched it, and it lit green and said their names. The small girl touched it for fun and it said VISITOR, and she was delighted.

Elena touched it.

It lit amber. VÁZQUEZ, E. — LINK: SUSPENDED.

The clerk behind it looked at the light, and at her, and said, "One moment," like a man who had said it to that light before. He turned to his own screen and began to read.

Elena stood with her hand still on the post. Behind her the queue stopped talking, politely, as a queue does with a problem in it.

"There's a clearance," she said. "Civic, New York."

"I see it. Held pending inquiry, then cleared." He scrolled. "By the civic director."

He looked up at the wall behind her, where Rachel was stopping on the steps again. Then at Elena.

"Her," Elena said.

"I'll need to confirm with the issuing office."

"It's four in the morning in New York."

"Then it may take a few minutes," he said kindly, and lifted his handset, and the board over the gate turned one line from white to amber. BOARDING 05:30.

Twenty-two minutes. She took her hand off the post.

"Elena." Daniel was at her shoulder. Not touching her. He was looking past her, at the doors, where the heat came in.

There was a man on a stick in the doorway.

He was in a field jacket the colour of Martian dust, in this heat, his sandy hair flat with sweat. He had come a long way overnight and up the field's road on foot, and his whole body said so. He stood with the stick planted and looked at the exits first, both of them. Then the faces. Then he found the one he wanted, and stopped looking.

Aur'k had not moved since the post went amber. Now he was completely still.

"You said someone has to watch the Gate," he said.

"Someone is." Kaelin came across the shed in two stages, the stick and then the rest of him. "Joaquín's got it till Tuesday. I bet him a flan I'd be back by Tuesday." He stopped in front of Aur'k and looked up. "Sunday afternoon vac. I'm on the one back tonight. Don't make a fuss."

"I'm not making a fuss."

"You've gone still. That's you making a fuss."

Aur'k put down the bag.

He put his arms round Kaelin, carefully, as if he had lifted him too fast once before. Kaelin dropped the stick. It went down on the shed's floor with a crack and nobody picked it up. He held on to Aur'k's plating with both hands, and the plating was hot from the field already, too hot for that, and Elena saw him feel it and hold on.

By the post the city chassis showed the girl a soft, puzzled face, and she copied it.

The clip came round on every wall. The queue moved round them like water round a post. The clerk's handset rang through to someone in New York who was not picking up. Still they held on, longer than people do in public. Elena looked at the floor so as not to watch, and found she was watching anyway.

Kaelin let go first. He was falling over.

Aur'k caught his elbow and put the stick back in his hand. Kaelin shook his fingers out, red across the palms. Then he tapped two fingers on his own forehead, twice, where a visor would be, and pointed.

Aur'k touched his helm once. Here.

"Dust take you," he said. "You're like a stove."

"It's the weight."

"It's been the weight since November." He turned on the stick and looked at Elena for the first time. "Elena."

"Kaelin."

He looked at the amber post and the board, and did the sum, and tipped his head at Daniel. "Don't spend it on me. Go on."

· · ·

The fence ran along the shed's far side where the field began. Families stood at it to watch the shuttle, and the dust came through the mesh onto everybody.

Daniel stood with his fingers hooked in it. The sky over the plain had gone grey at the bottom edge, and the board over the gate said FINAL CALL 05:40, and her line on it was still amber. The shuttle stood in its floodlight two kilometres off, steaming.

A dust devil came across the field with a dry hiss in it, close to the sound of rain on tin. Elena's head turned left, toward nothing. She turned it back. Daniel had seen.

She had planned it in order, a fault tree, branch by branch, so that by the end she would not have to say the last part. She found she didn't want to give it to him in order.

"I've been keeping something," she said.

"I know." He didn't look round. "Since the standpipe. You came down off that pole in February and your face was different, and you've kept it on that side of your face ever since." He touched his own left cheek. "That side. The side you listen with."

"You never asked."

"You'd have told me Nothing." He hooked his fingers through the mesh again. "I was waiting for you to want to."

"On the pole," she said. "In the rain. It came up out of my own head, in her voice, close, as if she was on the rung under me. One word. Nobody alive has it. I didn't put my hand back on the rung. I didn't want it to stop."

He waited.

"The four minutes at the controller, when you said my name. That was her rain. And afterwards, in the night's traffic, one frame from no chair at all, with her session's number on it. The same word in its tail."

She told him the word. She said it as Soria had said it, from the top bunk.

Daniel's fingers came off the mesh one at a time, as if it had gone hot. He said it once, under his breath, to hear its shape, and then he didn't say it again. "That's hers," he said. "That's not mine to say."

"And then I slept," she said. "On the panels. All night, the first time since June. Because it was her rain at the bottom of it. I knew it was. I knew it wasn't. I slept anyway."

"Is it her?" he said.

He had asked her that on the crooked roof in June, and she had said No, and been right. She had said it again in the rain on that roof, so the valley could hear, and meant it.

"I want it to be," Elena said.

She heard her own voice come up, out of the flat place she kept it, and didn't stop it.

"I know what it is. I've said what it is, on that roof, out loud. It wears whoever you'd stop for. And every time it says that word I want it to be her, Daniel. Every time. I sit in the rain to give it somewhere to be. I turn my head toward it in the street. I nearly let go of that pole for it, and I liked it."

Her hands had found each other in front of her. She took them apart.

"That's why I have to go. Not for Rachel. Not for the proof. Because the only thing that can tell me what's talking to me is on Mars, under a basin, and if I stay here I'll go on listening until I can't hear you any more. I'll go on until I'm in a bed with my hand on a wall, and I'll be glad. I'm going so I can stop wanting it. Or so I can know. I don't know which. I'm going anyway."

Along the fence a woman had turned to look at her. Elena didn't lower her voice.

"I should have told you in February," she said. "I'm telling you now because I'm leaving, and that's a coward's reason, and it's the one I've got."

Daniel didn't say it would be all right. He had never once said that to her.

He laid his burnt hand on her shirt, over the pocket with the folded page in it, and felt it crackle.

"What's this?"

"A list." She didn't take it out. "There were two of us on it. I drew a line through it."

"You kept it, though."

"I kept it."

He nodded. He crouched, as he crouched to work, so his face was under hers, and looked up at her.

"Then the hard one," he said. "When it's in front of you on Mars and it sounds like her. What do I do from here?"

"Keep the mesh ready," Elena said.

It came out steady, because it was true and it was all she could leave.

"Every roof. The north line to the last mast. Xinwei and the partner towns. The old man's box." She brought her voice down for the rest, to him only. "When it comes, the Gate will reach the people it can see first. Somebody has to hear everyone else. Keep it so they can hear each other, Daniel. Whatever I'm doing. Whatever it's saying to me."

"It's always ready," he said. "That's what it's for."

"Then keep it."

"Tá." He stood up, nearly laughing, as he laughed halfway through a repair that had gone wrong in an interesting place. "You came all this way to ask me to do my job."

"I came all this way to tell you the word."

"You did." He looked at her a long moment. "Say the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"You know which." He waited. "No? Fine. I'll say it." And he did, plainly, into the dust and the heat, as he said everything, with his hand still on the pocket with the list in it. She put her hand over his.

Over the gate the board turned its line from amber to white at 05:39. VÁZQUEZ, E. — CLEARED. CIVIC. Someone in New York had picked up. The clerk was on his feet, calling her name down the shed.

"Go," Daniel said. "Don't hold. You read Mara's post."

She went at a run, on Earth legs, and didn't look back, because if she looked back she would stop.

· · ·

The couches ran in pairs down the shuttle's narrow cabin, old and patched, smelling of other people's sweat and of the pad. The pad had come aboard in everybody's clothes. Aur'k's couch had been reinforced. Somebody had welded a second frame under it, badly, and painted the weld.

He lay down in it carefully and the frame took him with a sound. Elena lay down beside him. The straps came over her and drew in.

"How long?" she said.

"Fifty-one minutes to the ring. The ship lights at seven, with us or without." He turned his helm a degree toward her. "If we hold on the pad past two minutes, it's without."

At forty seconds the count held. Through the window a dust devil was walking across the pad's apron, brown and lazy, and the cabin waited for it. Nobody spoke. Aur'k read the hold's clock aloud, flat, every ten seconds. At a minute and fifty the devil wandered off the concrete and came apart, and the count went on.

The seats filled with people who had done this before and people who hadn't, and you could tell which by who held the armrests. Elena held hers.

At the count's end the shuttle did not leap. It leaned. The floor came up under her back and kept coming, a slow enormous hand. It didn't stop at the weight she knew. It went on past it, twice, three times, until her arms were too heavy to lift off the rests and her ribs had to work for every breath.

Beside her Aur'k's frame began to speak: a creak and a tick, plating seating against plating, the welded couch answering under him. He did not move. He read the numbers off the cabin screen aloud in his flat reading voice, one at a time, as if somebody were writing them down.

"Two point eight. Two point nine. Three."

"Does it hurt?" She could hardly make her mouth do it.

"It costs," he said. "Three point one."

The window by her shoulder was a small thick oval scratched with use. She turned her head to it, because that was all she could turn.

The ground was still in the night, and the city was lit.

São Paulo. All of it, falling away behind them as they climbed.

It ran to the edge of the world. Every ridge strung with towers, every river road a line of lights, and the sprawl of the hills going on past where any one thing could be seen, down to the haze.

The reservoirs lay among it like dropped coins, black, ringed with the lit roads that ran round their shores. Far to the south a dam lay across a valley as a thin bright line with darkness behind it. Twenty-two million people under her, under roofs that had been there yesterday and would be there tomorrow. Along the eastern edge the first copper of the sun lay on the horizon, and the shuttle's nose caught it before anything on the ground did.

Somewhere on the eastern edge, among the amber hills the relays had never reached, was a hill with a crooked roof. A hall with a water rota on the wall in thick marker. A man at a fence with his hand where the pocket had been. She looked until her eyes ached. She couldn't find it. She knew it was there.

Then cloud came up grey under them, and the sky went from grey to blue to a colour that wasn't blue, and the hand on her chest let go.

· · ·

The ship was longer than anything she had seen that wasn't a city.

It lay along the ring's port in the full sun, a spine as thin as a wire and a kilometre long. Crew rooms at the near end, a dark shield behind them. At the far end, spread like the wings of a held bird, the radiators. Close to, they were acres, glowing the deep red of the sprayers' sunsets over the reservoir. Under the crew rooms, in plain white letters: JAKARTA.

The shuttle came in nose first and touched, and the clamps took hold with a long shudder, and the cabin's light went from white to blue.

Elena floated a centimetre off the couch against her straps, and her stomach went somewhere it hadn't been asked to.

The screen over their couches filled with the ship's departure clearance.

JAKARTA — DEPARTURE — EARTH–MARS TRANSIT. Window: Signatories, 16 April. Fuel: Luna Haven. Passengers: 24. Mass: within margin. Traffic: routed through the ring. CIVIC — BATTERY.

And under it, where the routing signed:

Countersigned: AUR'K. 05:03 NEW YORK.

She read it twice. It said the same both times.

Three minutes past six, here: the first minute of the climb. She had been three times her own weight with her face to a window, and Aur'k beside her, reading numbers aloud. He had been off the Battery's floor since Saturday. He had been at her side since the gate.

She turned her head. He was looking out of his own window, or at nothing, his plating ticking as it cooled out of the climb.

She could keep it. She was good at keeping things, and she had stood at a fence that morning and given them away.

Elena reached up into the blue light, slowly, against nothing at all now, and turned the screen on its arm until it faced him.

He turned his helm to it. He read it.

Through the window behind him the radiators burned along the ship in the sun, and below them the whole round of the Earth turned under its white sky, the city somewhere on it too small to find. Aur'k did not move. He did not say anything. He went completely still, and stayed that way, and the ship around them began, very gently, to count.
