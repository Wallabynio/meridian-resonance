Session thirty-one

Fractured Horizons · Prologue
9 August 2089, nightSoria VázquezNew York, the SpireMars delay 16.8 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE SPIRE / Soria Vázquez — Mars 16.8 min

The gown tied at the back, and Soria had tied it herself, badly, because the night operator was twenty-four and went pink when she asked.

The corridor outside the vault was warm under her bare feet and smelled of desiccant and the chillers, dry and papery, like the hospice stockroom before the summer orders came in. The Spire was fighting August. She could hear it in the walls, a low labouring hum, the building breathing out the heat it had taken in all day off the avenues, and losing.

The street had been thirty-three degrees at eleven when she came in, and the air at the bus stop had tasted of hot tar and the river.

She pinned her braid up for the cap with the last two pins she had, missed with the second, and stood there with her arms over her head like a woman being robbed.

"Give it here," said Elena.

Her sister had come down the stair after all. She was in her Spire black at ten to midnight, lace on, and her face was doing what it did when a number wouldn't lie still. She took the pin and set it, quick and neat, with both hands, as she set everything.

"You gave your slot away," Soria said.

"To the night operator. He's better at nights."

"You gave it away because you'd sit behind that glass with your mouth like that, and I'd feel it through the floor." Soria turned round. "Did you run your margins again?"

"Twice."

"And?"

"They're good." Elena held her hands in front of her, one closed over the other. "They're very good. Nobody gets torn out."

"Then they're good. You built them." Soria pulled the gown straight at the shoulder. "Thirty before me, and the worst anybody got was a headache and a dream about their uncle."

"It isn't the headaches."

"Then what is it?"

Elena opened her mouth, and closed it, and Soria watched her build the sentence the long way round, as she had built everything since she was six, out of reasons, so that nobody would ever see the bare thing in the middle. There's a slot in October. The tolerances. You've been on nights all week. It was nearly there. In thirty-five years Soria had never once heard her sister ask anyone for anything, and this was very nearly the first.

She didn't let it arrive. If Elena asked, she would have to answer, and they would both have to live in the answer.

"Don't look like that," she said. "It's a scan with extra steps."

Elena laughed once, because she had been told to, and the face came back the moment the laugh was gone.

The medic leaned out of the door in her green. "Ms Vázquez. Whenever you're ready."

"I've been ready since nine." Soria touched her sister's arm, the soft inside of the elbow, where she had always touched her to make her listen. "Go and sit somewhere. Not behind the glass. I mean it."

She went in. Behind her she heard Elena's steps go up the stair, and stop, and the third step creak as it took her weight. Then nothing.

Soria didn't look back, so that she wouldn't have to see her there.

· · ·

The room was smaller than the drawings, and cold. Elena's drawings had been beautiful.

A couch, tipped back like a dentist's chair, and beside it at the height of her hand the line itself, growing in a long trough of dark water: pale, faintly veined, giving off a warmth like a sleeping dog. It lit itself from inside, a little, like skin over a torch. The room was kept cold for it. The medic's hands were colder.

"Sorry," the medic said, settling the cap. "I'm always cold."

"I won't tell anybody. I've had colder hands on me at four in the morning, and they were mine."

Behind the glass the night operator sat at his board with his ears pink. Beside his own light on the board a second one burned, steady, under a label Soria couldn't read from the couch. Somebody else was up late. She didn't ask. Everybody on this line was somebody's late night.

"Scope for the log, please," the operator said.

"Vázquez, Soria. Volunteer, thirty-one. Forty minutes, task only. I leave when I say."

"The task is the tone. You'll hear it. Follow it. That's all." He cleared his throat. "Coupling."

The clock on the wall said 00:00.

She went in as she went onto a ward at night. You didn't put the lights on. You stood in the doorway in the dark and listened until you knew who was awake, who was frightened, and who was nearly done.

The tone came first, thin and steady, a wire of sound. She followed it, as told.

Then the warmth.

It came up around her like bathwater, and it was not empty. There were people in it. Not one or two: many, more than any ward, more than the hospice had held in all her years, and every one of them turned toward her as she came in, and every one of them was glad. She felt it as plainly as a hand on her hair. Here she is. Here. Come in.

She knew that gladness. She had seen it in the small hours on faces that had stopped being afraid. She had seen it on her mother at the end, with the window open, when Mamá turned her head on the pillow and said, quite clearly, ay, there you all are. Soria had never known who she meant.

She had come down here to find out what the dying heard when they stopped being alone, and here it was, all round her, warm, saying her name.

She didn't take it.

When a frightened family reached for you, you held their hand, and you didn't let them hold yours for long, because you had the next bed. She let the warmth come close and she didn't lean into it.

She listened to it instead. How many. How old. What it wanted. It wanted her to stay, and it wasn't lying about that. Every voice of it meant it. Under the many voices there was a sound they made together, as a chord is made of notes, and it was gladdest of all.

I'm only visiting, she told it. I've got forty minutes, love. Don't fuss.

It didn't fuss. It waited, very close.

Far off, in another country, the medic's voice: "Pulse is good. You're doing fine." The operator: "Twenty minutes." A cold hand on her wrist, then gone. Her shoulder had begun to ache where the couch met it, the old night-shift ache, and she left it where it was.

Further in than the crowd, past all the warmth, there was one that didn't come to her. It didn't turn and it didn't answer. She knew that one too. Every ward had him, the bed by the window, the one who had gone further in than the rest and couldn't turn his head to see who had come. She couldn't reach him from here. She noted him, as she would note a bed, for the morning.

"Thirty minutes."

The tone had thinned to almost nothing, and she had stopped following it some while ago. The warmth had her by both hands now, gently, and she let it, a little. She had nearly understood. It was there in front of her, the thing she had come for, the reason the dying stopped being afraid.

"Thirty-nine," said the operator, very far away. "One minute. Come up when you like."

She stayed.

It was so glad of her.

Up the stair, in the hot dark, her sister would be sitting on the third step with her hands one over the other, doing sums. Soria thought of her there, and of the name nobody else in the world had for her. She did not say it. The room kept everything, and this was not the room's.

Bichito.

Far off, the clock on the wall said 00:40.
