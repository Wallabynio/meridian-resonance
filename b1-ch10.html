Fractured Horizons · Chapter 10

The breathing walls

Fractured Horizons · II-A. The bridge

10

22 AprilElenaLuna Haven, the Rows and the TubesMars delay 5.4 min

THE COMPACT — LUNA HAVEN, THE ROWS / Elena Vázquez — Mars 5.4 min

The board at the first junction said a hundred, and someone had been rubbing it out for weeks.

Elena stood in front of it with her bag on her shoulder and the shuttle's deceleration still in her knees. AIR TODAY, in chalk, and under it the number, written large and straight. The slate kept what had been written on it before. Under the hundred she could read three ghosts, worked into the grain by the heel of a hand: ninety-four, ninety-two, and a lower one that had been rubbed out harder than the rest.

A series. Someone had counted down, and then, all at once, back up.

The Tubes were changing shift around her. People came past in soft shoes and close layers the colours of earth, speaking low, and her lace took their Talk and handed it to her in English a fraction behind their lips. Two keepers in dark suits stopped at the board beside her and read the number out of habit.

"How's the Gulf?" said one.

"Worse. The fever's not broken." The other kept her eyes on the slate. "My mother says the arcades are full by nine and nobody goes out till dark."

"Mine says it's early this year."

"Mine says that every year." She touched the wall as she went on, two fingers, as if she were touching the hand of somebody in a bed.

Past the board, where the honey light in the wall ran thickest, four children were asleep in a row. They sat on the floor with their backs to the wall, heads tipped against it, bags in their laps, waiting for someone off the night shift. The smallest had her cheek flat to the wall and her mouth open. None of them stirred when a cart went by.

Her lace chimed. Daniel. Are you lying down? Send proof.

She had not slept on the shuttle. She had read the protocol again instead, all ninety pages, in the dark. The page she kept going back to was the plan of the Rows, and the one room at the end of them.

Soon, she wrote. They sleep against the walls here.

The answer came back almost at once. Try the wall.

She was smiling at it when Mara Ekeberg came round the curve of the Tubes.

She knew her from the review's file: the braid, the dark suit, the palm laid on the wall as she walked. Mara stopped a metre off. She looked at Elena's face, then at her jacket, then at her face again.

The Haven pause went by.

"I asked the review for a medic," Mara said. "They sent me an architect."

"They sent the review's observer."

"Systems architect, Foresight. It's on your form." The palm stayed on the wall. "Why you?"

"Because the review needs someone on the wall who has read the protocol."

"I've read it."

"Then you know what the scope says." Elena gave it to her, because it was the best thing she had. "One sweep. Twenty minutes, start to end. Four keepers in the loop, chosen by the lattice keeper. The lattice keeper on the lever and not in the loop. The lever pulled at the keeper's word alone. If it is pulled, the test is over and is not repeated. Every after-report on the four goes to the review unedited."

Mara heard her out to the end. People at home did not do that.

"Word for word," she said. "You've read it more often than I have, and I signed it." She took her hand off the wall. "Iverson's face I'd started to trust. You're not her."

"No."

"The sweep's at eleven. The review wouldn't let it run without its observer on site, so my four have been waiting since the scope was signed." Mara turned back into the Tubes. "Waiting for you. You can stand by me. Don't touch the walls."

Elena followed her into the Tubes, overshot the first long step on an Earth stride, and caught the rail. Behind them a man in a keeper's suit had found the four children and was crouching to wake the smallest, and the child did not want to be woken. She turned her face back into the warm.

· · ·

The session room was the coolest place she had been since the lock, and everything in it was alive but two things.

The long low couch had been grown out of the floor, of a piece with the wall behind it and lit from inside the same honey colour.

Four keepers lay along it with their heads turned to the wall, so that the uncapped socket behind each one's ear rested against the light. Two men, two women. The oldest, a grey-bearded man called Ravi Castellanos, had brought a folded blanket for his knees. The youngest, Asha Kimani, had laughed at him for it and then put her own feet under the end of it.

The first thing not alive was the lever by the door, yellow and black, worn bright at the grip. Mara stood with her hand on it.

The second was the Charter's. A squat grey drum the size of a bucket, clamped to the living wall at the head of the couch, its cable running out under the door toward the surface mast. It carried the Foresight mark. Elena had seen the drawings in January. She had not expected it to look so much like a thing somebody had put down and forgotten to move.

"Coupling ready," the drum said, in the Spire's flat procedural voice. "Sweep at 11:00. Duration, twenty minutes."

Elena opened the observer's log at the edge of her eye. Four traces, one for each keeper, came up green and steady. A fifth line lay under them, the sweep, still flat. At the foot of the log sat the field for a stop, outlined in red. Rachel's red line, carried up to the Moon.

Six years ago, in the vault under the Spire, she had watched traces like these, of people who had volunteered.

"Eleven," said the drum. "Coupling live."

The sweep line rose.

Nothing in the room moved. Then all four keepers let go, together, in their shoulders, as a crowd does when the music starts. Ravi's hand came off the blanket and lay open on the couch. Asha smiled with her eyes shut, at nothing Elena could see.

Elena watched the traces. They were good traces. The sweep climbed through them on the curve the Lantern's engineers had promised, and the lattice took it, and the walls of the room brightened very slightly, all at once, and the room drew a breath.

She had wanted to be on that couch since January. She had not known it until now, standing a metre from it with a log open, watching four strangers lie down where she wanted to be.

Minute nine. Minute twelve. Mara's knuckles on the lever had gone pale, and stayed pale.

At 11:16 Asha's lips moved.

They moved on a word, the same word, three times, a short one, and nothing came out. Her hand on the couch closed and opened. Her trace stayed green. The word kept not coming, and on the fourth try she gave up on it, and her face went smooth and pleased again, as if the word had not mattered after all.

The stop field sat at the foot of Elena's log, red-edged. Pulling the lever was Mara's alone. Flagging it was hers. A flag in the log at 11:16 would sit beside the sweep until the review read it.

She did not touch it.

When she looked up, Mara was not watching Asha. Mara was watching her.

Mara said nothing. Her hand stayed where it was.

At 11:20 the drum said, "Sweep complete. Coupling released," and clicked, and the walls dimmed back to what they had been.

The four came up slowly, like people coming out of warm water. Ravi sat up first and rubbed his face with both hands and laughed into them.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that was lovely."

"It was like the—" Asha's hand made a shape in the air, a rounded thing, a bowl or a sun. She laughed at herself. "The. You know."

"Warm," said Mara.

"Warm," Asha agreed, gratefully, and took it.

A medic from the Ward came for them with a chair nobody needed. They went down the Rows in a slow cluster, talking low, bumping each other, Ravi with his blanket over his shoulder like a man going home from a beach.

Elena was alone with Mara and the couch. Four dents were going out of it as she watched, the grown stuff easing back flat.

She put her hand on it. It was warm.

"Don't," said Mara.

Elena left her hand where it was. "The review's observer reads four reports tonight and signs that four people are well. She has never been where they went. She doesn't know what she's reading." She made herself take the hand away. "I'd like a session. Bounded. Your scope, your lever. Ten minutes."

"Why?"

"So I know what I'm signing."

Mara looked at her for a long moment.

"My scope has four names on it," she said. "Yours isn't one." She opened the door. "Read their reports. You'll know by tonight."

· · ·

At Haven's evening the Tubes dimmed, and the Ward kept its light.

Its beds had been grown into the wall, a long row of them, so that everyone in them lay with the warm at their back. The four keepers had the beds at the end. Outside the Ward's tall narrow door a girl of about four was asleep sitting up against the wall, a cup of broth cooling on the floor by her knee.

"Asha's," said the Ward medic, following Elena's look. "She'll wait till her mother comes out. She always does. You can't move her. I've tried."

The medic was tall, taller than anyone Elena had seen up here except the Haven-born, and she spoke English with New York in it. She had the four's reports open on a slate and was reading them standing up, as Mara did.

"Do you want them raw," she said, "or do you want me?"

"Both."

"Good." The medic glanced up. "Before you start. If anyone from the Battery asks, I called my mother."

"Did you?"

"I'm going to. My mother calls my cousin when I don't answer, and when Rachel Iverson came up here for your test, my cousin asked her to pass it on. His boss. On the Moon." She shook her head at the slate. "I'm calling her tonight."

Elena read them in order.

The two at the near end were well. One had after-images, honey light when she shut her eyes, and was enjoying them. The other had slept since the sweep and woken hungry, and was eating his second bowl of mushrooms with the absorption of a man who had been told he could.

Ravi was sitting up against his wall, telling the medic about his allotment.

"The oyster ones come up in a week, if you keep them wet. The trick is—" He stopped.

He stopped as people stop when they hear their name called in another room. His head turned a little toward the wall. His eyes went to the middle distance and stayed there.

Elena counted. Six seconds. Seven.

"The trick is the dark," he said, from somewhere else in the sentence, and smiled at them both, and did not seem to know he had been gone.

Asha was holding a cup. She held it up to Elena, pleased, offering, her Talk arriving a fraction behind her lips.

"Do you want—" Her eyes went to the cup. Her mouth made the shape of the word and then the shape of a different word. "Do you want the. This."

"Cup," said the medic, lightly.

"Cup," said Asha, and laughed. "Cup. It's been doing that since lunch. I keep putting things down somewhere safe." She drank from it. "Is my girl still out there?"

"Still out there."

"She'll wait."

Elena opened the protocol at the edge of her eye, page twelve, the table she had read on the shuttle. Expected effects after a bounded session. Fog. After-images. Disturbed sleep. Transient word-finding difficulty, resolving within seventy-two hours.

Transient word-finding difficulty. There it was. It covered Asha. It covered the cup.

She looked for the line that covered Ravi, and did not find one, and read fog again, and let it.

"She's bleeding a little," said the medic, beside her. "Words. Ravi too. Six hours out."

"It's on the table." Elena heard her own voice, level and helpful, the voice for a review room. "Transient word-finding difficulty."

"Does it give an onset?"

"No."

"No," the medic agreed, and wrote a line on her slate, and did not show it to her.

Mara had come in without either of them hearing. She stood by Ravi's bed with her palm flat on the wall above his head and said nothing at all, and when Ravi reached up without looking and patted her hand, she let him.

It was the normal cost. The normal cost of linking, paid in small change.

She said it to herself again on her way out, and stepped round the sleeping girl at the door.

· · ·

Earth hung in the dome over the Commons, the Gulf under a haze the colour of weak tea.

Elena sat at a table by the wall with the note open in front of her, and the Commons empty but for a choir somewhere down the Tubes, going over the same three bars.

Observer's note. Lantern bounded test, Luna Haven, 22 April. Four after-reports attached, unedited.

Finding:

There were two choices, and she had helped write the form that offered them.

No finding. Or: Finding: review to sit.

She knew where the second one went. It went to Rachel, who would read it at the Battery before breakfast and call a sitting because her rule said she must. The sitting would take the coupling off Haven's wall until it had sat, and the Lantern's schedule would wait on it, and Bellamy would know whose note had stopped it.

The observer's posting would end with the test, and the observer would be on the next shuttle down, with ninety pages read and nothing seen.

No finding, and the test closed clean. Haven's lattice stayed in the Lantern, and the observer's secondment ran on to the review's close: weeks on Haven, one wall away from the Rows, and time to ask Mara again.

The only living lattice she could reach would be a slate number on a board, a day away, behind a woman who did not trust her face.

Her hands had found each other on the table. She took them apart and wrote No finding.

Under it, in the box for the observer's remarks, she wrote: Effects within the protocol's expected range (p. 12). Word-finding difficulty in two of four, transient. Monitoring continues.

Every word of it was true. She read it twice, and it was true both times.

Mara sat down across from her.

She had not heard her come. Mara laid both palms on the table, flat, and looked at Elena, and the pause went out and came back, longer than the Moon needed.

"Are my people all right?"

"They're within the expected range."

"That's not what I asked."

The choir stopped in the middle of its bar and started again from the top.

"Yes," said Elena.

Mara looked at her a moment longer. Then she nodded once, as if she would keep the answer, and stood, and put her palm on the wall as she went.

Elena signed the note.

The log took it, and wrote in its margin in small grey letters, as she had built it to: Filed 21:42 — E. Vázquez, observer. Under her name the four after-reports went up raw and unedited, as Rachel's rule required, Ravi's seven seconds and Asha's cup and the medic's line she had not been shown, all of it, where anyone on the review could read it.

She sat on under the dome until the choir finished.

As far as she could tell, nobody read the raw. In five weeks nobody on the review had said one word about the question she had sent on the hard-stop channel.

· · ·

The girl was still at the Ward door when Elena came back past it, and Asha was crouched beside her, lifting her, the child coming up too easily, light as a coat, warm and boneless and cross.

Asha saw her over the girl's shoulder and grinned.

"Look at you," she said, in Talk. "You look like my night shift."

"I haven't slept."

"I can tell." Asha shifted her daughter higher on her hip. "Sleep's only practice, you know. And you need the practice."

Elena stood still in the dim Tubes.

Soria had said that. Not like it. That.

In the spring of 2089, at four in the morning, with her hand on Elena's arm, to Elena half asleep over a desk, and Elena had laughed with her face still on her sleeve: Sleep's only practice, you know. And you need the practice. Elena had not heard it since. She had never heard anyone else say it at all.

And Asha had said it in English, every word, the only English Elena had heard from her all day. Her lace had had nothing to translate.

"Where did you hear that?"

Asha thought about it. Her face went puzzled, then easy.

"I don't know," she said. "It's just a thing people say."

She carried the girl away down the Tubes, the child's face turned into her neck, and the walls lit her as she went.

Elena put her palm where the girl had slept. The wall was warm. An hour ago she had signed that it was the normal cost.
