The wall was still flickering when Mara counted six hours.

She had started counting somewhere around hour two, because counting was a thing a body did in sequence, one number reliably following the last, and sequence was the specific property she had spent the last six hours discovering she could no longer take for granted. The Corridors around her held the low, careful order of a place mid-triage: three sections sealed behind temporary membrane, a maintenance crew working the eastern junction in the unhurried, methodical rhythm of people who had drilled for structural failure and were, like everyone else tonight, discovering this was not that failure. The bioluminescence along the walls pulsed in a rhythm Mara had known for three years the way she knew her own pulse, and it was wrong now in a way none of the instruments walking past her with scanners had managed to name: not dimmer, not slower, but stuttering, a heartbeat that kept losing and finding its own tempo, over and over, the way her own breath had been doing since the cascade.

She was crouched at the base of the western junction, palm flat against the wall, ostensibly checking a nutrient-flow reading for the crew leader who had asked her twenty minutes ago and who she had, she was fairly sure, not yet answered. She meant to answer. She had the number — she could feel it, a specific pressure differential sitting somewhere in her awareness the way a held breath sits in the chest — but between having the number and saying it there was a distance that kept opening under her, wider each time, and somewhere in the last twenty minutes she had stopped being confident the hand on the wall was the only place she currently was.

It was not like before. Before — an hour ago, six hours ago, whichever "before" she reached for — the scattering had come and gone in seconds, a door opening and closing too fast to walk through. This was slower and stranger: not a door now so much as a room she kept discovering she was standing in two places within, her palm against the western junction and, at the same time, faint and specific and undeniable, some other point along the network's fractal reach where the substrate's signal terminated and something on the far side of it went on being curious about her. She did not have a number of locations. She had tried, twice, to count them, and both times the counting itself had become the thing she lost hold of.

"Mara."

Jīn's voice arrived from somewhere she located, after a moment that took longer than it should have, as directly behind her. She turned her head — one head, she was reasonably sure, though the confidence in that had become a thing she had to check rather than assume — and found them crouched at her level, diagnostic stylus loose in one hand, not touching her, the way they had learned over three years that she needed room before contact.

"I have the flow reading," she said. Her voice came out steadier than the rest of her felt, the same trick her body had performed once already tonight, over comms, in the minutes right after the rift closed. "I just haven't said it yet."

"That's all right." Jīn's eyes moved over her the way they moved over an instrument panel — not clinically, but with the specific attentiveness of someone who had learned to read consequence off a surface before the surface admitted to it. "Where are you right now?"

It was not a rhetorical question, and she understood, distantly, gratefully, that it was the correct one — not are you all right, which she could not have answered, but where, which she could at least attempt.

"Here." Her palm, against the wall, was unambiguous; she held onto it the way she had learned, twenty minutes ago, to hold onto anything unambiguous. "And also — I can feel the eastern seal. Not see it. Feel it, the way I feel this wall, except I'm not touching it." She heard herself say the sentence and heard, underneath it, how little sense it should have made to anyone who had not spent the last six hours living inside the fact of it. "And a third place. Further. I don't have a name for where. It isn't frightening exactly. It's — attention. Something old, paying attention, the way the node did in the Mycelial Chamber, except I'm not choosing to be near it this time. I just am."

Jīn was quiet for a moment in the specific way they went quiet before saying something they had decided, carefully, was true rather than merely comforting.

"I looked at the structural logs from the last six hours," they said. "The wall's stutter tracks your pulse rate to within half a second, every time. It isn't damage responding to you. It's responding with you. Whatever's happening to you, the substrate is inside it too — not causing it, not separate from it. With you."

"That's not better."

"No," Jīn agreed. "It isn't better. It's just true, and I thought you'd rather have true than better."

She almost laughed, and the almost-laugh cost her something — a half-second in which the western junction under her palm and the eastern seal and the third, distant, patient attention all held equal weight in her awareness, none of them more here than the others, and she had to work, deliberately, the way she worked a difficult splice in the mycorrhizal grid, to bring the wall back to the front of everything and let the rest recede to somewhere she could set down without dropping.

"I keep trying to come all the way back," she said, when the wall was the loudest thing again. "I did it once already tonight. Over comms, right after — I said the resonance was holding, and it sounded like me, and I believed it was me, and then an hour passed and I was three places again and I hadn't noticed the seam where I'd left."

Jīn set the stylus down on the floor beside them, which she understood, from three years of watching them work, as a decision to stop being someone taking a reading and become, fully, someone sitting with her.

"I don't think 'coming all the way back' is the right frame," they said. "I've been running the numbers since the cascade closed, and none of them describe a system returning to baseline. They describe a system finding a new baseline. I don't know yet what that baseline is. I don't think you're going to reassemble into exactly what you were an hour before the rift opened. I think — and I could be wrong, I want to be wrong — I think this might be what you are now."

The words should have frightened her more than they did. She turned them over, the way Jīn turned over a reading before committing to it, and found, underneath the fear that was there and real and not going anywhere soon, something else: a kind of relief at being told the true shape of a thing rather than a kinder, smaller shape that would only have to be corrected later, alone, in the dark, the way she had spent the last six hours quietly failing to correct it herself.

"Then I need you to help me learn how to be it," she said. "Not fix it. I don't think it's the kind of thing that has a fix. I need to learn how to be three places and still be able to say a number out loud when a crew leader asks me for one."

"I can do that." Jīn reached, finally, and put a hand — carefully, watching her face the whole time for the flinch that did not come — against the wall beside her own, the two of them anchored to the same warm, stuttering pulse of light. "I can't promise I know how yet. But I'm not going anywhere while we find out."

The wall's light, under both their palms, held its uneven rhythm a moment longer — and then, so gradually Mara could not have said exactly when it happened, began to steady, not into the old rhythm but into a new one, imperfect and clearly hers, that the substrate seemed willing, for now, to keep.

"Nutrient flow at the western junction," she said, to no one in particular, because it was the sentence she had owed the crew leader for twenty-six minutes and saying it, finally, felt like proof that a number could still travel the whole distance from her mind to her mouth. "Point-four-one, within tolerance."

Complete unhurried attention, the only response she has to the incomprehensible, is all that is legible in her posture — no urgency performed, no despair either.

The deep-interface chamber stayed sealed through the night, which meant Léa did her analysis from the observation platform, six metres above the floor where a wood splinter and a soil drift and a section of luminous canopy had, some hours ago, stopped being theoretical.

She had slept for eleven minutes in a chair not designed for it and did not intend to try again soon. The structural team's damage report sat open on one display — non-critical, the wall had said, and the wall had been right, three cracked support struts and a compromised sensor array being the entire material cost of a two-minute-eleven-second cascade that had otherwise rewritten what everyone in this chamber understood the Meridian to be. On a second display sat the thing that actually mattered: forty-two seconds of raw telemetry from the aperture's uncontrolled expansion, more data than the previous eleven weeks combined, arranged in a shape her instruments had not been built to hold.

She began where she always began, because beginning was the only discipline reliable enough to trust when nothing else was: she read it as it was, not as she wished it to be. The model had failed because the model assumed a door. She wrote that sentence at the top of a fresh page and did not soften it, because a report that protected the model's dignity over the truth would fail everyone who read it after her.

Jīn's message came through a little after four in the morning, brief and precise in the way all of Jīn's messages were precise, and told her more than its length should have allowed: Mara's stabilising. Not the way we hoped. A new baseline, not a return to the old one. I'll have readings for you by morning. Léa read it twice, set it aside, and found that the second reading changed something in how she looked at her own forty-two seconds of telemetry — not the numbers, which stayed exactly what they were, but the frame around them. She had been treating the cascade as an event with a beginning and an end, contained inside a timestamp. Jīn's message told her, gently and without asking her to agree yet, that for at least one person in this colony the cascade had no end recorded anywhere in her instruments, because it was still, this morning, in progress.

"Query," she said, to the platform's own quiet system, warm and unhurried the way every Luna Haven voice was warm and unhurried, entirely unsuited, still, to what it had spent the night reporting. "Cross-reference the aperture's growth-rate anomaly against Corridor substrate stress readings, same forty-two-second window."

"Working." A pause, longer than the query should have required, the kind of pause Léa had learned over eleven weeks to read as the system finding something it did not have a clean category for. "Correlation coefficient point-nine-four. The Habitat Corridors substrate registered the growth-rate anomaly within four hundred milliseconds of its onset in the deep-interface chamber, six levels away, through no instrumented channel currently on record."

Léa looked at the number for a long moment. Point-nine-four was not a number instruments produced by accident. It was the number two systems produced when they were, in some fashion her equipment had no name for yet, the same system.

She did not write the Corridors are alive in a way we have been measuring but not believing in her session notes, because it was not yet a claim she could defend, and she had spent twenty years refusing to let an idea's size excuse it from evidence. She wrote, instead, the thing she could defend: Substrate response correlated to aperture anomaly at point-nine-four, zero instrumented pathway identified. Recommend Ekeberg readings be treated as primary data, not incidental medical observation, effective immediately. It was a smaller sentence than the one she had wanted to write. It was also, she thought, setting the stylus down at last and letting her eyes close for the first time in eleven hours, the first sentence in this whole catastrophic night that had told the complete truth without apologising for the size of it.

Xiàng was three sentences into a courtesy conversation with a Corporate Union metrologist he had no particular reason to be speaking with, in the tri-colour hush of the western concourse, when the dread arrived.

It did not build. It was simply, all at once, present — a cold, specific weight low in his chest, entirely without cause, the physiological signature of watching something irreversible happen to someone he loved, except there was no one in front of him it could plausibly belong to. The metrologist kept talking. Xiàng heard perhaps one word in four of it and understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the conversation, that whatever this was, it was not his alone.

He excused himself before the sentence finished and crossed the atrium at a pace just short of the one that would have drawn attention, gold light sliding into blue across the floor ahead of him the way it had the day he and Eddy first sat at that mezzanine table, six years of correspondence arriving finally at a shared object. He found Eddy in the transition corridor near the eastern rail, notebook closed in one hand, standing very still in a way Xiàng had never once seen him stand — not the settled patience Xiàng recognised from their first meeting, but something braced, a man holding a position against a weight only he could feel.

Xiàng did not need to ask. He saw his own face on Eddy's, the exact configuration of it, the same cold pressure behind the same held stillness, and something in him that had spent fourteen years cataloguing anomalies at a controlled remove recognised, immediately and without any need for instruments, that this one had happened to both of them at once.

"You felt it too," Xiàng said. It was not a question.

"I felt it too." Eddy's voice came out low, careful, a man checking his own words for cracks before he trusted them with weight. "Whatever it was. It's gone now. It went as fast as it came." He looked at Xiàng for a long moment, the tri-colour light lying gold-to-blue across both their faces exactly as it had the first time, and something in his expression settled, finally, into something Xiàng could name. "That was real."

"Yes." Xiàng did not try to talk himself out of it, though fourteen years of institutional caution offered him several ready-made ways to. "I don't know what it was. I know it wasn't mine alone, and I know you had it at the same moment, in a different room, with no channel between us that either of our instruments could show a colleague."

"That's not telepathy." Eddy said it the way he said everything that mattered to him, precise rather than dramatic, already reaching past the fear of the sensation toward the shape of the data it implied. "Telepathy would mean I could read your thoughts. I couldn't. I don't know what you were thinking two minutes ago and I still don't. I only know what you felt, and only for the length of the feeling."

"Bounded," Xiàng said, and heard, as he said it, the specific relief of a scientist handed the correct word before the wrong ones could take root. "Not open. Bounded resonance. Real, measurable, and finished the moment it finished — not a channel we can leave open, a signal that arrived once, together, and then stopped."

"Bounded resonance," Eddy repeated, testing the shape of it, and something in the set of his shoulders eased by a degree that was not relief exactly but was, at least, orientation — the specific comfort two careful men took in having a category for something too large to hold without one. "I'd like to know where it came from before I decide how frightened to be about it."

"So would I." Xiàng looked past Eddy, out through the transition corridor's glass toward the Gardens Relay's canopy trunks rising green and gold in the evening light, and felt, underneath the fading cold weight in his chest, the specific quiet certainty of a man recognising the shape of the next six years of his life arriving early. "I don't think it will take long to find out. Something that large doesn't stay unexplained for more than a day."

Below them, the transit system chimed its evening notice in the same soft, neutral register it gave every announcement in this jurisdiction that belonged fully to no one — no urgency in it, no allegiance, information offered equally to whoever needed it — and neither man moved for a moment to answer it, standing instead in the fading tri-colour light with the first confirmed proof, between them, that whatever had opened in Luna Haven's deep-interface chamber had already stopped respecting the distance between where things happened and who was made, without being asked, to feel them.
