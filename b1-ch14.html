Fractured Horizons · Chapter 14

The first sleeper

Fractured Horizons · II-A. The bridge

14

28 MayKaelinMars, Cobalt Basin StationMars delay 4.2 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Kaelin Drake — Earth 4.2 min

Petra caught him dealing off the bottom, and said so, and was right.

"I deal from where the good ones are," Kaelin said.

"That's the bottom. That's what the bottom's for." She held her hand fanned flat against her chest so he couldn't see it, though he had dealt it and knew every one. "I'm telling Ines."

"Ines knows. Ines taught me."

At the next bunk Ines didn't look up from Bao's cap. "I taught him to cut a dressing. The rest he stole."

Medical had five bunks, and this morning three were full. Selma lay in the second with a scanner cap over her hair and her boots still on, because she meant to be up the minute Ines let her. Bao lay in the third, reading a pump manual on his handheld with his lips moving. They came down on caps for every sweep now. They hated it, and they came.

Petra had the fifth bunk, as she had since April, with her hair scraped up out of the cap's road and the lighthouse deck squared on the blanket by her knee, and this morning she was up, really up, for most of an hour. It was the longest in a week. She had known his name the whole time. He had counted.

"Hearts," she said, and laid a spade.

He let it go.

"You let that go." She narrowed her eyes at him. "Funmi never let anything go."

"Funmi would've had it off you before it left your hand."

Petra laughed, and it was her old laugh, too loud for the room. Bao shushed her without looking up from his manual, and she threw a lighthouse at him and missed.

Kaelin looked at his wrist. His arm still did that on its own. One minute.

On the sky repeater over the bunks the light had gone the colour of weak tea. The season's dust had been coming over the rim since dawn, and the arrays on the berm went under a little more each hour. The crew who went up to brush them came back looking as if they had been rolled in paprika.

On the overlay above Petra's bunk her trace ran low and even. It would swell soon. It always swelled.

"Your lead," he said.

She studied her hand. Her fingers picked at the edge of the fan, restless as they had been on the rig, as they were at the slate. She drew one out and looked at it and put it back.

"You've been dealing off the—" she said.

On the overlay the swell came up.

She didn't finish. Kaelin waited for the small shake of the head, for her to come back puzzled and say what? as Selma had on the frame. Her hand went down slowly onto the blanket. The fan slid apart. A lighthouse tipped over the edge of the bunk and lay face up on the deck.

"Petra."

Her eyes were open. They were on him, and then they weren't on anything.

The swell went down on the overlay. Then the line lay flat, and stayed flat. Under it her heart and her breath ran on, steady as a pump.

Ines was at the bunk before he said her name. She laid her hand flat on Petra's breastbone and said, loud and cheerful, "Petra. Petra Lind. Squeeze my hand." She shone a light in each eye. She pressed a thumbnail hard into the bed of Petra's nail, and Kaelin flinched, and Petra didn't.

Petra breathed. He counted it. Twelve in the minute. Her chest came up under Ines's hand and went down.

Selma had sat up in the second bunk with her cap askew. Bao had put the manual face down on his chest.

Kaelin picked the lighthouse off the deck and put it back in her fingers. They didn't close on it.

"Your lead," he said.

Nothing.

"Funmi's cheating. She's got the tide gauges in her overlay. I watched her eyes go."

Petra's eyes stayed where they were. Somewhere past his shoulder, past the repeater, past the dust.

"She's breathing," he said.

"She is." Ines kept her hand where it was. She looked at him across the bunk, and he knew the look. He had watched her give it across a lot of bunks to a lot of people, and never to him. "She's gone quiet, Kaelin. The body's here. She isn't."

"Then she's somewhere."

"Then she's somewhere I can't reach with anything on this station, and I've tried everything on this station on worse than her."

Kaelin counted Petra's breaths to sixty, because he could. Then he stood up.

"Send her home," he said.

"Home where?"

"Earth. A hospital with a scanner that cost more than this place." He was already working it, the route out, as he would work a crawler's route in a storm. "There's a ship up there. The programme's. It goes home after the opposition."

"The Funafuti." Ines took her hand off Petra at last. "Your share's spent. You spent May on the first."

"Then whose isn't?"

"Medical's." She was pulling the form up on the overlay already, beside Petra's flat line. "A transfer doesn't come off anybody's share. That's the one thing they got right."

She wrote it plainly. Name. The trace since March. This morning, the time to the second. Request medical transfer, Earth, earliest berth. Attendant care available aboard. She signed it with her thumb, and held the plate out to him, and he put his thumb under hers.

· · ·

The reply took a quarter of an hour. Kaelin spent it on the crate, counting Petra's breaths, and then the chief sent for him.

She had it on her desk screen when he came into ops.

Medical transfer request received. Return berths aboard the Funafuti are allocated under the Lantern programme. Crew member named is station personnel. Refer to station allocation, next window. No further action.

"I didn't send it to you," Kaelin said.

"Medical copies command. That's the form." She didn't turn the screen away. "I'm sorry about Petra."

"Show me the manifest."

She looked at him for a moment, and then she showed him. FUNAFUTI. DEPARTS MARS ORBIT 9 JUNE. Under it, fourteen berths. Eleven had names in them, and none of the names were from the station. The last three lines were grey.

PROGRAMME RESERVE.

"Those are empty," he said.

"They're reserved."

"For what?"

"For the programme."

He counted them again. Three bunks, paid for in fuel to the gram, going home with nobody in them.

"She drilled their bores," he said. "Bore three. She put her hand in the rock for them."

"I know."

"Then she's programme."

"She's on station strength. Station strength goes home on the station's own ship. The next one leaves Earth in April. It's here in June. It goes back when it goes back."

"That's a year."

"And a bit."

On the wall behind her the schedule had changed since the first. The Mars end ran green across it to today, and today was ticked. Beside the tick, in a new colour, a line Kaelin hadn't seen before: TEST PULSE. EARTH END TO MARS END. 21:00.

"They finished," he said.

"Your three clamped the last runs at eleven. It went green while you were in medical." She said it without any edge. That was worse. "First full pulse tonight. It lights at the boundary. Field operations signs."

"And Selma and Bao?"

"Off the frame and out of the loop. The review on Earth has a new stop. Any word lost in the loop halts the sweep." She folded her hands on the desk. "They aren't in the loop."

"You took them off so it wouldn't stop."

"I took them off because you told me on the first that Bao had four weeks on that frame. I'm sorry it took Earth to make me." She meant that too. He could see her mean it.

Kaelin put both hands on the back of the ops chair.

"Ask them again," he said. "You. Command. Not medical. Ask for one of the grey ones."

"I'll ask. They'll say what they said."

"Then tell them field ops won't sign tonight." He heard how steady it came out. "Not until she has a berth. I'll sign anything you like after. Every pulse to the opposition. Just one of those three."

The duty officer at the next screen had stopped pretending to read it.

The chief looked at Kaelin for a long time. He had watched her do that across this desk often enough to know she was doing the arithmetic, honestly, to the end, and to know before she moved how it came out.

"Then command signs it," she said.

She stood and laid her palm on the plate. The screen wrote her name on the field-operations line, where his had gone in January under a director's code, and where it had gone every time since.

"It doesn't need your name, Kaelin," she said. "It needs a name. I'm sorry."

He looked at the plate for a while. Then he went back down to medical.

· · ·

Earth night came round with the sky shut.

By evening the dust had closed over the station, the arrays on the berm had gone under for the season and nobody was going up to brush them any more, and the galley's sky repeater showed brown, and then a darker brown, and then nothing at all. The greenhouse lights had gone to half, and everybody was in two layers, and the passages smelled of grit and warm wiring.

The feed got through anyway. New York was minutes old and white. Every frame had a red band along the bottom of it. Heat warning for the Polder. Heat warning for the Shallows. Heat warning for the wall itself, which Kaelin hadn't known you could have.

A street camera in the Polder showed the canopies misting at a corner and not one person under them, and Kaelin, who had never in his life watched weather without counting who was out in it, counted nobody and liked it less.

The slate had gone over to it. Forty-one by Thursday. Breaks Saturday, storm. Doesn't break. That last was Selma's, and she had underlined doesn't.

Bao had cooked. It turned out Bao could cook. He had done noodles in the big pan with greens from the half-lit greenhouse and the last of the third bottle of hot sauce, and the galley smelled of it, and nobody had said a word about the hot sauce.

"He counted them," Selma said. "Every noodle. He thinks they're runs."

"They're better than runs," Bao said. "Runs don't need salt."

Kaelin stood in the hatch with his plate. The step-crate was taken. Bao had chalked the morning's brush rota on the bulkhead by the hatch, six names in square capitals, and Kaelin's wasn't one of them.

He set his plate on the shelf and took the chalk.

"We've got it, boss," Bao said, with his mouth full. "You're on nights."

Nobody had asked him. Nobody had needed to. Kaelin looked at the six names for a while, and put the chalk back on its ledge, and picked up his plate. On the ops repeater in the corner the evening's line sat in green: 21:00 TEST PULSE. FIELD OPS: COMMAND.

Ines was in the good chair with the marker on its string in her hand.

Petra's column ran down the slate between Selma's and his own, in her slanting hand, every bet a forecast and an accusation. Rain by six. Funmi's cheating. Snow on the Walk. Funmi's cheating. Sun, and everybody's cheating. The last was from April. The column stopped there.

Ines got up out of the good chair, which nobody had ever seen her do on Earth night, and wrote under it.

Thunder by Sunday. Breaks hard. Somebody's cheating.

She stood back. The galley looked at it.

"She'd want it underlined," Selma said.

Ines underlined it, once. "Twice is Funmi's," she said, and sat down.

Nobody laughed. Bao went round with the pan, and Dae-jung held out his cup for noodles he would not eat, and they were put in it gravely, and that was nearly a laugh.

Aur'k came in from the passage and stood behind the crate. There was dust on his plating up to the knee.

"I'll be at the boundary at twenty-one hundred," he said.

"For the pulse."

"For the boundary."

Kaelin looked at him. "Will it wake?"

The stillness. Kaelin had been watching it for six years, and he watched Aur'k build the answer to stay true.

"I don't know," Aur'k said. "Tonight is small. I'll be there."

"Hold it, then."

"I will."

He didn't offer to sit with Petra. He didn't say she would be all right. Kaelin nodded to him, once.

He took his plate and went back down.

· · ·

They turned her at twenty to nine.

Ines took the shoulders and Kaelin took the hips, and on three they rolled her toward the wall, slow, so the feed line didn't pull. Ines wedged the roll of blanket at her back. Kaelin checked her heels, one and then the other, for the red that meant a sore coming, and found none, and told Ines, and Ines wrote it on the overlay.

Two hours. Then the other side. Then the back. All night. Every night until next June.

Ines hung the bag. Kaelin wiped Petra's mouth with the corner of the sheet.

Her eyes were half open. He closed them with two fingers. They stayed closed.

"Ines put you down for thunder," he told her. "Sunday. You'd have hated it. You'd have said she was cheating."

Selma and Bao were in the second and third bunks under their caps, for the night, because the chief had said out of the loop and Ines had said in medical, and Ines outranked everybody in here. Bao was asleep already, on his back with his mouth open. Selma lay with her eyes on the ceiling and her hands folded on her chest like a woman waiting for a lift.

"Go to bed," Ines said.

Kaelin went to stores instead. He came back with a folding cot, one of the cots the forty had slept on in March, with the Alexandria's stencil still on its frame. He opened it between Petra's bunk and the wall, where he could reach her without standing.

Ines watched him do it.

"You're going to sleep down here," she said.

"I'm going to sleep where the work is."

"Bao's got your brush rota in the morning."

"I saw."

"They'll manage."

"I know they will." He sat on the cot. It gave under him, and held. "That's the trouble."

Ines didn't answer that. She went to the good chair, which she had carried down from the galley again, and sat in it with one hand flat on Petra's shin.

The speaker clicked. The duty officer, clipped and flat.

"Test pulse. Earth end coupled. Mars end coupled. Boundary lead at the boundary. Field operations, command. Sixty seconds."

Kaelin counted. He couldn't stop himself, and he didn't try. Above the bunks the overlay held three traces: Selma's low and restless, Bao's low and even, and Petra's flat, as it had been flat since the morning, since a lighthouse went over the edge of the bunk.

Twenty. Nineteen.

Selma turned her head on the pillow and looked at him.

Ten.

Ines's hand stayed where it was.

Three. Two.

Nothing in the room moved. Nothing made a sound. It never did.

On the overlay Bao's trace stood up, high and narrow, and Selma's beside it in the same instant, and Petra's, flat since the morning, stood up with them.
