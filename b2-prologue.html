The Narrow Gate · Prologue

The dyke

The Narrow Gate · Prologue
27 January 2096a dyke-keeper in XinweiXinweiMars delay 14.9 min

THE CONTINUITY — XINWEI, THE PEARL DELTA / Leung Wai-fun — Mars 14.9 min

At twenty to five the sea was already higher than the fields, and still coming.

Leung Wai-fun stood on the old sea bank with her lamp off and her boots in the wet grass, and listened to it. A king tide made no noise on the way up. It only leaned. She could feel it through her soles, the delta pressing on the earth her grandmother had carried here in baskets.

Hoi was asleep on her coat at the top of the bank, curled like a prawn, in the red new-year jacket he had refused to take off for three days. His mother was on nights at the hospital in Guangzhou till the fifth, and he would not stay in the house alone, so he was on the dyke.

She crouched and put the lamp on the inner toe of the bank.

There, in the strip of mud between the bank and the first of the fish ponds, a little ring of sand had pushed up through the silt, like a crab's breathing hole. Then another, a metre on. Water came out of them, clear, quick and cold.

Two years ago, on a lower tide than this, the same boils had come in the same place, and the whole town had stood here with sandbags till the water turned. The bags had gone into the autumn breach. It was the third morning of the new year, and the town was asleep.

She took the handheld out of her pocket. Her own table was on it, the one she worked out each month with a pencil and her grandmother's book and the Moon, and sent along the masts on the dyke to every roof in Xinwei and the free towns upriver. The new moon had gone over two nights ago. Tonight's peak, in her own small figures: 05:52, two hundred and sixty-one on the old stone.

The old stone stood in the water at the gate, with her grandmother's highest mark cut into it in 2031, a metre below where the sea came now on an ordinary spring. Tonight it read two hundred and fifty-four.

Behind her, on the land side, the outer ponds lay black and still in their squares, sixty of them, between the old sea bank and the new inner bank. Every one was full of fish two weeks from market. Past the inner bank, the rice. Past the rice, the houses on their platforms, still dark.

"Wai-fun."

Old Chan came along the bank at a trot, in his singlet in the cold, his torch bobbing. He had the outer ponds. He had had them since his father had them. He saw where her lamp was pointing and stopped.

"It's boiling."

"It's boiling," she agreed.

"How much more tide?"

"Seven centimetres. An hour and a bit." She showed him the handheld. "Two sixty-one."

He looked at her figures, and then upriver into the dark. "The co-op's table says two fifty-five."

"The co-op's table is two days old. Their roof's been down since Wednesday."

"So wait for it. They'll have it back by six."

"At six it's done, one way or the other." She turned the lamp on the second boil. It had widened. The water coming out of it had grey in it now. Silt. The bank was giving away its insides. "If the sea bank goes, it goes here, all at once. The sea comes through your ponds, over the inner bank and onto the rice, and nobody plants those fields for three years."

"And if you open the gate, my ponds go first."

"Yes."

He knew. That was why he had run.

"The sea's pushing on this bank from one side and nothing's pushing back," she said. "Let it into your ponds, and the water stands on both sides of it. The bank stops being pushed. I fill them to the old mark and shut it, and the inner bank holds the rest."

"And my fish die in the salt."

"And your fish die in the salt."

"There's no forecast says it'll break."

"There's no forecast says anything. There never was." She looked at him. "I'm the forecast. You voted for me."

"I voted for you to keep the dyke."

"I'm keeping it."

He stood there with the torch shaking a little in his hand. Behind them on the coat, Hoi turned over and said something about dumplings in his sleep.

Chan switched the torch off.

"Drown it," he said, low, to the ponds. "Go on, then."

· · ·

The outer gate was a steel leaf in a concrete throat at the corner of the bank, older than she was, and the wheel took both of them.

It turned a quarter, stuck, turned. The sea came in under the leaf with a hard gulp and then a roar, brown and cold, and smelled at once of salt and weed and the bottom of the estuary. It ran out across the first pond in a long dark sheet, and into the next, and the next, filling the squares and the channels between them.

She didn't go back to the boils. She stood at the wheel and watched the water climb the pole in the first pond, a hand, two hands, and counted under her breath with her eyes on it, because if she looked at the bank she would want to look at it every second.

At the old mark she said, "Shut," and they put their weight on the wheel together, and the roar went down to a gulp, and to nothing.

Then she went back along the bank with the lamp.

The first boil had stopped. The second was still running, slower, the water clear again. She crouched beside it and counted to a hundred. It slowed. She counted to a hundred again. It stopped.

At 05:52 the old stone said two hundred and sixty. She had been one high. She wrote it down.

In the first pond, the fish were coming up to the top, mouths open, silver in her lamp.

Chan didn't watch them. He sat down on the bank with his back to the ponds and his hands between his knees.

"I'll pay you out of the van," she said. "The nodes."

"I don't want nodes."

"You'll want them in the typhoons."

He didn't answer. After a while he said, "Two sixty," and laughed once, sourly, and wiped his face with the bottom of his singlet.

· · ·

At half past six Hoi woke up and wanted to know why the ponds were the sea.

"For a bit," she said.

"Are the fish dead?"

"Some of them." She swung him up onto her shoulders, which she had told herself last month she was too old to do, and did anyway. He was six and heavy, and his hands went straight into her hair. "Uncle Chan's fish saved the rice. Go and wish him a happy new year, and mean it."

The path along the dyke was pink with last night's firecracker paper, and the pump house still had its red couplets pasted either side of the door.

Along the dyke road, from upriver, the van was coming: one headlamp, solar on its roof, the cooperative's circle painted on the door. It was late, which meant it was on time.

It stopped by the pump house. The driver got down and opened the back, and the town came out to meet it in slippers. Boxes of nodes, a coil of mast cable, a roll of solar film, everything wrapped in newspaper printed in Portuguese, from the cooperative on the other side of the world whose nodes these were.

They paid in fish. Today that meant the dead ones from the first pond, still good if you were quick, in tubs of ice from the co-op freezer, and half a sack of dried shrimp to make up. The driver took a fish for himself and put the nodes in Chan's arms, all of them, and Chan stood holding them like a grandchild he hadn't asked for.

The sky was going pale in the east. There was no wind. The winter rain was a month late and the hills were brown with it, and the sea lay grey and flat over the outer ponds as if it had always been there.

"Ah ma," said Hoi. "Look."

He was pointing west, over the old factory stacks on the waterline, where it was still night.

In the west, two hands' width above the hills, a line of small lights had come out. Brighter than stars, and in a row, a thread of them across that part of the sky, every one lit white and gold by a sun that hadn't reached Xinwei yet.

"That's the ring," she said.

"What's the ring?"

"Where the power comes from. For the cities." She put her hands round his ankles. "Count them."

He counted. He got to eleven and lost his place, and started again, and got to sixteen. Below them the van's engine started, and the driver waved and went back up the dyke road toward the next town.

"Sixteen," said Hoi. "Uncle Chan says bits fall off them."

"Bits," she said. "Little bits. They burn up."

"Will they fall down?"

Wai-fun looked up at them, the thin bright thread over the brown hills, so far up that no tide in the world would ever reach them.

"No," she said. "Those are the safest thing in the sky."
