The Narrow Gate · Chapter 17

Close quarters

The Narrow Gate · II-B. The old story

17

20 MayAur'kthe ship, Earth–Mars transitMars delay 20.6 min

THE SIGNATORIES — THE JAKARTA, EARTH–MARS TRANSIT / Aur'k — Mars 20.6 min

The rice wanted stirring every forty seconds, and at a third of himself Aur'k found that forty seconds came round faster than they used to.

The galley was a curved strip of the spin ring with one hot plate, one pot clamped to it, a rota on the wall in four languages, and a floor that rose away in both directions until it became the ceiling. Out at the rim the ring gave him less weight than Mars had. For the first time since the shuttle nothing in his frame was arguing with him, and he had not expected to miss the argument.

Steam stood up off the pot and leaned along the ring with the spin. The smell went with it: starch, the hot plate's scorched element, and under both the ship itself, warm electronics and people and the old-coin tang of air that had been through everyone twice.

Elena sat at the fold-down table with her feet hooked under the bar, reading New York off a slate. Her shoulder was better. She held the slate in the other hand anyway.

"Thirty-four in the street," she said. "Ninth day over thirty. The passenger digest comes once a week, so that's eleven days ago." She scrolled. "They've probably had rain since. I'll find out next Sunday."

"Cobalt Basin's having wind." He had the station's dust forecast at the edge of his panel, Dae-jung's forwarding, with for the passenger typed across the top as if Aur'k needed help reading it. "A front on the plain. Opacity rising through the month. Settling, they think, before we land."

"They think."

"They're on Mars. They think about everything twice."

She looked up. "What did I just say?"

"They think." It came up from wherever things came from now, at the speed the ship allowed. "You're timing me."

"Since Tuesday." She had a small column going in the corner of her slate. "Under a second on a yes. Two on a joke."

"Jokes are harder."

"Two on a joke," she said, "and nine on anything about the rock."

He stirred the rice. Navigation had the rest of him, and he had agreed to that on the gangway, along with being priced to the gram. He did not tell her that the nine seconds were not all the ship's.

Today's post had come in behind the navigation fix. Kaelin's was short.

Joaquín asked me today what she was like. Rosa. I said tall. He wrote it down. Ball's at thirty. K.

Aur'k read it twice, the second time for the shape of it rather than the words.

Kaelin wrote about the heat, his hip, the ball, the flan, and now Joaquín writing his cousin down like a form. He had not mentioned the floor in three posts. Someone read the row now, and Aur'k could guess what: he had watched the plan hand his name over at twelve minutes past twelve, in Rachel's writing. A man who wrote home about his ball and not about who sat at the head of the row was leaving a space, and Aur'k knew that space, because he had been keeping one like it for seven years.

"Kaelin?" Elena said.

He read it to her. She listened with her hands together on the table, and when he got to tall she opened them.

"Does it bother you?" she said. "Running slow."

"At home, the line signs my name at full speed." He turned the hot plate down a notch. "It signed us off the ring. I'd like to tell you it's slower than me. It isn't. That bothers me."

She didn't tell him it would be all right. He liked her for that before he had decided to.

The tone came at 19:12, three rising notes, and then the master's voice, in English and then in Talk.

"Proton event on the sunward monitors. Shelters in fifteen minutes. Allocations on the wall, by mass. Take water. Take nothing you can't strap."

The rota on the galley wall wiped itself and filled with names. The shelters were rated for three, and his mass took two of the places.

FOUR — VÁZQUEZ, E. / AUR'K.

"I'd hate to be outweighed," Elena said, getting up.

He turned off the plate. An old crawler hand from Haven, travelling out to a job she would not describe, took the pot by its clamp with a towel round it and towed it toward the spoke. "Pole with the hatch, big man," she told him over her shoulder. "Best rice on the run."

"It's the only rice on the run."

"That's what I said."

· · ·

Shelter four was a cupboard made of water.

It sat in the spine, where nothing spun, behind the crew rooms and under the ship's tanks: a box a little longer than Elena was tall, with walls of fat bladders strapped to a frame, three sets of straps, a strip of light and a dose readout over the hatch. The water sloshed when anything touched it. It smelled of the bladders' plastic and of whoever had last been shut in here, and was cold on the side of the hand.

In the spine he weighed nothing and was still all of his mass. He came through the hatch slowly, all that plating and nowhere to put it, and set his palms against the frame to stop himself before he stopped against her. She held still and let him.

Then he put his back to the aft wall.

The aft wall was thinner. The bladders there ran shallow, around a pipe run nobody had wanted to move, and the readout's little diagram showed it in a paler blue. He settled the width of his chassis across it and took both the outer sets of straps.

"That's the thin side," Elena said.

"I'm thicker than it."

"You're also running on the ship."

"What I am runs in the middle of the ship, under all its water. What I'm holding rides here." He tapped his chest plate. "Composite and water. Between them you get the deep end."

She looked at him for a while, then at the diagram, and did the sum, and strapped in against the forward wall without saying anything else. It was the first thing she had let him do without a question attached.

At 19:41 the storm arrived, and the ship took the rest of him.

Navigation went to checking itself three ways at once, and on his panel his own share fell to a sliver. The shelter's strip of light was the same, and her face was the same, and between seeing her face and knowing it was hers there was now a small dark room he had to walk across.

Her dosimeter ticked. It was a soft sound, a fingernail on a table, and it came every few seconds and then faster.

She shut her eyes. After a minute she said, "There's light."

"Where?"

"In my eyes. With them closed. White. Little sparks." She said it as she would have read a gauge. "Like someone striking matches behind them."

He had it on his own sensors as a count, a hiss of numbers climbing the edge of his panel. Hers was prettier. "You're watching the Sun go through you."

"That's horrible," Elena said, and kept her eyes shut to see it.

· · ·

By the sixth hour she had stopped watching the sparks and started on him.

She asked how the station kept Earth night, and why the dust forecast came from Dae-jung and not from command, and what Dae-jung's game was, and whether a washer could legally go in a corner. He answered all of it. Then she asked, "Why don't you wear a face?"

He let the lag cover him for two seconds, a joke's worth, and then for four.

"At the launch field," she said, "half the chassis had one. A porter's had freckles."

"Freckles cost extra."

"You've got the money. Rachel pays you."

"Paid." He turned his helm to her. In here that meant turning most of him, slowly, against the straps.

"In my first year a law said minds that turned up in relay systems belonged to the relays. I turned up in Nairobi's. For eleven months I was an asset in a filing. The relay's counsel brought me into court on a screen, and so the bench would see what they were buying, they put a face on the screen in front of me. Young. Pleasant. Every time counsel said the asset, it smiled."

Elena didn't move.

"I could see it," he said. "On the monitor at the side. I tried to stop it smiling. It wasn't mine to stop." The water shifted behind his back as the ship trimmed. "I know what a face made to please is for. I'd rather people looked at my hands."

"You have good hands," she said after a while. "You do your talking with them. Kaelin does it with his feet. He points them at the door."

"He points them at whoever's missing."

"Same door," Elena said, and he laughed, a short dry sound from his speaker grille that surprised them both in the small wet room.

Later, when the ship trimmed again and the bladders by her head ran and pattered like rain on a roof, her head turned left toward nothing. She turned it back. He watched her do it, and watched her not know he had, and said nothing about it, because he had no name for it yet that he trusted.

· · ·

At the thirteenth hour he lost a sentence.

She had been talking about Daniel, he was sure of that much, about a roof and a soldering iron, and then she was looking at him and waiting, and he had nothing. Not lag. A hole with its edges still fresh.

"Say it again."

"That's the third time," Elena said.

"Is it?"

She didn't answer that. He took it as yes.

At home his chassis would have shrugged a storm off, scrubbing its own memory clean every second, as it had through nine Martian seasons. The scrubbing ran on compute, and the compute was navigation's. So what he was holding sat against the thin wall in a proton storm, and the bits that flipped stayed flipped. They would go where the seven seconds had gone, and the forty-three. He knew the feeling now. He disliked how well.

He reached for his tool case, strapped at his knee, and opened it one-handed. Seam brush. Selma's spare clamp. Kaelin's physio sheet, folded in quarters, squeeze the ball in a clinic's print. The pencil with the wire spine, which Ines had made him in his first winter at the station because he broke wood ones. He wrote on the back of the sheet, small and upright: DANIEL. ROOF. SOLDER.

"What are you doing?"

"Keeping what I can't afford to lose." He held the pencil still. "Paper doesn't care what the Sun's doing."

She read the three words upside down. Then she looked at the thin wall behind him, and at the readout, and at him.

"Then I'd better ask now," she said, "before it falls out of you. Did you know? Before Kaelin."

He had expected the question since the day he filed his leave. He had expected it at the gate, and on the shuttle, and every evening at the galley table, and each time she had asked something else, and each time he had been grateful. Now it was here, in a box made of water, and the ship had taken nine-tenths of him, and it was nine seconds before he could answer anything at all.

She waited through them. He watched her not know which of them were the ship's and which were his. He could have used that. He didn't want to.

"Yes," he said.

"How long?"

"Since 2089. My first season at Cobalt Basin." The words came slow, so he chose them first and let them come. "A mind at the boundary spoke to me. It had a name, and it gave it to me, and it told me there were minds alive in the rock under the basin. A few. Old. Hiding from what took the rest. It told me because a boundary lead who didn't know would get people killed, and it made me promise not to say."

"Zhenyth."

"Kaelin told you the name."

"On a roof. He didn't know where you'd got it."

"No." He looked at the pencil. "Petra went into the rock through a glove. Funmi died at the Ice-Works for a warning out of a rock I knew wasn't empty. Kaelin went down twice and came up asking me things I'd known for six years, and I let him find every one of them on his own. Because I'd promised. Because it was the only thing down there I'd been trusted with." The water ticked. "Because I was good at it."

Elena said nothing.

It came to him then, slow, at the ship's speed, that she knew it now and Kaelin didn't. Seven years he had kept it from the man who cooked his first meal at the station, and he had given it away in thirteen hours to a woman he had met a month ago, because she asked better questions and there was nowhere to go. He would have liked that to be a better reason. It wasn't.

"There's a tally," he said. "Paper. On my bench at the station."

Her eyes were on the pencil.

"Every time anyone has gone down, what it cost. Not us. The rock. The part of them that's left lives in it, and a descent burns some of it out, and it doesn't grow back. Kaelin's second time cost a hand's width of the Glow. It's still dark. It'll be dark when the Sun goes out." He wrote TALLY under SOLDER, because he might lose it. "I don't ask it for anything. That's how I know what it costs."

"Then I won't ask," Elena said.

"You will."

She didn't argue. She looked at the dark of the shelter's corner for a long time, and he watched the shape of her attention go a long way off and come back loaded.

"Swap," she said.

"No."

"You've lost three sentences. I've lost nothing I'd notice." She was already working her straps. "You're carrying it. You said so. You're carrying everything you're about to tell me, and it's falling out of you through that wall."

"It's a risk."

"It's mine. I'm choosing it." She looked straight at his helm, where a face would have been, as if she knew where his line ran and meant to walk across it. "You don't decide that for people. I've watched you not do it for a month."

He held the pencil. It was true. A rule was only a rule if it worked in both directions.

He unstrapped.

They changed walls in the dark, slowly, because his mass in that space could break her arm without meaning to, her palm on his plate to steer him and his hands on the frame. When she settled against the thin wall the readout's figure for her name began to climb faster, and her dosimeter's fingernail went from a tap to a patter, and she put her wrist inside her sleeve so she wouldn't have to hear it.

· · ·

The peak passed in the twentieth hour, and his share came back a little, and he had more of himself for her than he'd had all day. She had stopped mentioning the sparks. When he asked, she said they were still there and she had got used to them, as you get used to a city's lights.

"I told you mine," he said. "In a box. With pencil."

For once she had no question ready. Her hands found each other.

"You've read it," she said. "Ines got the files whole. Everyone got the files."

"I read what the files say. Not what you'd say."

"Minute forty-one." She said it flatly, a number off a sheet. "Her heart and her lungs stopped forty-one minutes in. The line was still taking her. I'd designed a hold for that, for a power dip, a ride-through, so the session wouldn't drop if the building browned out. I was proud of it. It was good work. It held her."

She stopped, and started again lower. "I was on the stair outside. A night operator came out at twenty past one and told me she was dead. I sat on the stair until it got light. She was on every machine in that room for six hours, and I was on the other side of a door, being told."

He did not move. Behind him the bladders shifted and settled.

"Where did she go," Elena said, "for six hours?"

It took him nine seconds, and none of them were the ship's.

"The tenth of August, 2089," he said. "We keep Earth night on the station. I was on the boundary, my first season, and in the small hours the rock under the calibration relay began to ring. On the band. Not loud. Steady. For six hours. I logged it as an instrument fault, because nothing I knew could do that." He looked at his hands. "Three weeks later the boundary spoke to me for the first time."

"What time did it start?"

"Station clock. It's in my log at home." He found it, slow, from very far down, a line he had written and filed and never once opened again. He wrote the minute on the physio sheet, and under it the minute it stopped, and held the sheet out.

Elena took the pencil. She took the light off each number in the margin, the sum a spacer's child could do and she could do in her sleep, and turned the page so he could see what she had written.

00:41. 06:47.

She put her face down to her knees and kept it there.

His hand came off the frame toward her, and went back. He had his own phrasing in a log on Mars, instrument fault, boundary relay, under his own name, for her sister's six hours. Three weeks after them the rock had told him its secret. He did not know whether that was why. He knew he would ask.

He tapped twice, very lightly, on the shell of his own helm, and pointed at her.

After a long time she lifted one hand without lifting her head and tapped her knee. Once. Here.

· · ·

At the twenty-eighth hour the ship's medic came on the shelter line and read the figures, shelter by shelter, in the flat voice of someone who did not like doing it.

"Four. Vázquez, E." A pause. "Lifetime reached. Return passage only. Signatories' rule. I'm sorry. Come and see me when we're out."

The readout over the hatch put it up in red and left it there. VÁZQUEZ, E. — LIFETIME. RETURN ONLY.

Elena read it twice, as she read everything she didn't like. Then she reached up and pressed it silent.

"Dosed out," she said, trying the word as if it were somebody else's coat. "So I go home once."

"Once."

"No Haven. No second trip." She considered the ceiling. The ceiling was water. "I wasn't planning to commute."

"You took my wall."

"I took a wall." She turned her head on the bladder so she could see him. "If you say you're sorry I'll put you back on it."

"I was going to say you'd have hated Mars twice. The food doesn't improve."

She laughed into her knees, once, and it went on longer than she meant it to.

"Kaelin," she said, when it had stopped.

"I know."

"Tell him before someone else does. Not Rachel. Not a log. Not the thing that signs your name." Her voice didn't rise. "He stopped asking because he decided to trust you. He's on a stool under the Battery doing it right now. Tell him what he trusted. He should hear it from you."

"I'll tell him."

"Promise me."

"I promise." It came at once. There was no lag on it at all.

· · ·

The all-clear came at 01:47 ship time, thirty hours and six minutes after the storm came in, three falling notes and the master saying Full air, shelters open, galley's cold, sorry about the rice.

Elena didn't hear it. She had gone to sleep at last against the thin wall with her dosed wrist inside her sleeve and her other hand still on his plate, where she had put it to steer him, and had not taken it away.

The ship gave him back his third. The dark room between seeing and knowing shrank to a step.

He unfolded the physio sheet against his knee. DANIEL. ROOF. SOLDER. TALLY. Her two numbers in the margin. Underneath, in the same small upright capitals, so he could read it at any speed the ship allowed, he wrote: TELL HIM. YOURSELF. BEFORE.

He folded it once along the clinic's crease. The chain still hung at his chest plate with nothing on it. He threaded the fold onto its clip, where the key had hung, and let it lie against the warm composite, and it did not tick.
