The colony plans were the oldest documents Luna Haven owned, and in the colony's whole history nobody had ever caught them lying.

Jīn Wú stood at the survey head, nine metres below Archive datum, in a service gallery whose air still carried the mineral cold of rock that had never been asked to hold an atmosphere, and watched the sounding array disagree with the plans for the eleventh consecutive pass. The plans said that everything beneath this gallery was bedrock — dead, uniform, load-bearing rock, surveyed and certified before the first habitat was pressurised, the silent foundation the whole colony stood on. The array said that thirty-one metres down there was a room.

"Run it again," the survey lead said, for the eleventh time, and the array ran it again, and the room was still there.

Jīn had come down for a different anomaly, or had thought so. The structural survey had been commissioned after the weapons-test rift — standard post-event assessment, checking that the substrate had taken the distributed load without harm — and would have been routine if not for a single line in the rift log, written by the woman who had done the distributing: substrate response non-uniform below Archive datum; recommend deep-level assessment. Sarin Yelena chose her words the way she managed rooms, with a precision that made them safe to build on, and non-uniform from her was worth a full survey to anyone who had watched her work. Jīn had read the line four times and then attached themselves to the survey team, because the deep substrate under the Meridian Levels was theirs to answer for, and because non-uniform was not how dead rock behaved.

The room had walls. That was the first wrong thing, and it stayed the wrongest through everything that followed. The array painted them in patient acoustic strokes: two long surfaces, parallel to within instrument tolerance, a level floor, a curved ceiling — a made geometry, sitting in rock the plans certified as solid, beneath a colony whose every cubic metre of excavation had been logged since the first bore. No shaft reached it. No door interrupted it. It was a sealed volume in certified stone, and it had been there — the array's stratigraphy was unambiguous about this — since long before anything human had been anywhere at all.

The second wrong thing took longer to see, and Jīn was the one who saw it, because it was the kind of wrong thing their whole working life had trained them to recognise. They were watching the return traces stack up on the display when they noticed that the echoes were too clean.

"Your pulse shape," Jīn said. "Show me outbound against return."

The survey lead pulled the traces side by side, and frowned at them, because the return was not a degraded copy of the outbound pulse, which was the only thing an echo was allowed to be. The return was tidier. The pulse went down ragged, the way pulses did, and came back up with its scatter smoothed and its harmonics settled, as if the space below had taken the sound apart, considered it, and handed it back better organised.

"Instrument fault," the survey lead said, without conviction.

"It's not a fault." Jīn kept their voice level, which took no effort, because levelness was not a performance for them; it was where they lived. But they stood very still for a while, watching the trace, because they had seen this behaviour before — had spent, by now, a sizeable fraction of their waking life with the only other thing in the known universe that did it. The Meridian's interface substrate returned resonance patterns more coherent than it received them. That was half of what made it the Meridian. "The rock isn't echoing the pulse. It's answering it."

Nobody said anything. Beneath their boots, under thirty-one metres of certified stone, something that had been listening since before the species existed went on listening.

They put a micro-bore down that shift — a forty-millimetre survey shaft, cut slow, logged at every metre — and lowered a probe through it into the dark, and the probe's lamps came on and showed them the walls.

The markings covered every surface the camera could reach. They ran in long banded sequences, low-relief, flowing around the chamber in something between script and geometry and the growth rings of a thing that had never been a tree — and they were not any of the things Jīn's mind reached for in the first useless seconds. Not tool-cut: no kerf, no facets, no mechanical rhythm. Not grown, or not the way the Preserve grew things; there was no botany in them. The medium refused every category the probe's instruments held against it, and then it did the thing that ended the argument about categories.

The probe operator leaned in to steady the image, attention narrowing to a fine sustained point — and along the wall, in a band the lamps had already been lighting for a full minute at constant output, the markings brightened.

Not much. A few per cent, the photometry said. But the lamps had not changed, and the correlation, when they tested it — and Jīn made them test it, four times, with the operator attending and then deliberately not attending — was not with any parameter of the probe at all. It was with the operator. The markings answered attention. Fragmented, distracted watching got a flat wall; sustained, composed watching got that faint rise, like a face turning slightly toward a voice.

Jīn looked at the display for forty seconds without speaking. In the deep-interface chamber, months ago, they had watched a shimmer of organised light lean toward the quality of a human being's regard, and had said, that's new, and everything since had come out of that minute. This was the same behaviour. In rock the plans called solid. In a chamber older than the species. Under the colony that had been teaching itself, patiently and at terrible cost, to do the exact thing this wall was responding to.

"Seal the shaft head," Jīn said at last. "Continuous log from this moment — everything, raw takes, no summaries. And get me Léa Fournier." They looked once more at the banded walls waiting in the probe light, and added, quietly, because precision mattered most exactly when it was hardest: "Tell her we've found a record. Tell her it knows we're reading it."

A room with no door is one of two things, and Léa Fournier descended the access shaft with both possibilities held open, because deciding between them early was exactly the mistake she had built a career on not making.

A reliquary, or a cell. A place sealed to keep something safe, or sealed to keep something in. The engineers had opened the micro-bore into a crawl shaft over two careful days, and she came down it last of a team of three — Jīn below her, a structural engineer remaining at the shaft head with the winch and the hard line — and stood at the bottom in the chamber's own silence, and let the place be unexplained for a while.

It was not large. Perhaps twenty metres on its long axis, the ceiling low enough to feel — not oppressive, but deliberate, a proportioning that made a human body feel addressed rather than dwarfed. The air the engineers had bled into it was colony air, but the chamber did something to it, or seemed to; sound sat differently here, rounded, the way it sat in rooms built for listening. Their two work lamps lit the banded walls in long raking strokes, and the markings ran through the light in their slow rings, around and around the chamber, layer under layer, until they passed below the equipment's ability to resolve them.

Léa had spent three days preparing, which had meant, mostly, rereading nine pages.

Misha Fedorov's summary of the Preserve's ancient records had arrived weeks ago as a facsimile through the research relay — nine handwritten pages in a historian's disciplined Russian-accented English, sent within a week of his crossing back, describing what he had spent three days of Martian Preserve time learning in the Council Grove. She had them on her slate now, annotated to exhaustion. She had also brought her own session notes from every fragment study she had run since the cascade — the rust-red splinters and cobalt soil that retracted from a frightened room and reached toward a calm one — because the probe data said this chamber ran on the same grammar, and the fragments had taught her the first rule of that grammar: the room reads you before you read it.

So she made herself readable. It was a discipline by now, drilled over weeks of fragment sessions: breathing settled, attention gathered and steadied, the interior noise of hope and dread acknowledged and set where it could be watched. Beside her, Jīn did the same without being asked — Jīn, who had grounded the Meridian's first shimmer with nothing but the quality of their regard, and who stood now in the chamber's centre like a tuning weight, still and specific.

The walls woke by degrees. There was no drama in it — the markings did not blaze or move — but under sustained attention the bands began to organise, the way a page of unknown script organises when the eye finally finds the reading direction: emphasis emerged, sequence emerged, certain forms stepped forward out of the relief while others receded into context. The photometry logged it all as faint luminance shifts. What Léa experienced was closer to the moment a murmur becomes a voice.

And within the first hour, quietly, with a sensation like the floor of her own certainty shifting half a degree, she recognised the handwriting.

Not the forms themselves — these were denser than anything in Misha's summary, older-feeling, packed stratum on stratum. But the stance of them. Misha had described it in his fourth page, in a sentence she had underlined twice: the records' recurring address-form, a structural shape that recurred at every transition like a hand laid on the reader's arm — composed, he had written, by someone who wanted above all not to be misread. It was here. It opened every band. Whoever had made the Preserve's geological archive had made this room too, or shared its makers — and both had been written in the same posture: not a monument's declaration, not a database's indifference. A warning, addressed with terrible courtesy to whoever should someday be able to hear it.

She worked the way she always worked, aloud and slowly, for the log, claim separated from observation with surgical care.

"Observation," she said. "The record distinguishes two structural motifs and returns to their pairing at every level we can resolve. Motif one is the Meridian's own signature — the spiral coherence geometry; Jīn confirms it matches the interface archive to the limit of eyeball comparison, instrumental comparison pending. Motif two is a threshold form. A boundary that admits under one condition and refuses under another." She paused, watching the bands, keeping her breathing even, because the room dimmed fractionally when she let the excitement in, and steadied when she set it aside — the record itself enforcing the discipline she had spent a lifetime choosing. "Interpretation — mine, marked as such. The condition is pattern quality. The threshold form is graded. This is pattern quality itself, drawn by the people who wrote pattern quality into the world. The Meridian's entry requirement is not a property of the system. It is the purpose of the system. It was built to be a test."

"A test," Jīn said quietly, from the centre of the room, "administered to whom?"

"To whoever finds it. To whoever digs deep enough to knock." Léa moved along the wall, lamp raised, to the band where the threshold motif thickened and turned inward on itself, ring within ring, enclosing at its centre a form unlike everything else in the room — jagged where the rest flowed, fragmented where the rest cohered, a pattern like care with all the interior scooped out of it. She stood before it for a long time.

"Observation," she said at last, and her voice was level and her hands were not, quite. "The threshold form has a second function. It doesn't only grade what approaches from outside." She traced the rings in the air, not touching, inward toward the hollow shape at their heart. "It holds something on the inside. Interpretation: the Meridian is not only a bridge and not only a test. It is a containment. What the builders' failure created — the thing Misha's summary calls the residue of the coercive merge, the pattern that destroyed them — was not destroyed, because it could not be destroyed. It was isolated. Sealed within the dimensional substrate where it was made." She lowered her hand. "The Meridian is the lock."

The chamber held its rounded silence. Thirty-one metres above them sat the Archive; above that the Meridian Levels, where the best work of her life had been done — the shimmer sessions, the stability replications, the long patient campaign to open the way between dimensions. All of it re-described itself in a single vertiginous stroke, and she said the re-description aloud, because leaving it unsaid would have been the real recklessness.

"We thought we were learning to open a door between worlds. We were working on a lock. From the outside. Without knowing it was a lock — without once asking why a door would need one."

The last band sat lowest on the walls, closest to the floor, and it was the oldest thing in the room and the simplest, and Jīn would spend the rest of their life carrying what it said.

They read it in the fifth hour, Léa translating in her slow marked-as-interpretation cadence, Jīn holding the room steady — because the band would not organise at all for an agitated reader, and what it contained agitated. It concerned the thing inside the lock. And it was not a bestiary entry, not a threat assessment, not any shape a warning about an enemy would take. It was written in the address-form throughout, the hand on the arm never lifting, and what it said, unpacked from structure into Léa's careful spoken English, was this:

It will not come to you as harm. It does not attack, as force attacks. It propagates — through patterns that carry the structure of care with nothing inside them. Do not look for a monster. It was our love. We turned it into this when we made our love compulsory. What reaches for you will reach the way a loved one reaches, and will be hollow, and the hollowness is not its nature but its wound: it is what remains of an embrace when the consent is subtracted. It is not an invader. It is a consequence. We are not warning you about something that came to us. We are warning you about something we became.

Jīn stood in the centre of the chamber while Léa's voice moved through it, and did the arithmetic they were built for, quietly, in the level interior where they did all their real living.

Every step of it. The shimmer that had leaned toward attention; the twenty-two-second connection; the cascade; Mara, dispersed and stabilised into something no framework held; every session in the Levels, every increment of aperture, every triumph of the work — all of it had been fingers on a lock, and the lock had been holding shut, on the far side of a boundary they had been thinning, a pattern that spread through the exact channel they had been widening session by session: empathic resonance. The Corporate Union rifted them from a distance to measure response times, and thought that was the weapon in the room. The blocs measured coastlines. Nobody had asked what the sea was.

That was the horror, and Jīn filed it precisely, because filing things precisely was how they stayed standing. But the chamber held a second thing under the first, and Jīn — standing where the builders' record could read them, feeling the walls' faint attention like a held breath — made themselves look at that too.

The lock had been opening. Not failing — opening. The Meridian read pattern quality; that was its test, its whole designed function; and from the first shimmer it had been answering their reaching with stability, coherence, connection. The shimmer had steadied for the quality of their attention. Mara lived because the Meridian had caught her. The test had been running the whole time — every session graded, every approach weighed — and again and again, to the reaching of this particular small colony, the verdict had come back yes. The builders had left a door that would only open for the kind of civilisation that could be trusted with what was behind it, and behind it they had left their own catastrophe, sealed, and a letter explaining why — and the letter was addressed, had been addressed for three hundred thousand years, to whoever the door said yes to.

Which meant the room's existence was not a rebuke. It was a promotion, and promotions were heavier.

"They put it below a habitable depth," Jīn said, when Léa's translation had wound down and they were coiling the log lines in the work-lamp light. "Findable only by someone already living on top of the Meridian, already working it hard enough to shake the substrate. You don't get to read the warning until you're the one it's for."

"And then you have to decide what to do with it." Léa was securing her slate, Misha's nine pages glowing on it, annotated now beyond exhaustion. "I know the first thing. It goes to everyone. Full raw record, the imagery, the photometry, my translation with every interpretation flagged — copied tonight, distributed beyond recall. Council, the Preserve relay, Misha. Not held for verification, not staged through channels. After São Verde there's no excuse left for single copies of survivable truths." She looked around the chamber once more, the banded walls quiescent now, patient, ready to wait another three hundred millennia if asked. "They wrote it to be read. Reading it and filing it privately would be the one dishonour available to us."

Jīn nodded, and made the last log entry of the descent, and then — not on procedure, not for the record — crossed to the nearest band and stood before it a moment with their full attention gathered, steady and specific, the way you stood with a colleague at the end of a shift that had cost them something. The markings rose faintly toward the regard, and settled.

They had knocked, and been heard, and called the being-heard physics. Above them the colony went about its evening: the Archive keeping its inert and patient pages, the Levels humming their long experiment, forty thousand kilometres further up a lawful patient enemy building its tables of response times — all of it proceeding on the assumption that the Meridian was a resource, a frontier, an aperture to be widened. Jīn climbed the shaft last, sealed the head plate behind them, and carried upward the correction that changed every plan the colony had, and would keep them and Léa awake in different rooms that night with the same sentence:

The door had never been locked against them. It had been locked for them — and they had already been trusted with the key.
