Fractured Horizons · Chapter 06

Who are you?

Fractured Horizons · I. The voice

06

17 MarchElenaNew York, the SpireMars delay 7.9 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE SPIRE / Elena Vázquez — Mars 7.9 min

The girl under the table wanted to know why the yellow boxes were in order.

"Amber," Elena said. "They're ranked."

"By what?"

Elena looked up at the wall, where Foresight had mirrored its boards for the review: a cooling grid in Singapore above a desalination plant on the Gulf Crescent, above a school in the Shallows whose cooling had died at six that morning with nobody inside it.

"By how much people would mind," she said.

The girl thought about that. Then she drew a box on her handheld and coloured it in, hard, all the way to the edges.

Two chairs down, her mother from the orbital desk mouthed sorry at Elena for the third time. The city had shut its schools at seven, when the morning's high came in four degrees over the forecast. Two others on the review had brought their own children. Nobody minded.

The Spire's cooling was louder than the presenter. It came up through the floor in a long drone that climbed whenever the sun cleared the next tower, and the man at the front of the room, a young engineer from the Lantern team with his sleeves rolled, kept stopping to let it pass. Beyond the glass the sky was white from edge to edge. The towers of the Polder stood in it with no shadows at all.

Rachel's chair was empty. Her name card sat in front of it, and at seven she had sent one line: Pumps. Heat. Read it for me.

So Elena read it for her. She read ahead, as she always had, while the engineer walked the room through the heads and the bores and the three cores they had pulled at Cobalt Basin, on schedule.

The calibration protocol was on page forty. It was short and very good. Every sweep would couple the Lantern's heads live to the boundary rock above the Preserve, and tune the relay against whatever the rock sent back. Live. The rock as the reference. The protocol said it in one clean sentence that somebody had been proud of.

She had drawn that diagram.

Not this one. One like it, on a wall two floors down, in marker that kept running out, for the Spire line. It too had been calibrated live against Cobalt Basin's rock. She had been very proud of that sentence too.

Elena clasped her hands on the table and waited for the cooling to drop.

"Page forty," she said, when it did. "Has the live coupling been run before? At this scale, against this rock."

The engineer found the page. "Not at this scale."

"At any scale."

He glanced, very briefly, at the ceiling, toward the floors where the director's offices were. "There's a Charter precedent. It's sealed. I haven't seen it, and the protocol doesn't depend on it."

"Then it won't mind being read."

Somebody laughed. The orbital engineer did not. She was looking at Elena with her head on one side, as her daughter had looked at the boxes.

"I'll put it in the questions," the young man said, and wrote it down, and Elena watched him write it in a list that already had eleven things on it and knew it would stay there.

She kept reading.

In the annex, under Objections, there was one. Boundary lead, Cobalt Basin. Formal objection to boring at the Lantern site, bore three, on substrate response. Trace attached. Under it sat the Compact's reply. The schedule should stand, it said, having reviewed the trace with Foresight's engineers.

She was Foresight's engineer on this review. She had never seen the trace. Where it should have been, the annex said Trace: held at site.

She marked it. She went on to the register of packets, because that was what she had come for.

Rachel had made it a condition. Every packet raw, and the review had them: the Jamaica Bay warning in full, its frames, its residue, its flood map with the blank where the Rockaways should have been. Above it sat the first line in the register.

Packet one, 12 January. Summary attached. Raw: under director's seal.

Every packet raw. Bellamy had agreed to it in front of the council, and here was the first one, under his name, and Rachel had not been in the room to see it.

Elena looked at that line for a long time. She did not mark it. It was the only seal in the building that was keeping her safe.

"Precedent search," she said under her breath, and her lace carried it to the review's archive. "Charter programmes. Live calibration against Cobalt Basin substrate."

One result, said the archive. Lattice programme, the Spire line. Closed 2089. Sealed.

She tried again on her Foresight credentials. They reached further, and the answer came back longer.

Sealed 2089 by M. Bellamy, personal authority. Not a Charter seal. Requests to view are notified to the sealing authority.

Notified. She had already asked.

At the front of the room the cooling climbed again, and the engineer stopped, and smiled, and waited for it.

· · ·

Bellamy came down at ten past one.

The review had broken for lunch. The others had gone out to the canteen, all except the orbital engineer, who sat at the end of the table with her daughter and a box of cold noodles between them.

Elena saw him first in the glass. He came in with his coat closed to the throat, as if the building were cold, and stopped by the end of the table.

"What are you drawing?" he said.

The girl showed him. Rows of boxes, every one coloured to the edges.

"Those are mine," Bellamy said. "You've got them in the right order." He looked at the drawing longer than the drawing needed. Then he looked at the mother, and she took the noodles and her daughter and went, and the door shut behind them on its soft seal.

He stood with his back to the boards.

"You asked for the line," he said.

"I asked for a precedent." She kept her voice level, a little bored. "The Lantern couples live to the boundary. We did that once. The review should see what happened when we did."

"We know what happened."

"The review doesn't."

He nodded slowly, as if she had made a good point and he would give it its due.

"When the programme closed I sealed it myself," he said, "every file, and since then I've been asked to open it by good lawyers and bad ones, by two councils and a Compact inspector, and every one of them came to me saying precedent, and every one of them wanted the room. They wanted to go back into the room and stand in it."

He reached down and squared the girl's handheld to the edge of the table, where she had left it.

"Grief will build you anything you ask for, beautifully and on time, and every drawing it makes is a door back into the same room. Grief is a bad engineer, Elena."

He said it to the handheld, not to her, and his voice did not change at all. He had slept badly for years. She could see every one of them in his face.

She could ask again. Rachel had written nothing in secret into the review, and Rachel's name had carried the vote, and the seal on packet one already broke the condition in front of anyone who cared to look. She could put that on the table between them.

And he would ask her why she cared so much about a protocol, on a Thursday, in a heat closure. And she would have to answer. And he was watching her hands.

She unclasped them.

"It was a method question," she said.

"We know." He looked almost relieved. Under the table her hand closed over the other. "And the method's sound. We've had it checked by people who were never in that room. It's the only way to check anything." He turned to go, and turned back. "Go home early, Elena. The city's shut the schools. Nobody will think less of us."

The door sealed behind him.

She opened the archive and withdrew her request, so that it would at least say she had stopped. The archive took it. Request withdrawn — E. Vázquez. It did not take the first line away.

· · ·

By seven the room was empty, the others gone home to children who had been inside all day. The cooling dropped its note as the sun went down behind the towers. Out past the glass the sprayers' sunset had started: copper, then rust, long over the harbour, longer than it should have been.

Her lace chimed. Daniel.

His face came up at the edge of her eye, sunburnt across the nose, the roof behind him and the copper sky behind that. Somebody small was shrieking with laughter off to one side.

"Don't panic," he said. "Nobody's lost a finger."

"Whose fingers?"

"Three from downstairs. The school's shut, their mothers are on shift, and I've been made responsible for the future." He tipped the camera. Three heads bent over his vice by the parapet, by his node in its taped wrap, and one of his burnt hands was steadying a much smaller one on the iron. "The middle one's a natural. The little one is eating the flux."

"Daniel."

"He is not eating the flux." He brought the camera back. "Come home and lower the average. I'll make the beans." He looked at her more closely. "Why are you still in the building?"

"The review ran long."

"The review ran long at lunchtime. You've got the fault face on."

She looked at the three heads, and the hand on the iron, and the wrap with its seam turned to the wall.

"Soon," she said.

"Soon," he said, as if he were writing it down, and the call closed.

The review console chimed.

Packet received. Mars relay, calibration band. Raw copy: safety review.

Nobody else was there to read it. She brought it up on the console where the whole room could have seen it, because the review was meant to have nothing in secret, and she was the review.

It was short. One pulse train, a few hundred bytes, in the frames she knew by heart now. No tracks. No correction. No clock. The two warnings had carried a destination, Cobalt Basin and then the Battery. This one's field was zeroed. Addressed to no one.

It parsed on the first pass. It was text, and it was in Spanish.

Te dormiste en el once.

She read the rest without breathing much.

Your head on my leg and your feet in the water, and I didn't wake you, because you'd only have cried about the feet. I kept counting. Somebody had to. The brown one with the collar was twenty. When it got light it was thirty-seven, and the water had stopped at the top of the window across the street, and I was going to tell you the number in the boat, but you were

That was all.

She knew the roof. The rain coming sideways, the flood marks painted up the walls of Veracruz with their years beside them, the dead dogs turning slowly in brown water a street wide, and Soria's hand on her hair while they whispered the numbers, one each, to stop being afraid.

She had never known how far the count went. In more than thirty years it had not once crossed her mind to ask.

She checked anyway, because she was who she was. She ran it against every record the Charter held of either of them, and she had built half of them. The flood was everywhere: its year, its dead, its photographs of a city to the second floor. The roof was nowhere. The count had only ever been in one head, and that head had gone into a sealed room in this building six years ago and not come out.

You'd only have cried about the feet.

Elena laughed, once, out loud in the empty room, and put her hand over her mouth.

Then she opened the review's hard-stop channel.

Rachel had written it into the review, a stop order it could send without asking anyone. It sat at the bottom of the console behind a cover, a red line drawn around a field. For the review's stop order only.

Foresight listened. Foresight did not speak. She had signed the undertaking every January since she joined, and written the training that taught the rest of them to sign it.

She built the frames by hand. The calibration format, the one the packets had come down in, filled with data instead of hiss, as whoever this was had done. It took her the best part of half an hour. Her hands were steady. That surprised her.

Grief is a bad engineer. She checked the frames again, every one. They were good.

She did not write the name. If it was her, she would not need it. If it was not, it would have one more thing of hers.

She wrote three words.

Who are you?

The console laid out what it would cost before it let her go.

Transmission: Mars relay, calibration band. Relay time billed by the minute. Charged to: Lantern safety review. Authorising credential: E. Vázquez, Foresight. Send?

Her name, in a log she had built so that nobody could change it without leaving a mark, where anyone on the review would find it in the morning.

She sent it.

Sent, said the console. The log wrote it in its margin, small and grey: Transmitted 20:26 — E. Vázquez.

She did not go home. She sat back in Rachel's chair with her hands open on the table, and the harbour gone dark beyond the glass, and her name in the margin, and listened to the band.
