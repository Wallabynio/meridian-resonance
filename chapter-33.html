The state of emergency arrived at 04:00 as a sentence, and by dawn it had become architecture.

Xiàng Fēi heard the sentence the way everyone in the residential tier heard it — through the dormitory address system, in the Coalition's formal register, a voice that had been engineered decades ago to sound like the state thinking aloud. By directive of Beijing Command, a condition of emergency is declared across all Coalition territories. Research installations of strategic designation pass to military administration, effective immediately. Personnel will present for revised credentialing at shift commencement. The Coalition thanks you for your discipline. No siren. The Coalition did not use sirens for things it intended to be permanent.

He lay still in the dark for a long moment, listening to the building change around him — doors cycling somewhere below, the deep clunk of magnetic bolts seating in frames that had never been bolted, boots in a corridor that had only ever known the soft tread of researchers who worked too late. Then he got up, dressed with his usual care, and went to see what his life had become overnight.

The walk to the laboratory towers took eleven minutes, and it was, absurdly, beautiful. It was always beautiful; that was the thing about Baikal that no security directive had ever managed to requisition. The complex stood on the frozen lake like a settlement on the back of something sleeping, and at this hour the ice stretched out grey-blue to a horizon serrated with black mountains, and above it the aurora was fading — long slow ribbons of green with a violet fringe, the only colour the Coalition had never specified, moving against a sky going pale in the east. Xiàng had watched that sky every working morning of his life here. This morning he watched it through a new fence.

The fence had gone up in the night — prefabricated sections, grey composite with cold state-blue piping along the rails, the blue indicator arrays on the checkpoint housings pulsing their slow official rhythm. At the tower entrance where there had been a card reader and an old joke taped to the inside of the glass, there was now a credentialing station, two soldiers in winter greys, and a queue of physicists standing in the cold with their breath rising, holding their identification like people who had been told their own names were under review.

"Purpose of access," the nearer soldier said, when his turn came. Not unkindly. That was the worst of it, Xiàng would think later — how little unkindness was needed.

"Resonance Laboratory. Interference calibration, scheduled." He held out his credentials and kept his hands steady, and they were steady, because he had long since learned to stand inside institutions with his body under separate management. Chalk dust in the creases of his fingers. A stoop he had stopped correcting. Nothing to see.

The station read him — retina, gait record, the pattern of him — and a formal voice, the same voice as the dormitory address in miniature, spoke from the housing. Credential revision confirmed. Access granted under military administration. Communications originating from this installation are subject to monitoring under the emergency condition. Compliance is appreciated.

Communications are subject to monitoring. The sentence went through him like a current through a fringe pattern and left everything, on the surface, exactly as it had been. He inclined his head to the soldier — the precise depth of nod that institutional life had calibrated in him — and walked in under the new lights, thinking, with a calm that surprised him, of a chamber four hundred thousand kilometres away, of a channel routed to look harmless in every log it passed through, of his own voice saying, ten days ago and half a beat delayed: when they militarise us — not if.

Not if. He had said it so that no one would mistake what he could and couldn't do. He had not expected the coming due to feel so much like weather — vast, impersonal, already here.

The security officer stood where the light was worst, and did not pretend to understand the experiment, and that was what made him dangerous.

He had introduced himself at shift commencement as the laboratory's oversight officer under the emergency condition — a captain, winter greys with the state-blue collar flash, perhaps forty, with the patient, unembarrassed attention of a man who had watched many people do many things he did not understand and had learned that understanding was not his job. His job was presence. He had a folding stool, which he did not use, and a terminal slaved to the laboratory's logging system, which he did.

"Proceed as normal," he had said. "The condition changes nothing about your work."

The condition changed everything about the work, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said it, and that was the new shape of the room.

Xiàng ran the morning calibration with his usual precision because his precision was the only room left to him that no one else was standing in. The interference chamber woke under his hands the way it had woken a thousand mornings — the fringe field blooming on the display, bands of light and dark like the cross-section of something breathing — and for a few minutes the old wonder came up in him despite everything, because the fringes were the fringes: the universe agreeing, again, to be measured, on conditions it had lately been renegotiating. Once, in this room, he had watched these bands rotate in a way that violated his boundary conditions and had sat still in front of the display for four minutes while the monitoring system logged his stillness and moved on. That stillness had been the first act of the rest of his life. The system had found it unremarkable.

The new system found nothing unremarkable. When the run completed, the laboratory spoke — the formal register, the state thinking aloud in a smaller room. Calibration complete. Fringe coherence within revised tolerances. Result logged and classified: restricted, defence application review. Onward transmission is prohibited.

Defence application review. Xiàng looked at the fringe data — his fringe data, the anomaly lineage he had been tending like a garden ever since, the same rotational signatures he had once folded into citation-forwarding traffic for a stranger in Neo Singapore because keeping them to himself had felt like a kind of lie — and watched the classification wrap around it in real time. Every result he produced from this morning forward would be born twice: once as physics, once as ammunition. The same numbers. The state had not changed the data. It had changed what the data was for, and it had done it with a sentence, the way it did everything.

"Interesting work?" the captain asked, from his place by the wall. It was, Xiàng understood, a courtesy. The man was being human at him in the manner prescribed for long assignments.

"Calibration," Xiàng said. "The instrument drifts. We correct it. Most of science is correcting the drift." He heard his own voice arrive level and pleasant and true, every word of it true, which was the only way to lie inside a building that measured you. The captain nodded, satisfied, and made a small entry on his terminal — logging, perhaps, that the subject had been forthcoming — and Xiàng turned back to the display, to the beautiful, breathing, conscripted fringes.

The revised tolerances were the tell. He had read the parameter file at shift start, as he read everything: the emergency condition had not simply put a soldier in his laboratory, it had appended a work programme — field-projection thresholds, pattern-fragmentation coefficients, range and penetration studies. Someone above him had decided what resonance was for, and had written it down in the bloodless grammar of experimental design, and had scheduled him to build it.

I'd like you all to know I've already decided, he had told the alliance, with his image stuttering across half a planet. It just hasn't come due.

It was coming due at the speed of a scheduling system. Not today — today was calibration; the parameter file's worst entries sat some weeks out, patient as everything else here. He had that long to be two people. He logged the drift correction, requested the next run, and let the monitoring system record a man at work, unremarkable, still.

Tán Měi had been at the Baikal Complex for nine days, and the militarisation orders crossed her workstation at 05:12, and she read them twice, gloved hands flat on the workstation, before she permitted herself to understand that this was the morning she had spent four years arranging to be here for.

The transfer had been routine, which is to say she had made it look routine, which is to say it had taken her seven months. A military-technology officer with her service record and her reputation for exact, colourless competence was precisely what a strategic research installation wanted in advance of a security transition, and the personnel system had believed, correctly, that assigning her here was its own idea. She had spent her whole career being what systems expected to find. She had spent the last four years being it on purpose, in rooms selected for what could one day be carried out of them: the edge of Wáng Sù's official circle in the Beijing Broadcast Chamber, where she had stood three weeks at the correct distance and confirmed figures in the flattest possible register and noted everything; the technical committees; the review assignments. Positioned. The word the far side of her life would have used, if she had ever once permitted herself to speak to the far side of her life.

She had not. In four years she had sent nothing, said nothing, been nothing but the role. A network you have never used cannot have been detected being used. She had built her channel to the Sydney Hub the way the Coalition had taught her to build everything — patiently, deniably, in layers — four months of cultivating a data-backup verification pathway through an administrative system her military-technology role gave her every legitimate reason to touch, and then she had left it there, untouched, a door built into a wall and painted over. Meticulousness was not her method. It was her nature, which was why the method had never shown.

The orders themselves were, in their way, admirable drafting. The public announcement, released simultaneously, described an administrative restructuring of the Baikal Complex — a phrase engineered to be forgotten in the reading of it. The internal directive described the truth in the Coalition's other voice: the weapons-application programme at the Resonance Laboratory elevated to priority status, its technical parameters revised and appended — field-projection thresholds, pattern-fragmentation coefficients, range and penetration studies — its results classified at birth, its personnel placed under oversight. She read the appended parameters with a technologist's eye and understood exactly what they described, and sat with that understanding, gloved, still, for the length of one long breath.

Then she did her job, correctly, for six hours. She reviewed the credentialing rollout. She countersigned the communications-monitoring implementation — an irony she declined to feel anything about, since feeling things about ironies was a luxury belonging to people who were not standing inside them. She was, in every log and every doorway, exactly what the emergency condition required her to be.

At 11:40, inside the routine execution of her actual duties, she performed one additional task, forty seconds long, four years deep.

The summary was already composed — had been composed in her head since 05:12, in the same flat register she used for everything, because a summary that sounded like her was a summary no filter would ever catch: the militarisation orders, the priority elevation, and the appended technical parameters of the weapons-application programme, complete and exact. She wrapped it in the syntax of a data-backup verification, because the pathway she had spent four months cultivating was a backup-verification pathway and the wrapping was not a disguise so much as the truth of the route. The packet would cross the administrative system as maintenance traffic, and it would verify an actual backup, because Tán Měi did not build lies that were not also facts, and somewhere at the far end — the Sydney Hub, a relay maintained by a man she had never met and never would — the verification would complete, and inside its completion the Coalition's morning would arrive in the hands of people who would know what it meant.

She reviewed the packet once. She corrected nothing, because there was nothing to correct. She released it into the system's scheduled traffic, watched it become indistinguishable from the ten thousand honest packets around it, and noted the transmission in no notebook, on no tablet, in no place but the single unlogged room the Coalition had never credentialed: her own attention, which had been noting everything, at the edge of every circle, for her entire career.

In the logs, it looked like a routine data-backup verification. It looked like that because she was meticulous, and because she was meticulous, no one would ever look.

She drew her gloves straight, took up the credentialing rollout, and went back to being what the system expected to find.

The public announcement was fourteen lines long, and Lǐ Jūn read it standing up, in the blue glow of the Quantum Quarter's factory windows, and knew before he reached the end that he would be working late.

Administrative restructuring. He had been Arjun Patel's student long enough to feel the phrase the way a structural engineer feels a load-bearing wall that sounds hollow when knocked. Suppression for error left gaps — he had found his first gap in a seminar exercise, calibration entries that should have existed and did not, and had noted it in his physical notebook and reported nothing, and his education had begun. Suppression for inconvenience was different. It left surfaces. It left fourteen tidy lines.

The lab complex adjacent to the Quantum Quarter was technically closed at this hour, which was why the work happened at this hour. He keyed in through the side entrance, and the archive was where the archive always was: unremarkable storage, honestly labelled as something else, holding the internal research-status logs of the Baikal Complex programme that he had been quietly accumulating for months — not stolen so much as retained, copies of records that passed through Pearl Delta's research-administration mirrors in the ordinary course of a junior physicist's cataloguing duties, records whose only crime was that the Coalition would later want them never to have existed.

He laid the two records side by side, because side by side was the entire method. That was what Arjun had taught him in a closed lab at midnight, showing him the statistical signature of retrospectively normalised data: tidiness that is actually erasure. The alteration is always cleaner than the truth. Truth has residue.

On the left display: the public announcement. A routine security upgrade at a research installation. Fourteen lines, no residue.

On the right: six months of internal research-status logs. The weapons-integration programme at the Resonance Laboratory — instrument requisitions, parameter revisions, personnel reassignments, milestone reviews — running since the winter, and in the last three status cycles, visibly accelerating. Budget lines thickening. Timelines compressing. A programme the length and shape of an intention.

The discrepancy was not a gap. It was a wall built in front of a building, painted to look like the sky.

He documented it the way he had been taught: the announcement and the logs interleaved, every claim in the public text paired with the internal record that contradicted it, dated, sourced, annotated in prose so dry it could survive any tribunal's suspicion of rhetoric. His pen moved in the physical notebook alongside the archive entry — the notebook was not a backup, it was a discipline; a thing written by hand at midnight was a thing he could never later pretend he had not known. Around him the manufacturing towers hummed their permanent shift, and the blue light lay across the workstation like the light of an operating theatre, and Lǐ Jūn worked steadily through the small hours with a feeling he had learned to recognise and no longer tried to talk himself out of: that this — the collating, the pairing, the refusal to let a fourteen-line surface stand unaccompanied in the record — was not preparation for some future importance. It was the important thing itself, being done.

Arjun came in at two, as Lǐ Jūn had known he would, carrying tea, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had decided years ago which risks were his. He read the paired records over his student's shoulder for a long time.

"Six months," Arjun said at last. "They announce it the morning they can no longer hide it, and they announce it as housekeeping." He set a cup at Lǐ Jūn's elbow, away from the notebook. "Route a copy through my channel. Tonight, while it's one more piece of seminar administration among the term's hundreds."

Lǐ Jūn had already prepared the copy. Arjun saw that he had, and something moved briefly in the older man's face that was not quite a smile — recognition, perhaps, of the specific kind: the teacher finding that the method now lived somewhere outside his own hands, and would go on being applied whatever happened to him.

The copy went out at 02:31, folded into the seminar traffic, unremarkable. The original went into the archive, beside everything else the mornings would have brought different constraints for. Lǐ Jūn closed the notebook, capped his pen, and sat a moment in the blue industrial light, in the ordinary hum of a city that made things all night without asking what they were for.

An emergency, the announcement had not said, is a state. But an archive was a state too — a state of evidence, held against the day it could do good, and tonight it was fourteen lines larger, and truer by exactly the size of the lie.
