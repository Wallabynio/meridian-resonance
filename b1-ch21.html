Fractured Horizons · Chapter 21

The surge

Fractured Horizons · II-B. The drift

21

9 JuneRachelNew York, the BatteryMars delay 4.2 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 4.2 min

Joaquín wrote JULY on the flood glass and drew a hurricane under it, badly.

"That's a cinnamon bun," said the night lead.

"That's a category three." He added an eye. It made it worse. "Storm season. The pool is open. Day the first storm comes ashore anywhere between here and Hatteras. Closest day takes the pot, and the pot is my wife's flan, and I've been told to say it's an honour."

The floor at the far end gave him a slow round of applause. It was 06:35 on a Thursday, the harbour behind the glass flat and green and already warm, and the night shift had the hour's softness in it, the end of a long one. The night lead put her initials under the bun and 30, then crossed out the thirty and wrote 1. July the first. Somebody told her she was a ghoul.

The glass smelled of the pencil, wax and old coffee. Behind it the tide knocked on the bronze seam, once a minute, like a man who wasn't going away.

Joaquín held the pencil out. Rachel didn't take it.

"You haven't bet since May," he said.

"I'm working."

"You're listening to Mars." He tapped his own ear. "You've had that face since six."

She had. At 06:00 the director's office had put a post in her queue with a cover line in the Spire's grey. Per the chair's third safeguard: every Mars post touching the Lantern, raw. Attached for context: Cobalt Basin incident report, 5 June. Nothing else. Signed by the office, not the man.

She went up the gallery stairs to her room at the end of the glass and shut the door, and played it again.

"Cobalt Basin, the fifth of June, oh-six-forty. Kaelin Drake, field operations. To Iverson at the Battery, who chaired the Lantern's review, and to Luna Haven's keeper, because your wall is part of it. Bad news first."

A tired voice. A good one. A coast somewhere hot in the vowels. Behind it, close to the handheld, someone breathed. In and out, and in, slow as a tide. Rachel counted it without meaning to. Twelve to the minute.

Lind. It had to be. The woman who had stopped answering on the twenty-eighth. He had recorded it at her bed.

"There are minds alive in the Preserve, in the rock under this station. Martian. Not many."

She stopped it there, as she had the first time, and started it again. They call it the hungry one. Stopped it. It's made of a lot of people, and it says come in. Stopped it. The twelfth of April and the third of June. She wrote the dates on her wrist in pencil without thinking, as she wrote tide times. Nobody signed either one. That's mine. Nobody else's.

The second time it knew my name before I gave it.

Stop the calibration. Don't light it. Reply when you can.

Then the breathing, two breaths more, and the end.

She turned the incident report over and took out a pencil.

Two columns. She had done it in 2071 on the back of a school timetable and in May on the back of the dome report, and it had never once told her what to do, and she did it anyway, because a column made you write the thing down in words a stranger could read.

Right-hand column, first, because it was easy. Cobalt Basin 40. Broad Channel 1,412. The dome 1,611. Under it, smaller, because they were on no map: Rockaways 2,014. Every number on that side had names behind it, and she had carried some of them down stairs.

Left-hand column. DRAKE.

She looked at it.

A field lead on Mars had gone into a live boundary twice with nobody's signature, come out, and posted that a voice in the rock had known his name.

The incident report, the other side of this same sheet, said the rest in the boundary lead's square language.

Field ops lead K. Drake opened head two's jumper during the final calibration, against the boundary lead's refusal, with a frame hand aloft. Lock lost. Run reset. Lead suspended from boundary work pending this report. She had read it twice looking for a lie and found none. At 06:40 his time the man had said Stop the calibration. By the afternoon he had stopped it with his hands.

If one of hers came off a shift like that, saying the water knew his name, she would sit him in the van and look at his eyes. Daniel had asked her to do that much for Elena, a week ago, on pump house nine. She had told him Elena had earned a good day.

Under DRAKE she wrote VÁZQUEZ, and beside it above scale. Under that sensor bias. Under that ROCHA, 9, for the call that was on no board.

Four lines. Not one of them a reason she could put under a signature and defend in a room. A man who heard his name in a rock. An engineer off the top of the Ward's scale. A map that was only as wide as its grid. A husband.

On her board, three lines below the Battery's own, Luna Haven's line sat with its little green dot. She could ask Mara Ekeberg how her wall was. Mara would tell her. It would take a minute.

She put her finger on the line. The dot waited.

She took her finger off, and turned the report face down on the four lines.

She put the pencil down and picked the post up again and played it from the start, and counted Lind's breathing.

· · ·

Bellamy came at 08:10, in person. He never came anywhere before nine.

He came up the gallery with his coat on, the high black collar buttoned in a building kept at nineteen degrees, and the floor watched him pass as it watched weather. He stopped at the flood glass and read the pool. He smiled at the bun.

"May I?" he said at her door.

"It's open."

He didn't sit. He stood with his back to the map wall.

"You've read Mr Drake."

"Four times."

"We read him once, and sent him to you within the hour. We'd have been within our rights to hold it. The review's stood down." He let that sit a moment, gently. "We thought you'd want everything."

The post was stamped 5 June. The office's cover was stamped this morning. She had both stamps in front of her.

"I did want it," she said, and let him have the hour.

"He's a good man. He walked his crew home through a fall in January and carried one of them. He's sat at that woman's bed since the twenty-eighth." His voice stayed low and even. "He went into the rock twice without telling anyone, and on the fifth he opened a live jumper with a man on the frame. He believes what he heard down there. That's what frightens me for him."

Rachel said nothing. She was still. She was good at still.

"Foresight, three-twelve," Bellamy said. "On your wall, I think. Not the floor's."

The wall of her room woke.

New York went small, a grey thumbprint in the corner. The coast came in around it, south to the Carolinas, north to the elbow of the Cape, a thousand kilometres of shoreline in the old blue. Then the warning laid itself over it.

It came in as a smear, no addresses at all. Red from Cape Hatteras to Cape Cod, the whole length, thick along the shore and feathering inland, the Shallows inside it, the Rockaways inside it, the wall itself inside it, every walled city on the seaboard under one brushstroke.

"Foresight advisory," said the room. "Director's authority. Priority one. Caught 03:12. Tropical cyclone. Forms 2 July, east of the Leeward Islands. Landfall 9 to 12 July. Surge to five metres at landfall. Landfall point: unresolved. Track resolution: landfall minus forty-eight hours."

Five metres. The wall was twelve. The Shallows were built for two.

"Drown it," Rachel said, under her breath.

"In June," Rachel said.

"The season's a week old. The weather service won't see this storm until it forms, and won't see where it's going until it's nearly there." He turned at last, and looked at it with her. "We will. The track fixes two days out. On the band we have now, a packet takes most of a day to come through. This one started arriving at six yesterday morning. It finished at three this morning."

"So a day."

"So a day of our two. We'd know where with hours left. You know what hours buy on a coast."

She did. Broad Channel had taken most of a day, and Broad Channel was fourteen hundred people on boardwalks who all heard their names. The Shallows alone would take a week. Nobody had ever cleared a seaboard.

"And on the Lantern."

"Minutes. That's all it's ever been for." He took a slate from inside his coat and laid it on her table, square to the edge. "The run reset on the fifth. It finishes on the thirteenth, two-thirds power, if Mars lets it. The fourteenth is the first night it's ready."

The slate said GO-LIVE — THE LANTERN — 14 JUNE, 21:00 EASTERN, and under it three lines. For the Charter, by delegation of the board: M. Bellamy. For the Mandate: Wáng Sù. Civic director, host city: and a blank.

Two partners and a blank for the city the Earth end sat in.

"Haven," she said.

"Haven's well."

"Haven's readings since the fourth. I want them."

"Haven files to the programme now the review's stood down. I'll have them sent."

"Today."

"Today, of course."

"And my three." She didn't look at the slate. "Every sweep stops on any word lost in its loop. Cobalt Basin's frame readings to me raw. Every Mars post that touches it, raw. Not to a review. To me. Written into this, above the signatures, or there's no—"

"Of course," Bellamy said. "All three, as written."

He said it before she had finished. He had done that in February, and in May, four minutes after she filed. Both times she had heard it and called it competence.

He touched the slate. Three lines rose above the signatures in the Spire's grey, her own words from May, a comma different.

"Beijing's waiting when you are," he said.

· · ·

Wáng Sù was on the wall at 08:52, in a room with a dark window behind him and one lamp. It was nine at night in Beijing. There was a glass of water at his elbow. He folded his hands on the table and waited until she had sat down, which nobody else on any screen had ever done for her.

"Director Iverson. Thank you for your time. I know what the morning costs on your floor."

"Director Wáng."

"I have read the advisory. I have read the incident report from Cobalt Basin. I have read Mr Drake." "I would like to make my case to you, and then I will be silent, and you will decide. I do not think it is my place to hurry you."

"Go on."

"A system that sees further saves more people. That is the whole of it. Since January it has seen a collision on Mars, a barrier in your bay, a heat dome over your Shallows and a surge on your boardwalks, and every time it has seen far enough.

"Now it sees a storm that has not formed, and it cannot see far enough. It is not wrong. We have given it a narrow mouth." He moved his glass a centimetre, and did not drink. "Each week the mouth stays narrow is a week we will count afterwards. I would rather count before."

"Every save on our map sits under the grid," Rachel said. "Every one. Past the Line the map's blank. I've written sensor bias in two reports. Nobody's answered it."

"I will answer it." He inclined his head, as if she had done him a courtesy. "You have told me the system cannot see past your Line. You have told me it is blind where the poorest people live. Then I do not understand why you would keep it blind. A wider channel sees further. I am offering it eyes, Director. You are the one asking it to stay as it is."

She had no answer that didn't start with A man on Mars told me. She didn't give it.

"Mr Drake," Wáng said, as if he had heard it anyway. "I believe he heard what he says he heard. I believe it as I believe my grandmother heard my grandfather call her from the next room, the year after he died. I do not sign treaties on it."

"You don't know what he heard."

"No. Neither do you. That is my point, and I am sorry to make it." He was silent a moment. Then, more gently than anything he had yet said: "May I say one more thing? You may tell me it is not my business."

"Say it."

"In 2071 you stood in a school gymnasium with a list and a dead radio, and waited thirty-one hours for a procedure to tell you who went first. Nine people on your list died while you waited."

He did not look away from her. "I read it in your file when the council voted for you in February. I do not say it to wound you. I say it because you are the only person on this call who has counted the cost of a slow channel with her own hands. A narrow band is a slow procedure, Director. It is the same thing. It always has been."

Behind Rachel the harbour knocked on the seam.

Nine. She knew their names. She had never been able to say whether they had died waiting for her or for the procedure, and the man in Beijing had just told her which, kindly, from a lamp-lit room in the dark, and he was right about delay. He had been right about delay in 2053, at thirteen, on a truck.

Bellamy said nothing. He had moved to the side of the room where she couldn't see him without turning, and she didn't turn.

"Give me one minute," she said.

"Of course," said Wáng, and sat back, and waited.

She went out of her room onto the gallery.

The pool stood on the glass where she had left it, the bun and the initials and the night lead's 1 written over her 30. Joaquín was at the rail below with his handheld, frowning at a message he kept not opening.

Below him the day shift was coming on, jackets with names across their backs, the map wall grey with the ordinary city. Somebody was laughing at the coffee machine. The pumps under the floor hummed through her shoes, the hum she had loved for twenty years.

She took the pencil off its string. Under the bun she wrote IVERSON 14. The fourteenth of July, two days past the end of the warning's window. Nobody on the floor knew the window. It was the only way she had left to say she hoped the thing was wrong.

Joaquín looked up from his handheld and saw it, and stared.

She went back in and shut the door.

Wáng had signed. His name sat in the Mandate's script and again beneath it in the Charter's letters, green. Bellamy leaned past her and signed under the Charter's line with one long stroke, and straightened, and stepped back again out of her eye.

The blank waited.

She picked up the stylus. Her own three lines stood above the names. The storm stood on her wall, with its five metres and its unresolved. The four lines in pencil lay face down under her elbow, and not one of them was a reason.

R. Iverson, she wrote, civic director.

The door opened while the stylus was still moving. Joaquín came in without knocking, which he had done once in twelve years, with two coffees, and stopped when he saw the wall.

She lifted the stylus. The slate took her name and turned it green. "Thank you, Director," said Wáng's lamp-lit room, softly, and went dark, and the three signatures went out to Beijing and the Spire and the Signatories at once.

Bellamy took his coat from the chair he hadn't sat in. "Thank you, Rachel." He nodded to Joaquín at the door on his way past, as to a colleague, and went down the gallery, and the floor watched him go.

Joaquín put a coffee by her hand.

"You bet," he said. "July the fourteenth. That's not a bet, that's—" And he smiled.

He only smiled like that before bad news. That was how she knew.

"Rosa wrote me last night." He had his handheld in his other hand, the family line open on it, a long message in Spanish she couldn't read. "I didn't open it till just now. I'm sorry. I thought it was the baptism."

He looked at the screen. "Seven beds since the fourth. Keepers, a crop hand, a woman from the water plant. Since the sweeps. Six going, one gone quiet, a man with a beard, she turns him all night. She says nobody's answered her reports."

Rachel didn't move. Haven files to the programme now. I'll have them sent.

"She asks me," Joaquín said, and his voice went down into his other language and came back. "Primo, ¿fuimos nosotros? Was it us."

Rachel put her hand flat on the slate, over her name, as if the ink were still wet.
