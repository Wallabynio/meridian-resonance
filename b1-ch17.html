The lifeboat

Fractured Horizons · II-B. The drift

17

3 JuneKaelinMars, the PreserveMars delay 4.1 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, THE PRESERVE / Kaelin Drake — Earth 4.1 min

Bao was doing the voices again, and the pump manual had never deserved them.

"'The impeller,'" he read, low and grave, a man at a graveside, "'should be inspected for scoring at intervals not exceeding four hundred hours.'" He looked up from the handheld at Petra. "Four hundred, Petra. Not four hundred and one. The impeller knows."

"Three sixty on the old housings," said Selma from the next bunk. She had her scanner cap on and her eyes shut and her arms folded on her chest. "Read the footnote."

"Nobody reads the footnote."

"Funmi read the footnote."

"Funmi read the footnote to annoy people." Bao found it anyway, and read it in a high prim voice, as if the footnote were an aunt. "'Three hundred and sixty on housings of the earlier pattern.'" He lowered the handheld. "She's right. She's always right. It's very tiring."

Petra lay in the fifth bunk with her hair brushed out on the pillow, because Selma had brushed it, and her eyes half open on the ceiling. Twelve breaths to the minute. Kaelin had counted them at six and again at eight.

Tomorrow, on Earth's clock, the Lantern went live, and he had nothing that would stop it that anybody on Earth would read.

"Ten o'clock," Ines said. "Turn."

He took the hips and she took the shoulders, and on three they rolled Petra toward the wall, slow, so the line didn't pull. Ines wedged the blanket at her back. Kaelin checked her heels, one and then the other. No red. He told Ines, and Ines wrote it.

"Seals," Bao announced, scrolling. "This is the good bit. Petra, you'll like this. Somebody gets a gasket wrong and there's a diagram."

"There's always a diagram," said Selma.

"This one has a man in it. He looks sorry."

On the sky repeater over the bunks there was nothing but brown. The arrays on the berm were buried, the pile carried the station alone, and everybody wore two layers and had grit in their back teeth. Nobody at Cobalt Basin had seen the Sun since the twenty-eighth. Six days. Kaelin had stopped looking up to check.

In the repeater's corner, small, the Earth feed ran New York bright as a burn under its white sky, the surge flags up along the Battery and boats coming off the low moorings.

Ines had the minutes from Earth open on the overlay beside Petra's trace. She hadn't closed them since they came in overnight. So he read them.

The review finds no cause to halt. Seven ayes. Go-live on the fourth, as scheduled. Then the observer's answers, one per line, clean as a manifest. P. Lind: a contact injury at the core, recorded in March. The stop worked. And under it, his three traces from the twenty-eighth, Selma's and Bao's and Petra's, standing up together at the pulse: a coupling artefact on the station's power bus. No control.

"A power bus," Selma said. She hadn't opened her eyes. "I've been called worse. Bao calls me worse."

"Never on the record," said Bao.

At the foot of it: The review recommends a medical berth home for P. Lind on the next available ship, programme reserve or none. Then who it had gone to. The Signatories. The council. Beijing Command. Cobalt Basin. Luna Haven.

He read the last one twice. Then he went back up the page.

"Anything on the berth?" he asked.

"Nothing." Ines didn't look up. "It's a recommendation. They can recommend it into the sea."

The Funafuti left orbit on the ninth. Three grey lines on her manifest. Go-live was tomorrow.

Kaelin looked at Petra's trace, flat, waiting for the sweep to stand it up. A trace was a line. Anybody with a clean motion to send could call a line a power bus, and they had.

He needed words. Words a bus couldn't make.

"I'm going to the boundary," he said.

Ines looked at him then. She took in all of him and decided what to say about it, and what she said was, "Twelve o'clock's the next turn."

"I'll be back for it."

She didn't say that was what he'd said in April. She didn't have to.

· · ·

The boundary module was colder than medical by ten degrees, and bluer, and Aur'k was where Aur'k always was.

Every trace on the monitors was flat. At the next sweep they would all stand up together, as they had on the twenty-eighth.

"Take me down," he said.

Aur'k didn't turn. His helm was on the seam where the station's rock met the Preserve's. Dust had got into his plating up to the knee again and nobody had brushed it off.

"I read the minutes," Aur'k said.

"Then you know what they said about your traces."

"I know what they said." Now he turned. "I don't know what you want from the rock."

"Words. In plain words, from whatever lives down there, what's listening and what lights up tomorrow." He heard his own voice and kept it level. "Words I can put in front of the review and nobody can call them an artefact."

The stillness. Kaelin waited, and when it went on long enough he said, "Zhenyth."

Aur'k's helm came round to him, the whole of it, and stayed.

Then Aur'k went to the shelf by the monitors and took down a notebook.

It was paper. Kaelin hadn't known there was a notebook of paper on the station. It was thick and soft at the edges and had a pencil tied to its spine with a length of wire, and Aur'k opened it on the bench with care, as if it might come apart, and turned it to face him.

Columns, in Aur'k's square hand. A date, a length of time, and a third figure with no unit beside it. Halfway down the page Kaelin found his own night. 12 April. K.D. 10 min. Beside it, the third figure, written small.

There were pages above it. He could see their edges under the cover. He didn't count them. It was the first thing in his life he had decided not to count.

"What's that number?" he asked.

"What it cost."

"Cost what?"

"The rock. It doesn't grow back." Aur'k put one finger beside the small figure without touching it. "That was you. Ten minutes."

"Paper," Kaelin said.

"Paper doesn't get classified."

That was nearly a joke.

"Tomorrow they put the biggest relay ever built against that rock," Kaelin said, "and run it all night. What does that cost? Put that in your book."

"I will. Afterwards." Aur'k closed the notebook and kept his hand flat on it. "You want to go deeper than you did. Longer than ten minutes."

"As long as it takes."

"Then it isn't yours to spend." He didn't raise his voice. He never had to. "It isn't mine."

Kaelin knew that line. Aur'k had held it in ops in January with an ice frame coming down, and it had not moved since.

"Whose, then?"

"We ask," said Aur'k. "If the answer is no, you come up. Not at ten minutes. At no."

"And if it's yes?"

"Then the next sweep is at two minutes past eleven. You're up before it."

· · ·

At the Drop's head, Aur'k's palm hung a hand's width from the panel. Green. Logging.

"I can leave it running," Aur'k said. "Then it happened. Then there's a record."

Kaelin looked at the green light.

A record was a field lead who went down the Drop without sign-off, on the night before go-live, after the review had found no cause. He'd be relieved by noon. The Drop would be closed to its boundary lead by one. Whatever he brought up would be read first by the people writing his relief, and they would thank him for it.

And without one, whatever he brought up would come from nowhere. Words, and no way to say where he'd got them.

He knew. He'd take the words.

"Pause it," he said.

The green went amber. LOG PAUSED. BOUNDARY LEAD.

On the surface board by the panel the storm read sixth day, wind at the berm, the pile carrying everything. Then the cage took them.

He counted the collars on the way down and didn't pretend he wouldn't. At thirty-four the rock went blue. At the bottom Aur'k put out both lamps, and Kaelin looked back at the lock's telltale, and then at the Glow coming up around them out of the dark, the veins faint and branching across the walls and the roof. He'd thought the second time would be smaller. It wasn't.

The tent. The gauge. His breath going out white. He took the cap off his socket and it came stiff, and he closed his fist on it, and Aur'k laid his palm flat on the rock.

"Zhenyth," Aur'k said.

"It's me," said Kaelin. "The one who counts. I want to come further in than last time. Say no, and I go back up."

He laid his head against the wall.

· · ·

The plain came up under him slow, lit from beneath, swell after swell, the tide over a reef flat at dusk. He knew it this time. He knew the attention too, old and very near and very far off, and he knew before anything else that it was more tired than it had been in April.

He had a speech, built on the way down, collar by collar. The attention got there first. It didn't say no. It turned a small thing over in front of him, a closed hand opening, and then it was words.

What does rain sound like?

Far off, in a tent, he laughed. He felt his real mouth do it. Above the tent there were three hundred metres of rock and a storm in its sixth day, and down here the only weather was his breath.

"Rain," he said.

The attention waited. A whole dry world leaned toward a door.

So he gave it the Reef Coast. The house on its stumps with the tin roof. The first drops, a few, like somebody tapping to be let in. Then all at once, like gravel poured from a height, so loud you had to shout across the kitchen. The shutters banging on their hooks. The gutters giving up.

His brother under the table with his hands over his ears, small, and Kaelin under there with him, counting the gap between the flash and the thunder, one, two, three, four, so his brother would know it was going away. And after, when it stopped, the frogs. Thousands of them. All night.

He hadn't meant to give it his brother. It came out of him like breath, and the attention took it.

It took all of it, slowly. The plain went still under it, and he felt the old dry thing drink. Not a drop went back.

Then it gave.

· · ·

He was in the rock.

He had no body. He had wet. All round him, close, there were others in the wet with him, and he knew them, and they knew him. That was the world: the wet, the warmth, and the others.

The wet was going.

He felt it drain. The brine thickened round him into a slow salt that hurt to move through. Cold came down from above, and every year the others were further off, with less wet between them to reach across. Then the first of them went out.

Just out. A warmth that had been beside him, and then the dark where it had been.

Then the next. Then a run of them, east to west, and he had seen this before. He had watched it in a rover's mirror, the towers of the Ice-Works going dark one by one, and he could not stop it then either.

Then the call came.

It came warm, and it had been made, with care, by minds as frightened as he was. It came in many voices under one, glad, and every voice said the same thing. Come in. Nobody goes out alone in here. Into it, all round him, the others went. He felt each of them go, and the relief of it, the cold gone, the hurt of the salt gone.

He held back, the one he was. The salt hurt. He held back anyway.

He called after one of them, one who had been close beside him for longer than he had words for. Come back. And the answer came out of the warm, glad, in a thousand voices, and the one he loved was in it and he couldn't find which voice. He felt them try. He felt them turn for the way out and reach for it. It wasn't there.

Kaelin had loaded storm boats, gunwales low in brown water, children passed in over the side. You got them in, and at the other end you got them off.

This one had no other end. It took everyone and let nobody off. It got bigger. It got warmer. It called louder.

A few went the other way. He felt them go, down, into the deep wet rock under a wide basin, and close it behind them, and grow a guard at the door out of the rock itself.

The plain came back.

They were around him. He felt them now, and couldn't think how he had missed them in April. A few. Close. Wary of a stranger at the door in a bad year. One nearer than the rest, turned toward him, young and sharp and not at all tired.

"There are people down here," Kaelin said, with his real mouth, into the stone.

Nobody answered that. Nobody needed to.

"Who built it?" he said. "The boat. How did it start?"

The plain went still. It stayed still, and he knew that stillness. He had lived beside it for six years. It was an answer that was going to stay where it was.

"Fine," he said. "Later."

Far off, in a tent, a hand closed on his shoulder.

"Kaelin." Aur'k's voice, from the bottom of a well. "The sweep. Up."

He had agreed to that, at the bench, with the book open.

"Not yet," he said.

On the plain the needle stood up, white and hard and narrow, very well made, and he felt it go through the rock like a bit through a core.

The dark at the edges warmed.

It leaned in. It turned, the whole wide warmth of it, toward him, and it was the call. Many voices under one. Come in. And close in front of all of them, a laugh, too loud for the room.

"Liar," somebody said, delighted. "Kaelin. Who dealt this rubbish?"

It found his name at once. She hadn't found his name since April.

He went toward it. He didn't decide to. He was the one who went out for people. He had gone out for Sami and the three from rig two, and he had not gone out for Tuvalu and Funmi, and he had carried that for five months. This one he could reach. He could count the distance. The plain tipped toward her, warm, and nobody here would need him to say when, ever again.

The attention came between.

It came hard, and it cost. He felt it tear. A hand's width of the slow light went out under him. Somewhere a few minds flinched together. Then the words, in his own voice, or in none, plain as a post.

The voice your world is listening to is ours. It is the hungry one.

And he was out.

· · ·

Cold stone. His own pulse. Blood in his mouth, warm and then not, where he had bitten his tongue coming out. Aur'k's hand under his arm, lifting.

He turned his head to look at the wall, because he always looked at what he was leaving.

Where his head had lain, the blue had gone out. A patch of the knot the width of a hand, dark. He looked away from it, to the edge of his sight, where the veins always came up brightest, and the patch stayed dark. Every other vein in the cavern came up blue around it. That one didn't.

Aur'k saw it too. He didn't say anything.

At the top, in the grey module, Aur'k opened the notebook on the bench and wrote. The date. K.D. A length of time that was longer than Kaelin had thought. Then the third figure, and he pressed harder on that one, and the pencil lead broke, and he took it to the wire and sharpened it with two fingers and finished.

Kaelin stood by the Drop's head with his gloves off and the cap in his fist.

He had a question. It was right there. Did you know there were people down there? He could ask it. Aur'k would go still, and build an answer that stayed true, whatever it was. Kaelin had watched him choose a promise over him once, in May. He didn't know what this answer would be, and he found he didn't want to stand here and watch it built.

"Do I resume the log?" Aur'k asked.

Kaelin nodded.

The amber went green.

· · ·

The light had come up with him again. It lay along the passage floor in long slow bands that moved when his eyes moved, and he walked through them to medical.

The passage went past ops. On the repeater, in green: FIELD OPS SHARE: JUNE, UNSPENT. He could spend it in a minute, and a minute after it landed it would be classified under the same code as May's. Bellamy's code sat on every door that went anywhere.

If Zhenyth was right, Earth had been listening to it since January. Tomorrow they lit it.

He could tell Ines. She'd believe every word of it, and then it would be hers to carry too.

She was in the good chair when he came in, with the marker on its string in her lap, and she looked at his face and at his mouth, and got up.

"It's not twelve," she said. "Sit down before you fall."

At twelve they turned Petra together, Ines at the shoulders and him at the hips, on three, slow, toward the room. He checked her heels. No red. He told Ines. Ines wrote it, and wiped his lip with her thumb, and looked at the blood on it, and didn't ask.

"I'm going to eat," she said. "I left lentils on. I'll bring you some. You won't eat it."

When she'd gone, he sat on the cot.

Selma was asleep under her cap. Bao had fallen asleep with the manual face down on his chest, open at the man who looked sorry. On the overlay Petra's trace lay flat. The sweeps had come and gone and stood it up and laid it down, and he had missed the last of them, because he had been in the warm with her.

Or with what had her voice.

He took her hand. It was warm. It didn't close.

"There are people down there," he told her. "Not many. They hid. There's one who's young." He rubbed his thumb over her knuckles. "They asked me what rain sounds like. I told them about the frogs."

She breathed. Twelve to the minute.

"You'd have said I was cheating."

He didn't have anybody else. Not one person on two worlds who would hear it and could do anything with it before tomorrow. So he told the one who couldn't, all of it, the wet and the salt and the boat with no other end, and last he gave her the words he had brought up, as he had been given them.

"The voice your world is listening to is ours," he said. "It is the hungry one."
