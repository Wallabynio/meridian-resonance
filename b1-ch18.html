Fractured Horizons · Chapter 18

Opposition

Fractured Horizons · II-B. The drift

18

4 JuneElenaNew York, homeMars delay 4.1 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE POLDER / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.1 min

At 18:00 the review's register went grey, one line at a time, from the top.

Elena watched it go from the kitchen table. Every packet since January, every raw capture the review had fought for, every frame on the hard-stop channel she had sent her question up, and the answer that had come back down it. The overlay re-labelled them as they went. Lantern programme. Sealed: Director. At the bottom, the channel itself greyed last, and a line of type came up under it, polite and procedural.

The safety review has completed its work. Thank you for your service.

She read it through to the end. Then she read the channel's status again, in case it had changed. It hadn't. The window was open. Mars was as close as it would come all year, the Lantern's last calibration was due to begin at Cobalt Basin, and from tonight anything that came down that channel would go to Bellamy's desk and nowhere else.

The flat was at thirty-one degrees. The building's cooling had gone to stage two at noon, for the heat, and the fan over the table pushed the same air from one side of the room to the other. Through the ceiling came the scrape of Daniel dragging the misting rig across the roof, and his voice, and children's voices answering it.

Her lace showed her his name. She opened a message.

Can I

She looked at the two words. He would come down. He would stand in the kitchen with his burnt hands open and ask her what it was for, and she would have to tell him, and there was no way to say it that a reasonable man would hear as anything but grief. She deleted the two words.

She pressed behind her ear until the overlay went out. Then she went to his drawer.

The spare was where it always was, behind the fuses and the solder, its hinge taped, the cartoon fish on its back worn down to one eye. It found the roof node on its own. Everything of his found everything else of his. She wrote the knock, and the door she had built at his family's table four years ago took it, and opened, as it had in January.

Compliance had traced the route and ruled on the town. Nobody had re-keyed the layer it ran into, because the layer answered to her.

She didn't ask for a night this time, or a baseline. She asked for the channel, live, raw, whatever came.

The mesh told her what it would cost, roof by roof, out over the Shallows and back. It wasn't much. A few hundred bytes a pulse.

She took it.

The spare's screen went to the hiss she had known for years, the band's own noise, tonight with nothing in it. She turned the sound up until it filled her palm. It was close to rain, if you had grown up under rain.

Upstairs, Daniel laughed at one of the children. She put the handheld on the bedroom sill, face up, still talking, and went up to help with the misters.

· · ·

The towers went red at nine.

They did it all together, the old glass and the new woven grey, the Spire too, from the waterline to the crowns, and the roofs of the Polder answered with a noise like a crowd at a goal. Somebody on the next building let off a party horn. Somebody else told him to stop.

It was too hot to be indoors and too hot to be anywhere else. The sea wind came over the wall from the Walk in long breaths, and between them the air sat on the roof like a hand. Daniel had the misting rig going at the parapet end, a hoop of copper pipe on a stand with nine nozzles, two of which worked. The children of the building stood in it in their clothes and screamed.

"It's an artwork," he said, when Elena came up beside him. "The ones that don't work are a statement."

"About what?"

"About Continuity procurement." He handed her a cup of iced tea, the ice already half gone. He had his own cup in both hands, as he held every cup, as if it were cold out. "You're warm. You should be in the mist."

"I'm all right."

"You're the colour of the towers." He looked at her for a second longer, and let it go.

Pilar from the second floor came out of the spray with her hair flat to her head and a cup of melted ice she wanted Daniel to see. She was about eight and had decided in April that he was hers. He crouched to her height to look into the cup, very serious, and agreed that it was water.

"Show them the light," she said. "Show them."

"It's not a show. It's a safety feature."

"Show them the safety feature."

He sighed for the audience and went to the parapet, where the node sat on its bracket in its clear wrap, and the children followed him in a clump. Elena followed the children. He opened the lid with his thumbnail and turned it so they could see the underside.

In the corner of the board, soldered on a little stalk, sat a red light, dark.

"After they took the contract," Daniel said, "I gave the box a conscience. If anything pushes more than a pump's worth down a path that isn't on the hall's map, this goes on. And it stays on. It doesn't care who you are or what you meant."

He held up one finger. "It goes out when a person comes up here and puts this on it. No relay can switch it off. It's the most honest thing I've ever built."

"It's red," said a boy from the fourth floor, accusing.

"It's red for the opposition," said Daniel. "Very patriotic. Don't touch it."

The light was dark. Elena looked at it and did the sum, a long way under the heat. Less than a pump. It hadn't seen her, because she had sized it small, without knowing there was anything to see.

She drank her tea. The children went back into the spray.

Mars came up over the harbour while the towers were still red. It was low in the south-east, past the end of the wall, brighter than anything else in that milky sky, and it did not twinkle. A man on the next roof held up his little boy to see it.

The old man from the first floor had brought his radio up, older than the building's lifts, and it had a woman on it counting down to nothing, the city's own countdown, a party with no midnight.

"And there she goes," the woman said, as Mars cleared the stacks of the cooling towers. "Look at her."

Elena turned her head toward the radio.

There she goes. It was a thing people said. Anybody could say it about anything that rose. Soria had said it in Veracruz when Elena fell off the water tank the first time, flat in the dust with the wind knocked out of her, and then laughed so hard she couldn't help her up.

"You're doing your face," Daniel said, at her elbow.

"Which face?"

"The fault face. It's a holiday."

"I'm listening to the radio."

"It's an ad for cooling units." He bumped her shoulder with his. "Come and be ugly with us."

She went. She stood in the two working nozzles with Pilar and three wet children and let it run down her neck. It was good. The water was warm almost at once and still good. The roofs around them were full, every one, white roofs dark with people, and by the stack Pilar's grandmother had set out a table with a cloth on it, and a whole fish.

She was explaining to Pilar why Mars was red, oxidised iron, ordinary rust on a world-sized scale, and she got as far as it's the same stuff as—

The roof was full of voices. The children, the radio, the next roof's horn, a hundred conversations down the street, the hiss of the nozzles, and under all of it a sound she knew.

Many voices at once, not loud, not in any language. Like a hall at the end of a long day when everybody has come in from the wet and nobody has to go out again. She stood in the mist and listened to it, and she was not tired, and she was not anywhere she had to be.

"Same stuff as what?" said Pilar.

"Same stuff as—" Elena looked at her. "The bolts. On the misting rig."

"You stopped."

"I was thinking."

"You were listening," said Pilar, who was about eight and missed nothing, and ran off to be sprayed.

Elena stood in the water and felt where it had been. Warm, at the back of her head. She put her fingers there and found only wet hair. Her heart was going slowly. She noticed she was smiling, and noticed that she didn't mind.

· · ·

By half past twelve the radio had gone to music and the children to sleep on blankets along the parapet, in the wind off the Walk. Daniel was on his back under the misting rig with a spanner, telling it what he thought of it in Portuguese.

Elena was at the parapet, by the box.

She hadn't decided to come here. She had been on her way to the stairs, to the sill, to hear the hiss again, and the box had been on the way. The towers were still red. Mars was higher now, out over the harbour, a hard orange dot with nothing near it.

Under the music, a long way off, it was raining.

She knew it couldn't be. There wasn't a cloud from the wall to the sea, and the stacks were steaming straight up. But it was rain on a flat roof, sideways off a gulf, rain on tin, rain on a water tank, and somewhere inside it a woman laughing because her little sister had fallen off something again.

"Ya sé," Elena said. She was laughing too. "Ya sé, ya sé. No me mires así." She leaned on the parapet with both arms, toward the harbour. "Ya voy. Espérame. Ya voy."

"Elena."

Daniel was behind her. She didn't know how long he had been there. He had the spanner in his hand and grease to the wrist, and he wasn't smiling.

"Who were you talking to?"

She looked at him. Then at the roof. This end of it was empty except for the two of them and the box in its wrap, and the red light of the towers lying over everything.

"Nobody," she said.

Nobody he could see. He heard more in it, and he stood with the spanner and looked at her as he had at the field on Thursday, when she ran to him across the arrivals hall singing.

"You said I'm coming," he said. "In Spanish. To the harbour."

"It's a thing people say."

"To who?"

"It's hot, Daniel. I'm tired. I was talking to myself."

He didn't believe her. She watched him not believe her, and decide, again, to leave her the room, and it cost him more than it had in May. It showed at the corner of his mouth.

"Go down and lie down," he said. "Please. Lie down, like a person. I'll finish this."

"I'll go down."

She went down.

· · ·

The bedroom was hotter than the roof. She lay on top of the sheet with the spare against her ear and the hiss filling her head, and nothing came down the channel, and nothing came, and she listened.

At one o'clock she sat up and widened it. Not the channel. The whole band, everything coming in off Mars on the calibration frequencies, raw, live, the Lantern's own traffic thick in it tonight. If anything came, from anyone, she wanted to hear it arrive, not wait for it to be sorted.

The mesh told her what that would cost, roof by roof. More than a pump. She thought of the little light on its stalk, in the dark under the lid, and knew it would go on, and it seemed a small thing, a long way off.

She took it.

The hiss got fuller. She lay back down. It was very like rain now.

Somewhere above her, a long way off, a woman's voice was raised, and then a man's. Feet on the roof. The hatch. She listened to the band.

It was twenty minutes before she heard the flat door, and his steps, and then nothing, and she opened her eyes and he was in the bedroom doorway.

He had his own handheld in one hand. The other was still black with grease to the wrist. He didn't come in.

"Pilar's grandmother calls her sister on the mesh every holiday," he said. "Out past the Line. Her sister's in a town the relays don't reach. They've done it for six years. Tonight it kept dropping. Every thirty seconds. So I opened the box."

He held up his handheld and turned it toward her. He never tilted it away.

The node's own log. One route, in red. 18:04, from the cooperative's New York node, into a Charter maintenance layer. Widened 01:06. Open.

"The light's on," he said.

She sat up. The spare was still in her hand, still hissing. She didn't put it down. He looked at it, the fish with one eye, his own handheld from his own drawer, and then at her.

"Same door," he said. "Same layer. Same as January. That's not a route, Elena. A route you open once. That's a door, and it's been there the whole time, and you told me in this flat that was all of it." He breathed out. "I asked you to ask me."

"I know."

"What's on it?"

"Mars. The band."

"What won't the Spire give you, that you need it at one in the morning off my roof?"

"It's nothing that would hurt you."

"It hurt a woman of seventy-four who wanted to tell her sister about the fish." He said it without heat. His voice didn't have any heat in it at all. "It took my contract. It's taking my roof." He looked at the handheld against her chest. "What are you listening for?"

Nobody else had asked her that. Not the review, not Mara, not Bellamy, who had asked whether there was anything else in it. She opened her mouth to tell him, and what came was the hiss, and under it rain, and under that the room in the voices, warm, with space in it for one more.

She closed her mouth.

He waited in the doorway, his hands at his sides, the grease drying on one of them.

"Ask me something," he said. "Anything. Ask me to hold the torch. Ask me to sit with you while you do it. Ask me to stay." His voice went, and he didn't laugh at it this time. "Ask me, and I'll stay. That's all. That's all it costs."

She looked at him in the doorway, with the red light of the towers coming in round the edges of the blind and lying on his face and his hands.

She had built a door in his house four years ago so that the Spire could always reach her. She had never once asked anyone to help her carry anything. She sat on the bed with the handheld at her ear, and the hiss went on, and very far in was the rain on a roof in Veracruz, and she didn't want to miss it.

Her thumb found the side of the handheld and turned it up.

Daniel nodded once, as if writing it down.

He packed in ten minutes. She heard every drawer. He took the vice and the good iron and the shirt his mother liked, and the box of mangoes from the school that he had been saving. He came back to the doorway with the bag on his shoulder.

"There's a vac south at three," he said. "I was always going to do the hall roof in Vila Verde in June. I'll do it now." He looked at her. "I've taken the building off the box. Pilar's grandmother can call her sister tomorrow. It's only you on it now."

He shifted the bag on his shoulder. "The light's still on. I didn't reset it. It's your door. You can go up and close it, and put your finger on the light, and then it'll go out. Or you can leave it on."

"Daniel."

He waited.

She didn't have the next word. She looked for it, and it wasn't there, and the hiss was.

"Eat something," he said. "Lie down."

The flat door closed. His steps went down the stairs and out, and she listened to them until she couldn't.

· · ·

At two the roofs emptied. She heard them go, building by building, the hatches and the stairwells and the voices going in, because by two the heat inside was only as bad as the heat outside, and people had work.

She went up the other way.

The roof was dark at the parapet end. The rig dripped. Somebody had left the radio, and it played to nobody. The towers had gone back to white, all but the Spire, red until dawn, and out over the harbour Mars hung where it had been, in the south now, as high as it would go, small and orange and steady.

She sat with her back to the parapet beside the box. Through the clear wrap she could see the little light in the corner of the board, on.

She could have put her finger on it. She could have gone down to the street and taken a pod to the vac terminal, and been on the platform before three, and asked.

She put the handheld to her ear, and turned it up, and listened.
