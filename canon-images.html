Image Canon

Locked. Applied to the characters, the builder, the Story Bible's Image Generation Prompts section preamble and the site. The location half governs the work from L2 onward.

The bible's layered generation format is sound. This document sets out the other half of the system: that an anchor image has a job, and that an alteration is an instruction, not a description.

It is written to be global. Two categories — characters and locations — each get one frame that must handle every case in that category without per-case invention. Adding the fortieth character or the twelfth location should require no new thinking, only a grade and a change line.

Both alteration frames have exactly four layers, always. Ⓐ is a constant, Ⓑ and Ⓓ are boilerplate selected by grade, and Ⓒ is the only authored content. So a new variant needs one decision (the grade) and one paragraph (what happened).

Rule 1 — ORIGINALS are anchors, and anchors have constraints

There is no fixed layer structure. Each anchor is a single, freshly authored prompt grounded in that subject's own canon: for characters, bloc identity, character identity, and setting content, all written together as one description; for locations, interior and exterior content written together as one description. What follows in this rule is the substantive content every anchor must carry, regardless of how the prompt that carries it is structured.

What "anchor" means

The anchor is the subject as it first appears — its earliest state in the trilogy, not "present day". A place or person may first appear in a later chapter and already be altered when they do; the anchor is still the earlier, unaltered state, inferred from canon where canon does not describe it directly.

This matters because it decides which way every alteration runs. Three consequences:

  1. An anchor may depict a state that never appears on the page. The Ice Citadel is first seen in Ch 33, already militarised; its anchor is the scientific facility it was before. Shaoyang Terraces is first seen in Ch 17, already carrying the spar; its anchor is the terraces without it.
  2. The anchor is then derived by subtraction, never by invention. Canon says what arrived — a spar, checkpoints, grafts — so removing exactly that and nothing else is safe. Anything canon does not account for must not be added.
  3. Alterations always run forward in story time. There are no backwards edits.

A subject that comes into existence during the trilogy. The anchor is the subject as it first appears — the earliest state in which it exists as itself. The Kazakh Scar is created by the Ch 17 cascade and is empty steppe before that, which is not a plate of the Kazakh Scar in any useful sense, so its anchor is the scar.

Anchor suitability

What is added is a constraint on the anchor's setting content, for one structural reason: the anchor is not merely the first image, it is the substrate every later image is cut from. Anything the anchor commits, every subsequent alteration must un-commit. An anchor is therefore deliberately plainer than a good standalone photograph would be. This is the rule that was missing, and it is the one that costs the most to discover late.

Character anchors — the pose and setting content must not:

  1. Commit the hands. No prop held, no tool in use, no hand resting on anything.
  2. Occlude the face or body. Nothing across the subject, no lying down, no turning away.
  3. Put legible text in frame. Generators render text poorly and edits smear it.
  4. Stage a moment. A state, not an incident. No mid-gesture, no mid-event.
  5. Depend on a prop for its meaning. If the reading collapses when the prop leaves, the prop belongs in a variant.

Location anchors — the interior and exterior content must not:

  1. Take a viewpoint that cannot be held. No vantage that only works for this one state — every alteration inherits this camera position permanently.
  2. Hide the signature feature. The one thing unique to this place must be unmistakable and unobstructed, because it is the invariant every alteration is required to keep.
  3. Put legible text in frame. Same reason.
  4. Depict a transient event. No weather event, no crowd, no moment. The place, not an occasion in it.
  5. Sit at an extreme focal length. A very wide or very long lens constrains every later edit to match it.

Both categories must also show the earliest, unaltered state, carry the bloc's material grammar, and light the subject with even diegetic light.

Location anchors — the interior content must also: where a window, aperture, or opening is physically plausible and doesn't contradict the location's own established mood, name it explicitly and connect it directly to the exterior content in one sentence — rather than leaving two disconnected paragraphs for the generator to reconcile on its own. Written as two separate, unconnected paragraphs, an interior anchor's exterior context does not survive into the generated image — that is the failure this prevents. Exempt by design — no window forced where one would contradict the point: Meridian Levels and Habitat Corridors (underground on an airless moon, no sky to see regardless of dressing), Broadcast Chamber (explicitly written as "airless... designed to erase the individual"), Mirror Bastion (explicitly "No windows," written as a sealed, hermetic executive chamber where that isolation is the point), and any other interior a future audit identifies as deliberately, thematically sealed.

Luna Haven's own version of a window. Where a Luna Haven interior does carry a window or aperture under this rule, it is pressurised viewport glazing — thick, load-bearing, structurally continuous with the colony's shell — never an open gap to vacuum, and it may show the lunar surface, sky, or horizon directly. This is a legitimate, generous feature of Luna Haven's architecture, not a rare exception: see World's "Luna Haven's sealed architecture and surface access." The airless-moon exemptions above (Meridian Levels, Habitat Corridors) remain valid for locations deep enough underground that glazing genuinely isn't plausible, but should not be read as Luna Haven's architecture being glazing-averse generally — a shallower or more central Luna Haven location is free to carry real surface-facing glazing as its window. If a scene ever needs a character physically outside the pressure envelope on the open lunar surface, that calls for a true EVA suit — sealed helmet, life support, thermal protection — visually unmistakable from the everyday bio-tech suit, and never rendered as Luna Haven's default indoor dress.

Naming, frames and captions

One filename shape for both categories:

<Bloc> - <Subject> - v<N>.png character <Bloc> - <Region> - <Place> - v<N>.png location

v1 is always the anchor; later numbers are alterations, as many as the arc needs. The state is not in the filename. It is looked up from state_arc[N-1], so a filename cannot contradict canon — it makes no claim about canon to contradict. The v is deliberate: a bare trailing number could collide with a place legitimately named something like "Sector 7".

Dropping a regenerated image. The filename only needs the subject and the variant:

Anders Halvorsen - v1.png Shaoyang Terraces - v3.png

Ingest rebuilds the canonical <Bloc> - [<Region> - ]<Subject> - v<N> from the roster, so a bloc or region cannot be mistyped into the wrong folder. A name with no - vN is treated as v1, and the full canonical name is accepted too. A variant number beyond the subject's arc is clamped and reported rather than filed silently. Each frame on the site prints its own drop name, so the format never has to be remembered.

Replacing the anchor. Same rule as any other drop: Anders Halvorsen - v1.png, or Anders Halvorsen.png, since no v-tail means v1. A replacement supersedes the previous file even if the extension differs.

But replacing an anchor orphans every variant edited from it — they now descend from a picture that no longer exists, which no text comparison can see. So each variant also records the anchor it came from, and re-ingesting v1 marks its descendants stale with the reason anchor replaced since. The order of work is therefore always anchor first, then its variants; doing it the other way round means editing from an image you are about to discard.

Every frame is captioned, including a lone one — "Anchor · <state>", then "Variant 2 · <state>" and onward. A reader cannot otherwise tell whether a single image is the anchor or merely the only one that exists, and the caption is what says which. Prompts carry the identical labels in the identical order, from the same helper, so a frame and the prompt that made it can never disagree.

Every state in an arc has a frame and a prompt. Until a real image exists, the slot holds a copy of the anchor under the filename the finished image will take, so the layout is honest about how many images the entry will have.

What gets pasted, and what stays behind

Anchor (generation) prompts are stored as a single continuous paragraph, with nothing to strip — the stored prompt is what the generator sees, unedited. Alteration prompts still carry their Ⓐ Ⓑ Ⓒ Ⓓ markers and layer titles in storage — they make the four-part structure legible in the bible and let the build validate the frame — but none of the markers reach the generator. The site strips them from the copy shown for pasting, because the author pastes each layer's content whole and never types the labels.

Character anchors carry no separate function layer, and none is missing for it: an abstraction like "insider-turned-exposure vector" is not visual content a generator can render, and the anchor's physical description and pose/setting content already carry everything about the character that an image can show.

Anchor length as pasted: characters mean 314 words (min 280, max 375), locations mean 348 (min 272, max 414). Length is what it takes to state a subject's own content fully — never a target, and never padding.

Rule 2 — The ALTERATION frame (both categories)

No filename. The image is attached. Naming a file wastes the most heavily-weighted position in the prompt and tells the generator nothing.

No canonical prose pasted wholesale. The bible's world-building paragraphs exist to conjure a world from nothing. Pasted whole into an edit they read as "render this world" and produce exactly the regeneration being avoided. Every line stays imperative and concrete, which is a question of register rather than length.

layerscope
ⒶDirective — edit the attached image; do not regenerateconstant, all categories
ⒷHold — what must survivespecific to this subject, drawn from its own canon text (character/location entry, or its territory's World Bible brief per Rule 8) — not generic boilerplate standing in for the real facts
ⒸChange — what the move or event doesauthored, per variant, in the same infrastructure/material-first, explicit-named-form language Rules 8–10 now use — as much detail as the change actually needs to be unambiguous
ⒹBound — what may change, and where it stopsspecific and itemized — critically, any anachronism-risk element the change itself opens up gets named explicitly here (a tier-4/Remade change touching a road gets its own "no cars, no vans, no wheeled vehicles" line, not an assumption that the reference image already prevents it)

Ⓑ and Ⓓ are a matched pair: hold and release. A grade is meaningless without both.

Explicit 2087 grounding. Whenever Ⓒ introduces content that isn't already visible in the reference photo — a new material, a new piece of infrastructure, a crowd, a technology — state plainly that it belongs to 2087, not the present day, the same explicit, scene-grounded way Rule 8/9's fresh-generation prompts do. This is not a layer and not a boilerplate line to paste in; it is a standing requirement on Ⓒ and Ⓓ's own authored content, written concretely for the scene in hand rather than as a generic "2087, not the present day" sentence.

No word budget. An edit prompt is not capped short: Nano Banana Pro performs best on one long, fully-detailed prompt whether it is generating or editing. State each layer fully rather than compressing it to fit a slot — length is whatever it takes to be unambiguous, not a target to hit or undershoot. If a layer reads as boilerplate rather than something concretely true of this subject, that's the thing to fix, not its length.

Rules 3 and 4 (character grades, location tiers) are a separate question — they define which visual invariants survive an edit, which is orthogonal to prompt length.

Rule 3 — CHARACTER grades

The axis is staging continuity: how much of the body and its relationship to the frame survives.

gradewhat it isHOLDRELEASE
1 · Restagedsame sitting, re-dressedidentity, pose, head angle, expression, framingwardrobe, background, light
2 · Recomposedthe state is postural — the body is doing something differentidentity, treatmentpose, framing, expression, background, light
3 · Transfiguredthe figure's relation to the frame changes; no longer a body standing in a placeidentity as likeness, treatmentpose, framing, spatial logic, the figure's solidity

Identity is never released at any grade. That is the floor. What varies is only how much staging travels with it.

Grade 1 is the default and will cover most cases — a defection is a change of clothes and a change of room. Grade 2 exists because holding pose across a postural change produces nonsense: Anders's dispersal has him in a medical bay, and locking his pose asked for a man supine in a workshop. Grade 3 exists for Xiàng Fēi inside the collective and Rina resolving into coherence.

Rule 4 — LOCATION tiers

The axis is fabric continuity. Recognisability does not come from little having changed — a bombed building shot from the same spot is still unmistakably that building. It comes from which invariants survive, in descending order of power: viewpoint, footprint, signature feature, material palette, light behaviour.

tierwhat it isHOLDRELEASE
1 · Markedsurface only — scorching, patched cabling, emergency lightall structure, materials, footprintsurface condition, light source
2 · Brokenstructural damageviewpoint, footprint, signature feature, palettegeometry in the damaged zone, light, surface
3 · Transfiguredaltered by a non-destructive force — drift, fracture, overgrowthviewpoint, footprint, signature featuregeometry, material nature, light, spatial logic
4 · Remaderebuilt or repurposed — new fabric on the old footprintviewpoint, footprint, signature featurematerials, surface, function, light

Viewpoint, footprint and signature feature are never released at any tier. That is the floor, exactly as identity is for characters.

Tiers are per variant, not per place. A location's state_arc behaves like a character's, so the Orbital Station can be Broken at Ch 48 and Remade at Ch 58 — two variants, two tiers, both edited from the same anchor.

A location needs no layered description of a later state. The change line is authored directly, so a place carries exactly one generation prompt — its anchor — and one alteration per later state. The character side also holds full descriptions for its variants, which is a legacy of writing complete prompts before we understood alterations are a different form; they survive as the derivation input for the diff. The fingerprint always hashes the prompt the image was actually made from — the generation prompt for v1, the alteration for everything after. Hashing the description instead meant a changed alteration went unflagged, and meant locations' variants were skipped entirely.

Two disciplines the tiers require

Tier 3 must not borrow damage vocabulary. Drift and fracture are dimensional events; written with damage words they come back as wreckage. Tier 3 draws on the bible's dimensional language — apertures, resonance, dimensional scarring, substrate — and carries a licence nothing else has: spatial logic may become ambiguous. Nothing needs to be broken for a place to be profoundly changed. Note that character grade 3 is the same idea on the other axis, and needs the same discipline: a transfigured person is not a damaged person.

Name the survivor. At tiers 2–4 the prompt must state the specific surviving feature aloud — "the twin shelving runs still legible along the left wall". It is the most effective line in a heavily-altered prompt: it gives the eye something to lock onto and the generator something it can honour. Unnecessary at tier 1, where nothing structural moved.

Recorded as a field, not re-decided per variant. Every location carries its own signature_feature in locations.json — the location equivalent of a face, decided once rather than reasoned out fresh each time a Ⓑ layer needs it, and set for all 31 locations from their own canon text. Ⓑ quotes it directly for tiers 2–4; it also doubles as Rule 7's grounding source when a territory's Level 0 shot needs to stay consistent with the locations that will sit under it.

Rule 5 — TERRITORY ESTABLISHING SHOTS

A third location category, alongside the anchor/alteration frames above. Most location interiors, even once Rule 1's window/aperture requirement is applied, can only show a partial slice of a territory's exterior. An establishing shot gives every territory one guaranteed wide/aerial exterior image that doesn't depend on any single room having a window — and is the natural home for that territory's preserved landmark, once one exists.

Structure: a single, complete, scene-grounded generation prompt — not a layered assembly. In practice (per Rule 9 below): camera and a real-world scale comparison, an explicit, non-generic "the year is 2087..." statement written for that specific territory, infrastructure and material grammar grounded in the bloc's own vocabulary, the dual register, population and crowd density, an explicit "not a commune" clause where the bloc's material grammar could otherwise read as small or rural, itemised anachronism exclusions, vegetation and fauna scaled to the bloc, and the aesthetic register — all authored fresh per territory from its own canon text, never a shared template. No interior layer: every territory shot is exterior/aerial, including Luna Haven, whose surface architecture — glazing domes, transit ports, communications spires breaking the regolith, "the iceberg," per World's "Luna Haven from the outside" — makes a true aerial exterior both plausible and the better fit for it.

Scope: one per territory — the nine Earth territories, Neo Singapore, Luna Haven, the Martian Preserve and the Turan Waste (thirteen total). The Unregulated Margins are a collecting category, not an exception to the filing: a Margins territory is filed Bloc → Territory → Site like any other and carries an establishing shot on the same terms. A further one enters by being added here and to ingest_images.py's TERRITORY_ROSTER together.

Filenames, logging and placement. <Bloc> - <Territory> - Establishing - v<N>.png, sorted by ingest_images.py into Assets/Territories/ and logged per-territory in image-log.json, alongside a reference field recording which territory or location image each character/location anchor was generated against — a specific location anchor rather than the generic territory shot wherever a character's canon setting is load-bearing to their identity. It shows on the World page for overview, and again on the Locations page beside that territory's own plates, where it does its second job as the visual reference those plates were generated against.

Rule 6 — CHARACTER ANCHORS ARE BUILT ON THEIR LOCATION

A character anchor is an edit against a real location plate, not a portrait invented beside one. Individual portraits are tight 16:9 close-ups, framed with room to breathe on either side, and the background is a shallow, defocused slice — but that slice is the one place a portrait can either belong to this world or float free of it. Written as text it floats: thirty-five prompts each describing "the suggestion of mirrored glass" produce thirty-five different rooms. Taken from pixels it belongs — the same wall, the same amber, the same depth of field the location plate already established.

The reference. Every character carries anchor_location and anchor_territory in characters.json, resolved by the Phase F linking audit. A character anchor is generated as an edit against that location's finished plate; where no location is tied, against that character's territory's finished establishing shot. A later state is edited from the character's own anchor, never from a second location — Rule 7's one-direction chain is unchanged by this.

Reframing is stated, not assumed. A location plate and a portrait are both 16:9, so there is no ratio to declare — but the prompt still says what happens: crop into the plate, bring the subject into the near field with room to breathe on either side, and hold the backdrop as the plate's own material and light thrown out of focus behind them. The backdrop is never re-described in words — naming it twice is how a generator ends up rebuilding it instead of keeping it.

What the backdrop may not do. It may not resolve into legible detail, compete with the face, or show a second person. It may not contradict the plate it came from. And it may not make the location's signature feature the subject of the frame: this is a portrait of a person standing in a place, never a picture of a place with a person in front of it.

Word order is unchanged. Rule 8's character branch still governs: identity first, the closing hard constraint last. The reference image changes what the prompt no longer has to say. It does not change the order in which the prompt says what remains.

Rule 7 — THE GENERATION HIERARCHY

Answers one question directly: what may be built from what, in which order, without ever looping back on itself.

Three kinds of node

GENERATED — an image whose FIRST build step is grounded in canon text alone, with no reference photo at all. Only one thing in the whole system is generated: the territory establishing shot (Rule 5). It exists because no reference image could do the job — a territory has no prior image to edit from. Only step 1 is generated; every rung above it edits the rung below (Rule 14), which is why the node is generated and its ladder is not.

ANCHORED — edited from exactly one reference image (Rule 2's alteration frame). Every location and every character image is anchored: variant 1 is edited from a Level 0/1 reference above it, and every later variant is edited from that same subject's own v1.

COMPOSITE — edited from more than one reference image at once, each drawn from a subject already above the compositing subject in this chain. The scene image (Rule 15) is the only composite in the system: it edits the tag's own character's finished v1 portrait together with the tag's own location's finished v1 plate — and, only when the scene itself shows territory-level content, that territory's own finished establishing shot. A composite still obeys the one-direction rule below; it simply draws from more than one already-finished node instead of one.

Three kinds of node, and every node climbs a ladder. A node is GENERATED, ANCHORED or COMPOSITE — there is no fourth kind. Within a node, the image is not made in one call: each node climbs the build-and-correct ladder of Rules 11 and 14, and it is the ladder's finished output, not any intermediate step, that the node below it may reference.

The rule that makes this terminate: an image may only be edited from a subject strictly above it in this chain — territory, then location, then character, then scene — never from a subject beside it or below it. A location is never edited from another location (only from its own v1 or its territory). A character is never edited from another character. A scene is never edited from another scene, and composites only its own tag's character and location — never a different tag's, never a different chapter's. Nothing is ever edited from a location, character or scene to produce a territory shot, and nothing is ever built from a scene: it is always a leaf. One direction, always; no back-edges; every reference chain is finite by construction, not by convention. This is also why the generation order is fixed in practice, not just in principle: a territory has to exist as a real image before its locations can be generated from it, a location or territory anchor has to exist before the character anchored to it can be, and a character's own v1 together with its tag's location's own v1 has to exist before any scene composited from them can be. Anchor first, then its variants (Rule 1) is the same rule one level up: territory first, then its locations; a location or territory before the character built from it; a character's v1 and its tag's location's v1 before any scene composited from them. One further rule rides on top of it, from Rule 11: what a node below references is always the finished image of the node above — never one of its intermediate build or correction steps.

"Vice versa" — consistency runs the other way in text, never in pixels

A territory shot is generated before its locations exist as images, so it can never visually reference them — there is nothing yet to reference. But the finished territory shot still has to not contradict what its locations will need to show. That consistency is carried entirely at the canon-text level, not the image level: when authoring a territory's Level 0 prompt, ground its landmark and material grammar in the union of its own locations' canon text and their signature_feature field (see Rule 4), so the territory image doesn't paint itself into a corner its locations can't honour once they're generated against it. Once the territory image exists, the dependency runs strictly forward from there — every location and, through it, every character is genuinely anchored to the pixels of that image, not merely written to agree with it.

Territory-pixel grounding. Once a territory shot is real, wording a location's edit against it well means treating two different kinds of continuity claim differently: named, distinctive landmarks (the Empire State Building, a specific ruin) get explicit, directional grounding language, because the generator can recognise and plausibly hold a named real-world structure across an edit; generic, unnamed structures get family-consistency language only (same materials, palette, density) rather than a false claim of exact position, because the model has no persistent 3D scene to compute true geometry from. Full detail and worked reasoning: Rule 10, location branch. A standing canon principle rides with it, no sealed locations: however sealed or windowless a location's own signature feature is, it still keeps at least one visual "window" to the wider territory — physical, holographic, or otherwise — so no location reads as fully disconnected from the world it sits inside.

What this changes about drafting order in practice

  1. Draft and generate a territory's Level 0 shot first, informed by every location that will sit under it (their signature_feature fields, their material palette) — even though none of those locations exist as images yet.
  2. Generate that territory's locations from the finished shot.
  3. Generate each character anchored to that territory — via the location Phase F has tied to them where there is one, otherwise against the territory shot directly — once the relevant finished image is real (Rule 6).

Rule 8 — PROMPT ORDER IS PRIORITY, WITHIN A SINGLE PROMPT

Not to be confused with Rule 7's drafting order above, which governs which image gets generated before which. This rule governs word order inside one prompt — where a given piece of content sits between the first word and the last.

The evidence, three independent sources agreeing:

  1. General transformer/LLM behaviour. Causal attention gives disproportionate influence to early tokens (primacy) and to the tokens nearest the point of generation, i.e. the end of the prompt (recency); content buried in the middle of a long prompt is measurably under-weighted — the "lost in the middle" effect. Nano Banana Pro (Gemini 3 Pro Image) is a frontier multimodal LLM, not a CLIP-style diffusion model, so this is the right behaviour to reason from — not classic Stable-Diffusion token-weighting folklore.
  2. Google's own published guidance for this exact model family: lead with Subject, then Action, then Setting, then Lighting/mood, then technical camera detail, then final refinements — subject first, technical spec last (developers.googleblog.com's Gemini 2.5 Flash Image guide; cloud.google.com's Nano Banana prompting guide).
  3. This project's own real-generator testing (African Belt/Nairobi Relay pilot, round 2 — Rule 9): moving material language ahead of infrastructure caused the generator to build a floating, directionless scene, because "material words read first anchor the generator's whole layout before infrastructure is even mentioned, and it improvises to reconcile them." Reversing the order fixed it. This is first-party proof, from this exact pipeline, that word order changes what the generator treats as structurally foundational versus secondary — not just a general claim borrowed from outside research.

The governing principle: whatever must never drift goes first or last; whatever is secondary or correctable goes in the middle.

  • Front-load the one thing that must survive every future variant — for a character, who they visibly are; for a place, its geometry/signature feature (Rule 1's anchor invariants).
  • Close with the hard constraint that must not be violated — an anchor's "no committed prop, no staged moment" language, an alteration's HOLD language. Recency reinforces a guardrail read last, immediately before generation.
  • Never rely on one generic clause for a risk that's specific — put an exclusion or must-not immediately next to the specific noun that creates the risk, not consolidated once somewhere else in the prompt. (Already the discipline for anachronism exclusions in Rule 8/9 of the Plan doc; extend the same logic anywhere a specific risk exists.)
  • Middle content should be secondary, not identity-critical — material palette, population, mood, and other supporting detail belong in the middle precisely because that's the part of the prompt the evidence above says gets the least attention. Nothing that must survive unaltered should live there alone.

The two categories differ.

Locations and territories: camera first. The territory-shot sequence (camera → temporal → infrastructure → material → dual register → population → itemized exclusions → aesthetic register; Rule 9 below) puts geometry/viewpoint first because for a place, geometry substantially is its identity — the thing that must never drift is the camera's relationship to the structure. Location anchors (Rule 9's location branch) follow the same sequence.

Characters: identity first, not camera. Camera is not a character's identity-critical element the way geometry is a building's — a face doesn't change with lens choice, and the single most expensive thing to lose across a 35-character cast with multiple variants per person is a consistent likeness. Per all three evidence sources above, character anchor prompts lead with subject identity. The full character-branch order lives in Rule 10 — summarised: subject identity first, then camera/composition, then temporal frame, then wardrobe, then a brief environment note, then expression/mood, then a signature detail if any, closing on the hard hands/no-prop/no-staged-moment constraint.

Alterations (Rule 2, Ⓐ Ⓑ Ⓒ Ⓓ): the close echoes the change. Ⓐ/Ⓑ front-load the invariants (camera lock, HOLD), and closing on Ⓓ leverages recency for a guardrail. Ⓓ also echoes the specific change in brief, not just a generic constraint, so the payload of the edit (Ⓒ) is reinforced at the recency-favoured close instead of standing alone in the middle. For example, not just "Damaged and fully in use," but "Damaged and fully in use — the scorching, the buckled panelling, the amber emergency light — nothing collapsed."

Corrections (Rule 11): the held invariants go first, the author's list goes last. A correction prompt front-loads the edit directive and the named holds, because those are what must survive, and closes on the author's numbered list, because that list is the whole payload of the call and recency is where a payload is safest. Nothing of substance sits between them.

Platform this rule depends on: Claude generates directly against Higgsfield's nano_banana_pro model (Google Nano Banana Pro) through the Higgsfield MCP connector (Rule 9 below, platform) — no manual upload step, and no separate style-preset control the way Leonardo's wrapper exposed one. There is nothing to set that could pull toward "glossy, hyper-saturated, illustrative" the way Leonardo's Dynamic preset could; the "photorealistic film still, grounded documentary realism" language every prompt already states is what carries that direction now, in full, because it is the only lever this platform gives it.

In the live data. All 35 character anchors in characters.json are stored in this order, and every alteration prompt closes on a Ⓓ that echoes its own change rather than a generic constraint.

Confirmed against the real generator: the territory branch. New Manhattan's establishing shot was generated from a prompt in this order and ingested — its site page shows "Tested against the generator" (derived live from the image being present, not a hand-set flag; see territory_img_files in build_site.py). The territory-shot discipline holds in practice: camera-first sequence, explicit non-generic 2087 statement, itemised anachronism exclusions at point of risk.

The character branch is not confirmed against the real generator. New Manhattan's result does not cover it: that is a confirmation of the territory branch, and the two branches order their prompts differently. Confirming this one means running a character anchor through Nano Banana Pro and reporting back. Until then the character order is applied, not validated.

Mutual exclusion — a prompt describes only its own state

A prompt must not describe a state other than its own. Nothing enforced this for the whole history of the project, and its absence let four location anchors depict post-opening states — Shaoyang Terraces depicting two at once, the Ch 17 spar and the Ch 61 shrine in one plate, which cannot both be true.

Each state records what arrives at it (state_arrivals) and the chapter it arrives in (state_chapters). Any other state's prompt containing that material is a contradiction and fails the build. The check is relational, not a keyword list — no vocabulary to maintain, it cannot go stale, and it works identically for characters and locations. state_arrivals has three uses: it builds the alteration's Ⓒ line, it dates the state, and it powers this check.

Writing an arrival phrase — three rules, each learned by getting it wrong:

  1. It must be unique to its own state. "Bio-tech suit" is Luna Haven bloc grammar shared by every resident state, so using it failed Xiàng Fēi's v3 against his v2. The build now rejects any arrival appearing in two or more of a subject's later states.
  2. Never a bare word canon also uses figuratively. "Fracture" failed Seam Edge's anchor, which reads "like the moment before a fracture" — a simile describing the anchor exactly.
  3. Never a generic noun. "Archive" matched Rina's "archive terminal room", which is a different archive entirely.

And the honest limit: this reads prompts, not pixels. A plate can still depict the spar while its corrected prompt does not mention it. The link between a corrected prompt and a corrected image is process — the plate is regenerated from the fixed prompt — and the staleness flag is what makes a broken link visible rather than silent: any image older than its own prompt is out of date and says so on Studio.

Validation — what the build checks

Because the frame is uniform, it can be enforced mechanically rather than trusted:

  1. Exactly four layers, Ⓐ Ⓑ Ⓒ Ⓓ, in order, on every alteration prompt.
  2. A declared grade or tier, from the allowed set for that category.
  3. Ⓑ and Ⓓ matching that grade's boilerplate exactly — no hand-edited holds.
  4. No filename, no layer numerals from the generation format, no master-prefix text.
  5. Word count at or near budget.
  6. Tiers 2–4 and character grades 2–3 name a survivor.
  7. Anchor constraints: variant 1 never carries an alteration prompt, and never anchors elsewhere.

Rule 9 — THE TERRITORY BUILD-STEP ORDER

Enforced in code. build_site.py's _audit_territory_prompts() checks step 7 on every build: a territory whose establishing shot is already ingested fails outright if its prompt carries no itemised exclusion at its point of risk; one not yet generated is listed as pending, and shown on Studio's territory campaign. Two advisory checks ride alongside — water in frame with no present-day vessel clause, and a named real landmark whose dominant present-day association is unaddressed.

Both build steps, from a per-territory World Bible extraction first

Step 0 — World Bible extraction (mandatory, done once per territory before any prompt is written). Pull every visual/structural fact the trilogy has already established for that specific territory — not the bloc in general — from locations.json's description/scene-arc fields, the bible's existing location-plate text, the 2087 Visual Vocabulary's per-bloc material table, and the location's own named characters' descriptions (their canon setting often carries detail the location entry doesn't). Write it up as a short, concrete brief: what it's built from and how (specific forms, not generic "biocomposite"), what its actual technology signature is (this territory's own, not a copy-pasted holographic-interface line), what scale it actually is (a compound is not a metropolis — check before assuming "metro scale, dense" fits), who is plausibly there and doing what, and exactly what marks its ruin/margin. Only after this exists does prompt-writing start — this is the step that was skipped building the original twelve, and skipping it is what produced generic, ungrounded prompts that then needed generic (and insufficient) negative clauses to compensate.

The content order, and how it divides across the two build steps. The eight items below are the order a territory's content is stated in: temporal frame explicit and early, infrastructure before material, population and the anachronism exclusions itemised at their point of risk, never a bare "no wheeled vehicles" line trusted alone. Under Rule 14 a territory's demands exceed the ceiling every time, so a territory is always built in two authored steps rather than one prompt — but the order does not change, it divides.

Build step 1 — composition. Items 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 8: camera, the year, infrastructure and geometry, the dual register, the itemised exclusions, the closing director line. Everything that fixes what is where and how big, which is expensive to add later and cheap to preserve. Text-only, no reference image.

Build step 3 — character. Items 4 and 6: material as a named built form, and the populated elements doing something specific. Authored as an edit against build step 2's corrected plate, under Rule 2's frame, with a mandatory Hold layer — step 1's geometry then survives as pixels rather than as words, which is the entire reason this step has budget to spend. A character step written as description rather than as instruction rebuilds the scene and returns a different place.

Each build step opens on its own camera line and closes on its own guardrail. Neither is half of a single prompt cut in two; each is a complete instruction that happens to be responsible for part of the frame. Between them, and after step 3, sits a correction step (Rule 11).

The eight items, in order:

  1. Cinematic lens & camera — format, lens, angle, depth of field, grounded-photographic register (explicitly not concept art/illustration).
  2. Temporal frame, stated explicitly, by year, immediately after the camera line — never skipped, never left implicit. A flat, direct sentence: the year is 2087, this many decades past climate collapse and the AI uprising, inside a deliberately rebuilt civilisation. Mandatory, never omitted. Itemized negative exclusions ("no cars," "no present-day pylon") cannot do this job alone — they help, but they are purely negative, and not one of them tells the generator what era it is rendering. A long list of things a scene isn't is not the same instruction as stating plainly what it is. Both are required: the explicit year statement here, and the itemized exclusions at their point of risk in step 7.
  3. Infrastructure, stated before any organic/biomaterial content — this territory's actual technology signature from the World Bible brief, specific and physical (an instrument bay, a mast array, a launch gantry — never a generic "holographic interfaces" line standing in for real content). Ordering organic material after infrastructure is what fixed the floating/directionless transit failure in round 2 of testing — material words read first anchor the generator's whole layout before infrastructure is even mentioned, and it improvises to reconcile them.
  4. Material, named as an explicit built form — precision-shaped/compacted/fabricated language plus a concrete named form (terraced walls, window bands, jointed timber beams), with the primitive readings it must not produce named directly (never adobe, never mud-brick, never thatch, never a windowless monolith) rather than trusted to an implied "high-tech" adjective.
  5. Dual register, stated as instruction — one short sentence naming the two things the frame must hold unsoftened toward each other, before either is shown.
  6. Populated elements, doing something specific — named actions tied to who this place's own canon says is there (a technician reading an instrument panel, a field worker checking a sensor stake), not a generic crowd description; scale honestly to what the World Bible brief actually established, not inflated to "millions" where the canon text describes a compound or station.
  7. Environmental background, with the anachronism exclusion itemized here, not left to the general canon clause — the visible ruin/margin content, and immediately beside any mention of a road, an explicit itemized list of what must never be on it: no cars, no vans, no trucks, no motorcycles, no wheeled vehicle of any kind, parked or moving. The general "this is 2087, replace 2020s objects with their equivalent" canon clause is too abstract to hold under generation load, and fails in practice. Every master prompt states the exclusion by name, at the point in the prompt where the risk actually is (next to "road," not just once at the top) — this is in addition to step 2's explicit year statement, not a replacement for it. Enforced in code, not left to memory: build_site.py's _audit_territory_prompts() reads every territories.json prompt and reports any that lacks an itemised exclusion, or that itemises one but leaves it far from the road or street the prompt itself names. A territory whose establishing shot is already ingested FAILS the audit outright; one not yet generated is listed as pending on every build and shown on Studio's territory campaign, so the backlog shrinks visibly instead of being rediscovered. Two advisory checks ride alongside it — water in frame with no present-day vessel clause, and a named real landmark whose dominant present-day association is unaddressed (the Sagrada Família and its construction cranes being the worked case). A step written down in this document is not a step that gets followed; that is the argument for the check.
  8. Director register — the closing aesthetic-anchor line (Villeneuve/Cuarón/Garland), one sentence.

Platform, stated once here rather than per-prompt: Higgsfield's nano_banana_pro model (Google Nano Banana Pro), called directly by Claude through the Higgsfield MCP connector — nothing is generated by hand or copy-pasted into a third-party UI any more. The model exposes resolution (standing setting: 2k, tracked in generator-settings.json) and aspect ratio as real parameters; it has no separate style-preset control, so the anti-glossy, documentary-realist direction Rule 8 requires is carried entirely by prompt text, not a platform toggle. A territory's first build step (Level 0, step 1) is text-only, with no reference image. Every step after it in every ladder — territory steps 2–4, and both steps of every location and character state — attaches exactly one reference image, passed as Higgsfield's image_references media: the output of the step immediately before it.

The eleven landmarks, and what may change one

The substitution protocol. A preserved landmark may be changed for exactly one reason: its territory moved, and took the landmark's real-world location with it. Not because a better landmark was thought of, not because a prompt would read more strongly with a different one, not because a brief proposes one. That is what happened to the Johnson Space Center, and it is the only thing that qualifies.

When it does happen, all four of these land in the same pass, or none of them do:

  1. the row in World's landmark table,
  2. the name in the eleven-sentence below,
  3. territories.json's preserved_landmark for that territory,
  4. a ledger entry saying which landmark left, which arrived, and why.

Enforced by _audit_landmark_lock(), which fails the build — outright, not as a pending item — if the eleven names in territories.json do not each appear in both World's tables and the sentence below, or if the count is not eleven. This is a lock, not a campaign: there is no version of it that is legitimately half-done, so there is no pending tier.

Gulf Crescent's landmark is the Burj Al Arab, retasked as the marine-lock command tower — the territory's own architecture, standing where its sea-lock command function needs it to stand.

"Yes to all the landmarks and elements the canon already has to make the places historically recognisable." All eleven confirmed preserved landmarks (the original five — Cristo Redentor, Sagrada Família, Sydney Opera House, the Burj Al Arab, Tiananmen Gate — plus six more: Empire State Building, Circum-Baikal Railway, Tian Tan Buddha, Nairobi National Park, the Merlion, the Apollo-flag vitrine at Dome Plaza) carry forward exactly as locked, no changes. They sit in Layer 2 — a landmark is part of what a place is built from, historically and physically — which is exactly where the Empire State Building already sits in Surveillance Spire's real, current, verbatim prompt above. Every territory's Layer 2 pass includes its landmark where the territory has one.

The vocabulary this bakes in

Scale and population, on the Level 0 shot only (this is where it belongs — deciding it once per territory rather than in every prompt underneath it): a mandatory clause tying the image to the territory's actual canon population share (Lunar Accord ~169M including Luna Haven's ~5M, Corporate Union ~139M, Sovereign Coalition ~67M), requiring multiple large structures at depth, a visible transit/throughfare at district scale, and crowd density appropriate to a settlement in the hundred-thousand-to-millions range — never one building standing alone.

An explicit "not a commune" clause, same location (Level 0 only), for any bloc whose material grammar could otherwise default toward small/rural — African Belt's rammed earth and repair-as-craft vocabulary is the case that surfaced it, but Lunar Accord's Earth territories generally and any future hand-crafted/salvage-coded palette need the same instruction: repair and recycling technology, deployed at metro scale, dense and multi-story, not a homestead.

A pre-generation compliance checklist — not a rule so much as a habit, because the evidence is that these rules exist and still get skipped. Before any anchor (character or location) is generated, check it explicitly against: the immaculate-people rule (no grime, no dishevelment — Kaelin's failure), the anachronism avoid-list (no handheld flat-screen devices — Amita's failure), and Rule 1's anchor constraints (no committed hands, no held prop — both Anders's and Amita's failure). This costs nothing structurally; it just needs to actually happen, every time, rather than being rules that exist in the document but not in the workflow.

Vegetation — held in the vocabulary bank deliberately, so it survives future rewrites on purpose rather than by luck.

The settled shot vocabulary

  • Population/scale figures: real-world comparison ("scaled like a district of Lagos" rather than a literal number or a size-class phrase). Mitigation: a bare city comparison risks the generator borrowing that city's actual present-day skyline. Every scale-comparison clause therefore pairs the comparison with an explicit "rendered entirely in [bloc]'s own 2087 material language — never that city's present-day architecture used as a literal template" instruction, so the comparison sets scale, not style.
  • Crowd density: varies by shot type — Level 0 establishing shots always mandate a substantial crowd at depth; Level 1 location anchors scale density to what that specific space would plausibly hold.
  • "Not a commune" clause: universal — every territory's Level 0 shot carries it, not just the organic/repair-grammar blocs, as insurance against future drift.
  • Vertical/multi-story density: mandatory everywhere except Luna Haven, which keeps its existing locked "intimate, vertical-subterranean" idiom — expressed as stacked habitat levels and corridor density rather than surface towers.
  • Vegetation: present everywhere, scaled to each bloc's material grammar, and moderate wherever people live. In Lunar Accord, African Belt and Luna Haven's living-wall idiom it is worked into the architecture as structure rather than trim — living-wall mycorrhizal substrate on the building itself, street trees, courtyard planting, roof decks — but tended and in balance with the built material: green visible everywhere in frame and dominant nowhere. Never overgrown, never a jungle, never a settlement being reclaimed. For Corporate Union and Sovereign Coalition's sterile, surveillance-coded blocs — not absent: small-scale, curated, and controlled rather than wild. Vertical hydroponic strips, engineered terrariums, manicured courtyard gardens. Author's framing: "plants and some animals should still be present in the sterile looking blocs, but at a smaller scale and in less natural setting." This isn't just softer — it's thematically sharper: a bloc built on optimisation and surveillance extending that same control to nature itself, rather than nature being simply missing from it.
  • Fauna: a hint, never a population. Birds in the air and a small number of terrestrial animals at the edge of the frame — a scatter of swifts over a roofline, a cat on a wall, a dog with the people in a plaza. Never posed, never grouped for the camera, never a flock or a herd, and never farm animals or livestock. An inhabited territory reads as a place people live, not as a farm, a zoo or a reserve, and the moment animals become a subject of the composition the clause has been over-applied. The same restraint governs the surveillance blocs' curated pollinator and songbird populations and their biometric-tagged local wildlife: present as a detail a viewer notices second, never as content the frame is arranged around.
  • The Martian Preserve is the exception to both. A landscape-scale biosphere at its historical peak, three hundred thousand years old and never human: there, vegetation and fauna are genuinely wild and may fill the frame, because wildness is what the place is rather than a register applied to it.
  • Clause reach: Level 0 plus a one-line echo in Level 1 — the full clause lives on the territory shot; each Level 1 anchor carries a short reminder line as a safety net in case the reference image is under-weighted.
  • General calibration: err generous, not gratuitous. "It's the future. It should be grand and technologically advanced without totally being oversized." This is the standing tie-breaker for every scale clause below: when in doubt, size a space up rather than down — but grandeur still has to read as purposeful and inhabited, not empty monumentalism for its own sake. A plaza sized for the crowd using it, a tower sized for what it houses — big because 2087 built it to hold real people and real function, not big as spectacle with nothing filling it.

Scientific plausibility discipline

"This is a hard science fiction. How we represent the future needs to be also scientifically plausible while daring." It is the genre commitment Front matter.md and Style Guide.md Part II already assert ("hard science-fiction trilogy"), operationalised here for the image vocabulary: biotech-composite and organic-architecture language has to carry a stated mechanism, not mood alone.

The rule: every daring 2087 technology or material named in a prompt should trace to a real, currently-known or plausibly-extrapolatable scientific or engineering principle — specific enough that the lineage from today's research to 2087's version is visible, even where the exact execution is fictional. The destination can be spectacular; the path there should be visible. Where no plausible mechanism exists, or where fixing it would mean touching already-locked canon, surface it rather than quietly reword it — Luna Haven's lighting below is the worked case.

How it lands: African Belt's "grown" architecture is grounded in real mycelium-composite/microbial-cementation research plus embedded structural lattices for genuine load-bearing height. New Manhattan's flood terraces are grounded in real storm-surge-barrier engineering (the Thames Barrier, Venice's MOSE), and Gulf Crescent's arc in the same lineage married to sea-lock engineering, with its climate domes grounded in the published projection that the Persian Gulf reaches wet-bulb temperatures past human survivability — the domes are what the physics requires, not set-dressing, and its "mirrored" surfaces reframed as energy-harvesting photovoltaic glazing rather than decorative mirror. Luna Haven's lava-tube habitats are grounded in NASA/ESA's real Marius Hills lunar-base proposals, and its living-wall material in NASA's actual myco-architecture research into fungal building materials for space habitats.

Luna Haven's light. This discipline's hardest case is whether Luna Haven's blue-green light can plausibly do the work the fiction asks of it: real bioluminescent organisms are orders of magnitude too dim to light a room, and today's best engineered plants — the fungal bioluminescence pathway, self-sustaining without added substrate, up to 100× brighter than earlier versions — still read as "soft, similar to moonlight," which reviews put far below functional illumination. Canon does not have to break for it, because the energy budget is not the barrier: matching a ~1,000-lumen streetlight through firefly luciferase would take roughly 0.3% of a fast-growing tree's stored energy. The barriers named in the literature are photon extraction and optical efficiency, surface-targeting the emission, coupling to fluorescent proteins, and sustaining metabolic flux of substrate — and Luna Haven's mycorrhizal network is a continuous flux-distribution system, so the fiction carries its own mechanism. World states it, with the calibration that keeps prose and prompts at one level: the walls give ambient working light, not sunlight and not a lamp, and anything needing real precision carries its own diegetic instrument light. All four Luna Haven location prompts pair bioluminescence with instrument glow and exclude lamps.

Rule 10 — LEVEL 1 ANCHORS, BUILT AGAINST THE FINISHED IMAGE ABOVE THEM

Scope: every location anchor (Level 1a) and character anchor (Level 1b), built as an edit against a reference image — the territory's finished Level 0 plate by default, or the finished plate of a specific Level 1a location for a character whose canon setting is genuinely load-bearing to their identity (Rule 6). The reference is always a finished image, never one of the intermediate build or correction steps that produced it.

One build step, then its correction. A single long, fully-detailed edit prompt against the reference image carries the whole subject — composition and character together, since a location or a portrait sits comfortably inside the seven-demand ceiling where a territory does not. The reference already carries palette, light, mood and register, so Level 1 never re-derives those; everything genuinely new to this one subject goes in that one build step, built from that subject's own canon text rather than generic bloc language. What the build step gets wrong is fixed in the correction step that follows it (Rule 11), not by rewriting and regenerating.

Two branches, because the two categories protect different things. For a location, geometry substantially is identity, so the camera leads. For a character the camera can move freely, but the face must never drift, so identity leads. The evidence is Rule 8's: Google's subject-first template for this model family, LLM primacy research, and this project's own round-2 infrastructure/material-ordering finding on African Belt.

Location branch:

  1. Camera/composition — this location's own viewpoint and footprint: the anchor-constraint content Rule 1 already requires — a viewpoint that can be held permanently, signature feature unobstructed.
  2. Temporal frame — the explicit "the year is 2087" statement Rule 8 requires, stated directly rather than left for the reference image to imply on its own; a short form is fine (the reference image carries most of the register), but it must still be a positive statement of the era, not only the exclusions in step 7.
  3. Subject-specific material/identity, concretely — whatever genuinely differs from the territory's general material language, not a restatement of the bloc's architecture or palette.
  4. A one-line palette echo, per the Decision log's existing "Clause reach" resolution — a short reminder, not a restatement, as a safety net if the reference image is under-weighted.
  5. Population/life, scaled honestly to the specific space and grounded in who this location's own canon says is there — a handful of technicians versus a dense market, never a generic crowd.
  6. Signature detail — the location's own named signature feature, never released at any tier.
  7. Any anachronism-risk element (a road, a device, a vehicle-adjacent space) gets its exclusion itemized at the point it's mentioned, exactly as Rule 8 requires — never left to the general canon clause alone, and never left to stand in for step 2's positive statement.

Territory-pixel grounding — how steps 1 and 3 use a real Level 0 image. Once a territory has a generated establishing shot, its location anchors open with an explicit edit directive against that image ("Edit the attached New Manhattan establishing shot…"), the same way an alteration prompt opens against its own subject's v1.

Two different kinds of continuity claim follow from this, and only one of them is something the tool can actually deliver:

  • Named, distinctive landmarks — anything the territory's own canon text names and depicts as a specific, recognisable structure (the Empire State Building, the ruined Statue of Liberty, a collapsed bridge span) — get explicit, grounded, directional continuity language: which one is visible, roughly where in frame relative to the new camera angle, and what state it's in. The generator can recognise a named real-world landmark and plausibly hold its identity and rough placement across an edit of the same reference image — this is close to what the tool is actually good at, and the wording should push as close to that real capability as it can, not settle for a vaguer claim than the tool can support.
  • Generic, unnamed structures — the rest of an unnamed tower cluster, a street grid, anonymous rooftops — get family-consistency language only: same material grammar (e.g. New Manhattan's "no two alike" faceted mirrored profiles), same palette, same rough density and silhouette register, never a claim that one specific unnamed building sits at an exact position relative to another. Nano Banana Pro has no persistent 3D scene; every generation synthesises fresh from the reference image's pixels plus text, so it cannot compute true parallax-correct geometry across two independently-composed shots — only family resemblance. Overclaiming precision here risks the exact failure the African Belt round-2 pilot already produced once (Rule 8, above): specific spatial language the reference can't actually support makes the generator improvise to reconcile an impossible ask, producing a floating, directionless scene instead of a grounded one.

The governing instinct: word the claim as close to the tool's real capability as possible — concrete and directional for what it can hold (named landmarks), general and resemblance-based for what it can't (everything else) — never either overclaiming exact geometry the tool cannot deliver, or underclaiming the recognisable continuity it actually can.

The reference supplies the world, never the camera. A territory establishing shot is a wide aerial taken from several hundred metres up. Almost no location looks at its territory from there: a street-level window sees the tower cluster at eye level with the harbour beyond and below it; a viewport partway up a tower sees a different slice again; a sealed room sees a filtered fragment on a screen. Left unstated, the generator carries the reference image's own viewpoint straight through the aperture and paints a bird's-eye plan view into a ground-floor window — the attached image is the strongest thing in the call, and an unexamined camera is the part of it that travels most easily.

So every location built against a territory declares its vantage in locations.json rather than leaving it to the prose: vantage.eye_level — where in the territory this location actually stands, and how high; vantage.aperture — what the opening is, glazed, holographic or otherwise; and vantage.shows — what is therefore visible through it, in that order, from that height. The prompt states all three and then refuses the rest explicitly: the attached image supplies material, palette, landmark identity and light, and none of its camera. _audit_vantage() fails the build on a territory-anchored location that declares no vantage, and on one whose prompt carries the reference without the refusal.

No sealed locations. Every location, even one whose own signature feature is explicitly windowless or sealed (Mirror Bastion: "no windows, deliberately"), still carries at least one visual connection to the world outside it — physical or not. A glazed opening, a holographic or material screen, an ambient data-feed panel, a crevice or gap in an otherwise sealed wall: something in frame that is legibly a window to the wider territory, even if what it shows is filtered, partial, or non-physical. A location is never allowed to read as fully disconnected from the territory it sits inside. Where that location also anchors against a territory shot per the grounding rule above, this "window" is the natural place to carry a partial, non-exhaustive glimpse of the territory's own skyline or ruin/margin register — it does not need to face any landmark head-on or show it in full; a partial angle and a small, legible sliver of the wider view (a skyline fragment, a hint of the ruined margin) is enough to keep the location honestly part of its territory rather than a set built in isolation.

Luna Haven addendum. Luna Haven has no ruin/margin register to glimpse, so its "window" under the rule above is architectural rather than territorial: pressurised viewport glazing — thick, load-bearing, structurally continuous with the colony's shell, never an open gap to vacuum — showing the lunar surface, sky, or horizon directly. This is a generous, deliberate feature of Luna Haven's own architecture (see World's "Luna Haven's sealed architecture and surface access" and Image Canon.md's matching addendum), not a rare exception, and future Luna Haven location prompts are free to use it. The existing airless-moon exemptions (Meridian Levels, Habitat Corridors) still apply where a location is genuinely too deep underground for glazing to be plausible. Separately: Luna Haven's everyday bio-tech suit is local dress, not survival gear — every indoor space is already fully pressurised — and is never confused with the true EVA suit (sealed helmet, life support, thermal protection) a character would need for an actual scene on the open lunar surface, a distinct and deliberately rare event.

Rewrite a territory's locations only after that territory's own establishing shot is generated and approved — never ahead of the real image existing, per the author's one-territory-at-a-time pace. Which territories are where in that sequence is derived, not written down: status.py and Studio's territory campaign.

Character branch:

  1. Subject identity, first. Name, bloc, the concrete physical description that must read the same across every future variant: skin tone, hair, defining features, age, expression baseline. This is the one thing an anchor exists to protect across a whole arc of alterations — it now gets the position the evidence says protects it.
  2. Camera/composition — framing and pose, the anchor-constraint content Rule 1 already requires: no committed prop, unobscured, no legible text, no staged moment.
  3. Temporal frame — the same explicit "2087" statement, short form is fine here (the reference image carries most of the register), but still a positive statement of the era, not only exclusions.
  4. Wardrobe / bloc material grammar, concretely — not a restatement of the bloc's general architecture or palette.
  5. A one-line palette echo, same as the location branch — a short reminder, not a restatement.
  6. Environment context, brief — a light hint only; secondary by design in a 16:9 portrait, never a rival to identity, never a full crowd.
  7. Expression / dual register — the mood the face itself must hold.
  8. Signature detail, if any — at most one or two specific marks, per the locked immaculate-people rule.
  9. Closing hard constraint, last. Hands/no-prop/no-staged-moment restated as the final line, leveraging recency to reinforce the guardrail immediately before generation — this is already how the live pre-Rule-8 examples on file are built and it stays exactly where it is; nothing here moves.

The reference is the tied location's finished plate (Rule 6). A character anchor opens with an edit directive against it, and step 6 shrinks accordingly: the backdrop is already in the pixels, so the prompt names what changes with no ratio to declare — subject brought into the near field with room to breathe on either side, the plate's own material and light thrown out of focus behind them — and does not re-describe the room. Naming the backdrop in words as well as attaching it is how a generator ends up rebuilding it. A character with no tied location edits against their territory's finished establishing shot instead, and everything else in the branch is identical.

The character branch's ordering is applied to all 35 stored anchors but not yet validated against the real generator. Treat it as correctly applied, not confirmed, until one reordered character anchor has been run through Nano Banana Pro and reviewed — the same real-generator confirmation Rule 9 got from the African Belt and New Manhattan pilots.

Rule 11 — THE ON-DEMAND CORRECTION STEP

Every build step is followed by a correction step. Rule 14 rations how many demands a single generation can carry; this rule handles the ones it drops. A build prompt is written before its image exists and can only predict what will land. A correction prompt is written afterwards, with the image in front of both Claude and the author, and names only what actually went wrong. Three rounds on one territory established that the failure is never that the generator could not read the prompt — it is that some demands arrive and others silently do not, and which is which cannot be known in advance. The correction step is the only place that knowledge can be used.

One call, the previous step's image attached, nothing else changed. A correction prompt opens with an edit directive against the step it corrects, holds that subject's own invariants by name — the preserved landmark for a territory, the signature_feature for a location, the identity line for a character — and then applies the approved list and nothing outside it.

At most seven corrections, and they are corrections. The demand ceiling applies to a correction step exactly as it applies to a build step, and for the same reason: the frame's budget does not grow because the prompt happens to be an edit. A correction fixes or adjusts what the previous step produced. It never introduces a new content demand that belongs in a build step — if the list is reaching for an eighth item, the eighth item is a build-step problem, and the build prompt is what should change.

Claude drafts the list; the author approves it. Claude looks at the image the previous step actually produced — not the prompt that was meant to produce it — and proposes up to seven corrections, holding the same stage boundary as any correction list (a composition correction may only touch geometry, mass, footprint, silhouette and scale; a character/detail correction is the reverse). The author confirms the list as drafted, edits it, or replaces it outright — nothing is generated as a correction until the author has approved what it will contain. This has already failed once in practice, on a location's correction: Claude drafted the list, went straight to generating it, and only showed the author the finished result afterward, spending a generation the author never had the chance to weigh in on first. The fix is not a one-time correction to remember — it is this rule, read again in full every time, in a continuing conversation or a session that has never seen this paragraph before: draft the list, show the author the image and the full list together, wait for an explicit answer, and only then call the generator. No kind is exempt — territory, location, character and scene all wait the same way, every time, including the first correction any session ever runs. Everything above the list is generated from canon and is never hand-edited; the list itself is written from looking at an image and can never be generated from canon text alone — only now Claude, not the author, does the looking and drafts first. The templates live in Project/_builder/prompt_templates.json, one per kind, rendered per subject onto the site and into the Story Bible — never copied per subject into the data, so a hold can never drift away from the canon it quotes.

Once approved, Claude generates directly. Claude calls Higgsfield's nano_banana_pro with the previous step's image as the sole reference and the approved list as the edit instruction, downloads the result, files it under its correct name, and runs ingest_images.py and a full rebuild in the same session. There is no separate ingest task and no intermediate draft left for a manual generation to catch up to later. correction-drafts.json stays live: the moment the author approves a list, Claude writes it there — the approved demands, the reference fingerprint, the date — as a record of what was just generated, not a draft waiting on a later manual pass. Studio's site page reads this file on every rebuild, so the correction prompt shown there always matches what actually happened, updating as the ladder progresses rather than sitting on a stale template. Scheduled Tasks.md's image-ingest and image-correction app tasks are retired for the same reason: both actions now happen here, live, instead of as separate manual steps.

A correction step is not fingerprinted. Its content is different on every run by design, so there is nothing stable to hash. Staleness is computed against the authored build prompts alone: image-log.json records a fingerprint for each build step's output and for the finished image of each world-state.

Rule 12 — THE DUAL ANCHOR: REBUILT FOREGROUND, RUINED BACKGROUND

Every territory establishing shot divides its canvas in two. One hyper-modern 2087 landmark, preserved and impeccably kept, held in the foreground; one recognisable pre-collapse landmark, genuinely derelict, held in the far background. Not two moods — two named structures.

This is not a new rule so much as New Manhattan's rule, generalised. That shot has always carried the Empire State Building restored among the towers and the Statue of Liberty tarnished at the harbour's edge, and it is the one territory plate that has been generated and approved. The other eleven were carrying a generic margin — "flooded suburbs," "collapsed apartment blocks" — where New Manhattan had a monument. Rule 12 is the instruction to name the monument.

How it reconciles with the preserved-landmark lock (Rule 9, "Landmarks — confirmed unchanged"). The two registers do not compete, because they are never the same structure:

  • The preserved landmark sits inside the rebuilt perimeter, kept impeccably, extending the old silhouette rather than erasing it. This is the foreground anchor.
  • The ruin anchor sits outside that perimeter, genuinely derelict — real decay, never a redesigned or softened version of it. It is a different building, in a different condition, on the other side of the line the territory drew around itself.

A territory therefore gains a ruin anchor without any preserved landmark being reopened. The one exception on record is Gulf Crescent, whose preserved landmark changed because the territory itself relocated — see the note at Rule 9's landmark paragraph.

Where it sits in the prompt. Not a ninth step. The dual anchor is a division of the canvas Rule 9 already describes, so it lives inside two existing steps: the foreground anchor with step 3 (infrastructure, stated first), the ruin anchor with step 7 (environmental background, where the itemised anachronism exclusion already goes). A prompt that names its ruin anywhere else is putting the ruin in the wrong half of the frame.

Data, not prose. territories.json carries foreground_anchor and ruin_anchor as first-class fields, each {name, prompt_cue}, so the pairing is something the build can check rather than something buried in 2,500 characters of prompt text. prompt_cue is the literal string the audit looks for.

Enforced in code. build_site.py's _audit_dual_anchors() runs on every build, on the same fail/pending split as Rule 9's step-7 audit: a territory whose establishing shot is already ingested fails outright if either field is missing or its cue is absent from the prompt; one not yet generated is listed as pending and shown on Studio's territory campaign. Silence is never compliance — an exemption has to be declared in the data to be accepted.

Three declared exemptions.

  • Martian Preserve — exempt from both halves. It is not a settlement and was never human: a landscape-scale pocket-dimension biosphere rendered at its historical peak, three hundred thousand years old. There is no 2087 infrastructure to put in front and no pre-collapse ruin to put behind, and inventing either would break the location's premise.
  • Luna Haven — foreground anchor is the surface crown (domes, transit ports, spires); the ruin half takes a substitute, because the Moon holds no pre-collapse ruin at all. The colony's own first-wave regolith-sintered founding shelters, half-buried and long abandoned outside the present shell, carry the register instead. This world's history, never a present-day agency relic — the prompt's standing exclusion on landers, rovers, flags and space-agency modules is unaffected.
  • Turan Waste — the ruin half is ordinary; the foreground half takes a substitute, because the rule's "impeccably kept" assumes an institution to keep it and the Margins have none. The substitute is the same landmark condition arrived at by a different route: a pre-collapse structure the settlements maintain themselves, aligned, clean and working, kept by hand rather than by mandate. That is not a weaker version of the rule — a landmark kept beautifully by people with no authority to keep it is the sharpest statement of the register the Margins can make, and it is why the foreground half is not simply waived here the way the Martian Preserve's is.

The failure mode this rule invites, stated so it can be watched for. Every territory carrying a recognisable ruined monument risks becoming a tic — every frame a postcard with a broken postcard behind it. Three things hold it off: the ruin stays genuinely distant, it stays genuinely unlovely, and it fails in a specific structural way (a severed deck, a hollowed tower, a leaning plinth) rather than dissolving into generic rubble. A ruin anchor that reads as picturesque has missed.

Rule 13 — THE FOUR BLOC FILTERS

A bloc's look is a constant, not something re-decided per prompt. Each filter below is the settled visual grammar for one group — surface, palette, light and the readings it must never produce — quoted into a prompt rather than paraphrased, so every territory and location under the same group cannot drift apart from one another.

The four bloc filters

Each gets one un-blendable rendering signature. These blocks are canonical text, referenced by name from a prompt rather than copy-pasted into thirteen of them — a filter edited here is edited everywhere.

FILTER: CORPORATE UNION — clinical optimisation. Materials: micro-textured matte carbon composite, self-cleaning polymer, glass panels carrying visible condensation or ocean-salt residue. Differentiated smart materials, never one uniform digital gloss. Light: clinical chiaroscuro — blindingly bright, crisp blue-white light off monolithic media tickers and biometric scanning arcs, throwing sharp, deep shadows into narrow skyscraper canyons and enclosed marine docks. Vegetation: geometric vertical bio-reactors under pulsing pink and violet UV growth light, leaves wet, flawless and uniformly optimised; curated pollinator and songbird populations, biometric-tagged as environmental compliance. Nothing wild.

FILTER: SOVEREIGN COALITION — utilitarian monolithism. Materials: brushed steel armour plating, weathered granite-polymer block, frozen iron alloy, massive mechanical gearing under winter frost or industrial grime. Immense physical weight and exposed raw engineering. Light: stark sodium utilitarianism — harsh high-contrast industrial sodium-vapour lamps casting oppressive orange-amber glare through freezing mist, heavy smog or sub-zero lake vapour; high-intensity searchlights slicing cleanly through atmospheric haze.

FILTER: LUNAR ACCORD — organic transparency. Materials: the tactile contrast between micro-smooth, razor-thin matte-white carbon-fibre skeletons and the rough, velvety skin of a living mycorrhizal network. Fabric renders as visible organic weave — hemp-cellulose textile, flowing eco-drapery. Light: ethereal bioluminescence bleeding from the living walls themselves, pulsing slowly in real biological teal and forest green. Calibrated, not re-litigated: that light is ambient working light — never sunlight, never a lamp — and anything requiring precision carries its own diegetic instrument light. See World, "Luna Haven's light." Vacuum transition, for macro iceberg surface shots: blinding direct cosmic starlight throwing ink-black shadows across surface ports, filtering down through radiation-shielded glazing to meet the warm diffused biological glow of the city below.

On the body, the Accord's filter splits. Earth-register garments carry the organic weave and nothing emissive whatsoever; Luna Haven's carry the bio-membrane and its teal seams. Enforced by _audit_wardrobe_register(); see World, Clothing grammar.

FILTER: UNREGULATED MARGINS — accretion. Materials: nothing standardised and nothing matching — salvaged sheet steel, rammed earth, timber, fired brick, sun-bleached polymer panel and cannibalised pre-collapse structure, each used honestly as itself and never dressed to resemble the others. Every structure is additive: grown outward as it was needed, no two alike, none of them designed as a whole. Repair is visible, frequent and often better than what it replaced — the mend is the craft, never the shame. Light: unmediated daylight and hand-made dark — hard open sun with no haze to soften it and long true shadows by day; after dusk only what people made, filament and salvaged strip strung between structures, cook-fire, lamp and instrument, never a lit facade and never a standard fitting. Vegetation: worked, neither curated nor wild — kitchen planting, windbreak lines and orchard rows where water reaches, bare crust where it does not, the two meeting abruptly with nothing landscaped between them. The readings it must never produce: rubble, camp, squalor, or a set dressed for poverty. Nothing here is provisional and nothing is waiting to be replaced. And no bloc iconography of any kind — the absence in frame is institutional, never an absence of care.

Rule 14 — THE DEMAND CEILING: SEVEN THINGS PER FRAME

A prompt does not fail because a generator cannot read it. Across three rounds on one territory the prompt grew from 3,174 to 6,616 characters — about 2.5% of Gemini 3 Pro Image's 65,536-token input limit — and the number of things actually rendered stayed at five to seven every time. Each round, whatever gained words landed and whatever kept its previous wording was silently dropped, at close to a one-for-one rate. The constraint is not the prompt. It is the frame.

The ceiling: no more than seven content demands in one generated image.

What counts as a demand, and what does not

A content demand is a distinct thing the frame must show or be true of: a subject, a structure, a system, a population, a material condition, a compositional share. These compete, and they are what the ceiling counts.

Frame qualities do not count — camera and lens, light and time of day, palette, aesthetic and director register, and the negative exclusions. The evidence is direct: the photographic register survived a run on the wrong style preset entirely, and the wheeled-vehicle exclusion landed in every round including the ones where most content was lost. These are cheap. They are not what is being rationed.

Every demand is stated in countable physical nouns

The ceiling is necessary, not sufficient. A demand written as abstraction loses to a demand written as objects even when both are under the ceiling. "Extending Gaudí's organic geometry" produced a generic steeple; "eighteen tapering towers in groups of four above each façade, the central tower higher than the rest, honeycombed spires crowned with ceramic fruit" produced the Sagrada Família, with nothing else in the prompt changed. Name things a person could count in the finished picture.

A real place-name is a demand you did not make

"Barcelona" and "Sagrada Família" are enormously strong tokens, and what they summon is the present-day city. Across two rounds the 2087 vocabulary lost to them both times: the prompt grew, the exclusions multiplied, and the frame still came back reading as a photograph taken this decade. Fighting a place-name with adjectives inside the same prompt is a losing trade — the name is one token and the correction is forty, and the ceiling counts them all.

Two things follow. First, name the physical form, not the place: the Sagrada Família arrived only once the prompt stopped calling it that and started counting its towers. Second, a present-day association is a demand against your budget whether you asked for it or not — a named landmark whose dominant association is scaffolding, cranes or tourists spends frame on refuting them, which is why _audit_territory_prompts() flags an unaddressed one as an advisory. Where a name cannot be avoided, the correction step is the cheaper place to fight it: one specific instruction against an image that is already wrong beats forty more words against an image that does not exist yet.

Over the ceiling, split the work — never compress it

A subject needing more than seven demands is built in more than one build step, never crammed into one. In practice that is every territory and no location or character: a wide aerial has to carry geometry, scale, a landmark, a ruin, a transit system, a population and a material era at once, while a room or a face does not.

  • A composition step — camera, geometry, scale, the landmark, the major masses, and each element's share of the frame. What is expensive to add later and cheap to preserve.
  • A character step — material, era, machinery, planting, and the detail that makes the place itself. Authored as an edit against the composition step's own corrected image, under Rule 2's frame, and its Hold layer is mandatory: the composition survives as pixels rather than as words, which is the entire reason the second build step has budget to spend. A character step written as description rather than instruction rebuilds the scene and returns a different place.

Two build steps, never three. Every edit softens something, which is why Rule 2 has a Hold layer at all; one degradation cycle is a fair price and three is not.

The ladder: every build step is followed by a correction step

A build prompt is written before its image exists. Rule 14 decides how many demands it may carry; it cannot decide which of them will arrive. So each build step is followed by a correction step (Rule 11) that fixes what actually came back, with the same ceiling of seven, and the ladders are fixed:

Territory            step 1  build     composition          text only, no reference
                     step 2  correct   composition          edits step 1
                     step 3  build     character            edits step 2
                     step 4  correct   character            edits step 3  → the finished plate

Location  (per state) step 1  build    composition + character   edits the territory's finished
                                                                 plate, or this location's own
                                                                 anchor for a later state
                      step 2  correct                            edits step 1  → the finished plate

Character (per state) step 1  build    composition + character   edits the tied location's
                                                                 finished plate (Rule 6), or this
                                                                 character's own anchor for a
                                                                 later state
                      step 2  correct                            edits step 1  → the finished plate

Only the last rung is the subject's picture. Everything a node below references — Rule 7's one-direction chain — is the finished image at the bottom of this table, never an intermediate step.

What the steps are called, and what is kept

The intermediate images are not world-states, and the v-scheme means variant equals world-state, so numbering them as variants would be a lie about canon. They carry a stepN tail instead, and the finished image of each state keeps the plain name it has always had:

<Bloc> - <Territory> - Establishing - v1 - step1     composition
<Bloc> - <Territory> - Establishing - v1 - step2     composition, corrected
<Bloc> - <Territory> - Establishing - v1 - step3     character
<Bloc> - <Territory> - Establishing - v1             the finished plate

<Bloc> - <Region> - <Place> - v<N> - step1           the build
<Bloc> - <Region> - <Place> - v<N>                   the finished plate

<Bloc> - <Name> - v<N> - step1                       the build
<Bloc> - <Name> - v<N>                               the finished portrait

Every rung is ingested; only the last is the subject's picture. Step images are filed in a Working steps/ subfolder of their own category's Assets directory rather than beside the finished images, so nothing that counts "does this subject have an image" counts a rung twice, and they are logged in image-log.json under a v<N>-step<M> key alongside the finished v<N>.

Only build steps are fingerprinted. A build step's image hashes the authored prompt it was made from; a correction step's content is different on every run by design and has nothing stable to hash. A single fingerprint across a whole ladder would make the staleness flag lie in several directions at once.

Steps are kept, not discarded. Each rung is the reference for the next: lose step 2 and the only way to re-run the character step is to re-run the composition step first. Replacing a rung also orphans everything edited from it — the same anchor-orphan rule Rule 2 states for variants, one level up, and equally invisible to a prompt fingerprint.

On the site the steps are production detail, not canon. The subject's plate is the finished image everywhere a reader sees one; a step's filename appears only inside the disclosure for the step that attaches it, where it is what the author needs in order to attach it.

Declared in the data, counted by the build

Demands are not a thing to hold in mind while writing. Each subject carries a demands list, one entry per demand, tagged with the build step it belongs to and each carrying the literal cue the audit looks for in that step's prompt text — the same mechanism Rule 12 uses for the dual anchor. _audit_demand_ceiling() fails the build on a list over seven for any one build step, and on a declared demand whose cue is absent from the prompt it belongs to. A subject with no list is reported until it has one, so the gap is visible rather than silent.

The list is also what makes a result readable: when an image comes back, each demand is marked delivered or lost in generation-failures.json. A demand that fails once is what the correction step is for. A demand that fails twice is either reworded into countable nouns or moved to the other build step — the correction step is not a place to keep asking for the same thing.

Rule 15 — THE SCENE NODE: A COMPOSITE EDIT, ONE PER LOCATION TAG

What a scene is. A full-page-width, 16:9 image placed immediately after one location tag in a manuscript-quality chapter, depicting a moment from the section that tag opens — not the location in the abstract, and not a repeat of that location's own establishing plate. A chapter with three location tags carries three scene images, one per tag; a chapter with two carries two. Display is full page width, 16:9, no parallax — distinct from the pinned hero-window treatment the site already uses for location and territory plates elsewhere.

COMPOSITE, the third kind of node (Rule 7). A scene is the only composite in the system: it is edited from more than one already-finished reference image at once, rather than the single reference every ANCHORED node uses.

The reference set.

  1. The tag's own character — their finished v1 portrait, as the primary reference and identity anchor. Never a later state (v2+): a scene is a new context for an existing identity, not an edit of a mood or wardrobe variant.
  2. The tag's own location — its finished v1 plate, as the secondary reference for place, material and light.
  3. That location's territory — its finished establishing shot, attached only when the scene itself shows territory-level content the location plate's own frame does not carry (an exterior view, a skyline, a transit approach). Held back by default; a scene entirely inside a location's own frame does not need it.

A tag naming more than one character composites each of their own finished v1 portraits in turn — the platform's own fidelity guidance holds identity reliably up to five characters in one call, well above what any tag in this trilogy names.

Prompt source. Auto-drafted from the prose of the section the tag opens — the same text a reader sees, run from this tag to the next (or to the chapter's end) — then corrected by the author against the returned image, the same build-and-correct pattern (Rule 11) every other node already follows. A scene prompt is never hand-written from nothing, and never accepted as the generator's first, uncorrected pass.

The demand ceiling still governs (Rule 14). The reference images themselves are not content demands — they are what the edit is grounded in, the same way a location's own v1 plate is not a demand of a character anchor built against it. What counts is what the prompt asks the generator to add or change on top of what the references already show: the specific action, the specific framing, anything the section's prose puts in the frame that isn't already visible in the attached images. Seven or fewer, exactly as every other build step.

The chain still terminates. A scene composites from its tag's own character v1 and location v1 (Rule 7's diagram) — never from another scene, never from a character or location state below it, and never as a reference for anything else in the hierarchy. Nothing is built from a scene; it is always a leaf.

Naming and the log. A fourth kind, scene, alongside character, location and territory in image-log.json and everywhere else a kind is named. Filename: ch<NN>-scene<N>-<slug>, where N is the tag's 1-based position in the chapter. Log key: scene:ch<NN>:tag<N>. Working steps and the finished plate follow the same Working steps/ convention Rule 9's closing note sets out for every other kind — a scene's single build step and its correction, kept, never discarded, so a re-run of the correction never has to re-run the build.

Order of work. A scene cannot be attempted before what it composites exists: its tag's character needs a finished v1, and its tag's location needs a finished v1, before any scene tied to that tag can be built — the same "anchor first" discipline Rule 7 already states one level up, extended one level further. Studio's queue reflects this without new code: a chapter's scene work simply does not appear until its tagged locations and characters are themselves finished, the same way a location's items do not appear before its territory is.

Piloted on Chapter 1, deliberately. Chapter 1 carried exactly the two-tag case this rule was written from — Surveillance Spire / Elena Vázquez, then Sydney Hub / Elowen — so it was the chapter that proved the rule rather than a chapter chosen for convenience. Its own base ladder (New Manhattan's territory shot, its two locations, its two characters) is the actual next item on the queue; the scene images themselves follow once that ladder is finished, not before.

Coverage spans all 37 drafted chapters. scenes.json carries an entry for every chapter registered in MANUSCRIPT_CHAPTERS — 107 tags in total, each grounded in the prose of the section its tag opens and checked against the manuscript's own scene headers rather than a treatment summary, the same auto-draft-then-correct pattern above. That check is what the coverage audit below formalizes: it is built to catch a tag drafted against the wrong character, or a chapter carrying more or fewer tags than its manuscript actually has.

A chapter's scene entries are drafted in the same pass as the chapter itself — house checklist. The author asked that a newly drafted chapter get its scene placeholders and prompts created automatically. The prompts themselves still take a human or Claude pass — this rule has never claimed otherwise — but the checklist item is not optional, and it now has a check behind it: build_site.py runs _audit_scene_coverage() on every build, comparing each drafted chapter's scenes.json entry count and each entry's own location and character against that chapter's actual manuscript scene headers, and Studio's own queue (via project_state.py's scene_gaps) carries the same comparison as a "next" item (entries missing) or a "fix" item (entries stale or drifted) for as long as a chapter goes without them. Nothing fails the build over a gap — a chapter absent from scenes.json simply renders no scene boxes — but the gap no longer goes unnoticed the way it did before this check existed. The house rule this enforces: when a chapter is drafted or revised, its scene entries are drafted or revised in the same turn, not left for a later pass.

The visual register

These three governed every image and lived in the Story Bible's World section, where they sat among reader-facing worldbuilding despite being production direction. They now live here only.

Overarching tone: philosophical action; wonder amid horror

Every image, scene, and character beat must simultaneously convey two opposing emotions, such as beauty and oppression, order and terror, or wonder and grief. No image, scene, or character moment should be purely one thing. This dual-register composition applies to every visual asset, per the Universal and location-crossing visual rules below.

Master cinematic references

  • Blade Runner 2049 (Denis Villeneuve) — primary reference. Monumental scale, atmospheric haze, controlled colour, the feeling of enormous systems and tiny humans, and — critically — the film's own contrast between gleaming corporate towers and the ruined, abandoned Las Vegas exterior. That internal contrast, not a single uniform grime, is the reference.
  • Arrival (Denis Villeneuve) — cold geometry meeting warm interiority; the long take that lets meaning emerge.
  • Children of Men (Alfonso Cuarón) — reference for the unrebuilt margins only: real decay, abandonment, and improvisation at the edges of a world that has otherwise moved on and rebuilt itself. Not a reference for people, who are never presented as unwashed or dishevelled.
  • Annihilation (Alex Garland) — ecological uncanny; light that behaves wrong; nature as alien intelligence.
  • High Life (Claire Denis) — deep-space materials; cold, lonely, precise; the moral weight of confinement.

Universal and location-crossing visual rules

  • No image is permitted to be purely beautiful or purely ugly. The dual-register law governs every frame.
  • All lighting must be strictly diegetic — drawn only from in-world sources such as scanners, bioluminescence, aurora, screens, industrial lamps, room lights, instruments, and natural daylight. No editorial fill lighting, no lens flare, no HUD overlays, no schematic annotations.
  • Human figures are present but not centred. Medium distance is the default; the world has weight equal to the people in it.
  • Photographic realism, not concept art. Photorealistic film stills with deep depth of field; the camera is a real camera.
  • All location and character plates are strict 16:9 cinematic landscape; a character plate keeps a tight three-quarter portrait crop within that frame, with room to breathe on either side, rather than filling it edge to edge.
  • 2087 aesthetic — three registers now, not two. Every territory in this bible contains at least two: (1) rebuilt zones — the cities, habitats, and civic cores where the story actually happens — rendered as strikingly modern 2087 architecture: sculptural, articulated, materially rich, and varied from location to location, never repetitive glass-box rows; and (2) unrebuilt or ruined zones — visible at the edges, in the background, from elevated or transitional vantage points — showing real, unglamorised structural damage, collapse, and abandonment consistent with the climate wars and AI uprising. The two registers should usually appear together within a single frame (a modern tower foregrounded against a ruined skyline slice, a repaired transit hub with a collapsed structure visible through a window) rather than as separate, un-contrasted images.
