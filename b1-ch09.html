The Preserve

Fractured Horizons · II-A. The bridge

TREATY STATION — MARS, THE DROP AND THE GLOW / Kaelin Drake — Earth 6.0 min

Petra said Kaelin was cheating. That was rich. Petra was cheating, and she was terrible at it.

"You can't play that," he said.

"I can play anything. I'm sick." She laid it on the blanket anyway, a seven on his nine, and swept the pair toward her with the edge of her hand. The deck had come on the last ship from somebody's grandmother in Durban, with a lighthouse on every back, and half the lighthouses were bent. "Mine."

"Seven's lower than nine."

"Not in my house."

"You haven't got a house. You've got a bunk and a scanner cap."

"Then not in my bunk." She grinned at him over her hand. She had given up hiding it an hour ago. "Ines. Tell him."

Ines was in the good chair at the foot of the bunk with her boots up on the rail and her eyes shut. "I'm off duty. I don't rule on hearts."

"See? She's on my side." Petra fanned the rest of her hand out on the blanket. "And I've got all the—" She looked down at them. Her thumb rubbed the corner of one, as if the word were printed there and had worn off. "Roses," she said, and frowned. "Not roses."

"Hearts."

"Hearts. I knew that." She laughed at herself, and he laughed with her, because she wanted him to.

Her hair was scraped up out of the cap's road, and the thread down her neck showed faint where the cap read it. A month ago she had cleared a jammed shoe at two hundred metres in under two minutes and marked it on her own side. Tonight it had taken her most of an hour to lose six hands to him and steal four.

He looked at his wrist. Three minutes to the sweep. He had stopped deciding to look. His arm did it every half hour on its own now, as it had once checked the tide.

Ines's overlay hung over the bunk with Petra's trace running through it like a long low coast.

"Deal," Petra said. "I'll let you win one."

He dealt. She picked up her hand and looked at it, and then she didn't look at anything.

Her head tipped, a little. On the overlay the swell began to come up, and Ines opened her eyes without moving anything else.

"Somebody's cooking," Petra said. She sounded delighted. "Can you smell it? Onions. In a big pan. Somebody's doing onions for a lot of people."

Kaelin smelled recycled air, and the antiseptic Ines measured out by the capful.

"Petra."

"It's so nice." The swell held. Her hand lay against her chest, forgotten. "They're all coming in from the wet."

Then it went down, and she blinked at him, and looked at what she was holding, and made a face. "Who dealt this rubbish?"

"You did."

"Liar."

Ines had swung her boots off the rail. She didn't say anything. She had stopped saying things about the swell a fortnight ago, when it had started lasting longer than the sweep that set it off.

Aur'k was in the hatch.

"Kaelin," Aur'k said. "A word."

· · ·

The passage outside medical was cold. At night the heat went to the greenhouse first.

"Show me the traces," Kaelin said.

Aur'k turned his whole helm toward him. "Which?"

"Every one you've closed when I walked in. Three weeks of nights, and every time I come down, the screen goes flat."

"You can't read them."

"Then read them to me."

Aur'k went still.

Kaelin waited it out. It had never yet got him anything.

"I've got field codes," he said. "I can pull the boundary logs on my own authority. I can post them to Ferrand's people with a note that says the boundary lead won't explain them, and they'll explain them for us. Foresight loves a trace. They'll have it in the protocol by Friday."

He hadn't known he was going to say it until he had. He watched it land.

"You'd do that," Aur'k said. It was nearly a question.

"Petra smelled onions just now. For a lot of people." His voice was steady. "I'll do whatever gets me a number."

The stillness went on. Down the passage a fan clicked over, and the deck ticked under their boots as it cooled.

"I can't tell you," Aur'k said at last. "I can take you down."

"Down where?"

"The Drop. Tonight. Off the record." His helm dipped a small distance, toward the side of Kaelin's head. "Take the cap off your socket before we go. They stick in the cold."

Kaelin's hand had gone there before he could stop it, to the plain cap behind his left ear. Eight years it had been on. He could feel the dust in the seam of it.

"That's a session."

"Yes."

"That's days, after. I can't lose days." He jerked his chin at the hatch. "Not now."

"Two days," said Aur'k. "Perhaps three. I won't tell you it's less."

He never had. In six years Aur'k had never once told him a thing was less than it was. He just didn't tell him the things at all.

Through the hatch, Petra was telling Ines a story that made her snort. Twenty-nine minutes to the next swell.

He went back in.

"I'm going down to the boundary," he said. It was true as far as it went. "Will you count for me?"

Ines looked at him, and at Aur'k in the hatch behind him, and back. She had a medic's look that took in the whole of you and then decided what to say about it.

"I always count for you," she said. "Go on."

Petra lifted her hand off the blanket and waggled it at him. "Bring me back anything that isn't hearts."

· · ·

They went by the galley. The Charter names were wiped from the slate, and the new Earth-night bets ran down it in three colours of marker: First 40 in the Polder: 3 May. 28 April. Never, it's April, grow up. Funmi's column still stood at the left edge, her last bet underlined twice. Kaelin kept walking.

The boundary module was the coldest room on the station, and the bluest. The monitors watched the seam where the station's rock met the Preserve's, and tonight every trace on them lay flat. On the surface board the night outside read minus ninety and falling, and the cold came through the module's outer wall so that frost had grown in the corners where the berm was thinnest. Kaelin's suit seals had gone stiff on the rack. He worked each one in his fingers before he trusted it.

The Drop's head was a lock in the floor, a steel hatch with a panel beside it, green, logging. Below the lock, the shaft held what Mars held: next to nothing. Aur'k laid his palm flat on the panel. He didn't look for where to put it.

The green went amber. LOG PAUSED, it said. BOUNDARY LEAD.

"That's for maintenance," Kaelin said.

"Tonight it's for maintenance."

Kaelin sealed his helmet. The lock pumped down around them, and the inner hatch opened on the cage. It took the two of them with room for a third, an open frame with a lamp on top and a floor of grating you could see down through, and the dark under the grating went on for a long time.

The winch let it down slowly. The shaft wall went by a metre from his visor, rough-cut rock with steel collars bolted round it every ten metres. Kaelin counted them. He couldn't have stopped himself.

Twelve. Thirteen.

"Terms," he said.

"Yours first."

Everybody said their scope before a session. He had never once had to say his.

"Ten minutes," he said. "Eyes and ears. Nothing else of mine goes in. I leave when I say."

"And when I say."

Aur'k's helm was turned to the shaft wall.

On the surface Kaelin was the one who said when. He had signed a burn on it, and left Funmi at her wheel on it.

Twenty. Twenty-one.

"And when you say," he said.

At thirty-four the rock began to show blue.

· · ·

At the foot of the Drop a second lock let them out into the cavern, and Aur'k put out his lamp, and then reached over and put out Kaelin's.

Kaelin stood in the black and looked back at the lock, because he always looked back at the way out. Its telltale was a green pinprick. He counted to sixty. He had seen the Glow once, through that lock's window in his first month, before he decided not to come down again.

At ninety the rock began to come up out of the dark.

The cavern went back and up past anything a lamp could have reached, and the rock itself was lit.

Veins ran through it the blue of a gas flame turned low, thousands of them, branching and joining across the walls and the roof, so faint that when he looked straight at one it went out, and when he looked away the whole cavern came up blue at the edges of his sight. It was the Reef Coast sky before the heat years, when there had been that many stars.

He had stopped walking. Aur'k was waiting for him.

"Here," Aur'k said.

Twenty metres in, the wall bellied out low, and the lattice ran thick in it, a knot of blue the width of two hands. Over it, pegged to the rock, stood a membrane tent no bigger than a bunk, with a heater and a bottle and a gauge. Aur'k walked to it in the dark without looking for the path.

They went in on their knees. The gauge brought the tent up to thin air, a few degrees above freezing, and held it. Kaelin unsealed his collar and lifted off his helmet, and his breath went out white in front of him and hung there, and the cold got into his ears at once.

A month ago a fingertip had touched this, through a split glove, for a second. He had wound the tape round it himself.

He took the cap off his socket. It came stiff, as Aur'k had said it would. He closed his fist on it.

Above him, Aur'k laid his palm flat on the wall.

"Zhenyth," Aur'k said, low, one word, as a man says a name at a door.

Kaelin barely heard it. He was looking at the knot of blue, and thinking of Petra smelling onions.

He leaned in and laid the side of his head against the rock.

· · ·

Nothing, for a breath. Cold stone against his ear. His own pulse.

Then the floor went out from under the world.

He wasn't falling. He could feel his knees, the grit under them, the ache already starting in his neck.

But the cavern had opened outward in every direction at once, and he was looking across a plain with no edge to it, lit from underneath by a slow light. It moved as the tide moves over a reef flat at dusk, in long low swells that took an age to cross from one side of everything to the other. There was no sky. Nothing here needed one.

He was being looked at.

He knew it all at once, with his whole body. No eyes did it. It was attention, from a great way off and from very close, and it was so old that the plain under it felt new.

And it was tired, past any word he had, tired as a wall that has held for a long time and knows how much longer it has to hold.

Far behind him, in a tent in a cavern, a hand settled on his shoulder. Aur'k's. He held on to it.

The light changed.

Rain.

It was falling on the plain, and then the plain was red ground, hills, a low grey sky, and long warm rain coming down on all of it, running off the rock in sheets and down the slopes in streams, into a sea.

There was a sea. It lay flat and dark under the rain to the end of everything, and the rain hissed on it, and the stones under the water were alive, every one, faintly, with the blue.

It was Mars. It was Mars, with rain on it.

He had stood out in the cyclones as a boy with his father's hand on his collar and watched the sea come up the street. He had never once thought of rain as a thing a world could lose.

The rain stopped. The sea drew back into the light. Into the place where it had been came a question, not in words, or in all the words at once: a small thing, turning over in front of him, a closed hand opening.

Behind it, far off and very small, came a word he knew, said low in a voice he knew, and the attention turned toward the word as a head turns at its name, and turned back to him.

What are you counting?

His lips were moving. Far off, on a cold floor, he could feel them.

"People," he said.

The attention rested on him. It was interested. It turned the word over gently, as Petra had rubbed at the corner of her hand. He gave it what he had: Funmi's column on the slate. Sami weighing what a sack of potatoes weighs at home. Forty faces stepping down from rovers. Petra under her scanner cap, smiling at a smell. He hadn't meant to give it that much. It came out of him like breath.

"There's a girl," he said. "She touched you. Through her glove. She's going. Can you—"

The light went still.

On the plain, far off, a light was being built. He watched it rise, hard and white and narrow, a needle standing up out of the slow glow like a thing driven in. It was beautiful. It was very well made.

All round the plain's edges, where there were no edges, the dark leaned in toward it, and the dark was listening. He could feel it listen. It was warm. It was so warm, and so glad, and there was so much of it.

The words came in his own voice, or in no voice:

Do not build the light. The hungry one is listening.

Then nothing pressed on him at all. The plain spread out and slowed, and he was only there, lying in it. He wasn't counting anything. He couldn't remember the last time he hadn't been counting. The swells went over him one after another, and nobody needed him to say when, and it was the best he had felt in eight years.

That was how he knew to go.

"Out," Kaelin said.

He said it again, louder, with his real mouth, into the rock. "Out. Ten minutes. Out."

Aur'k's hand closed on his shoulder and lifted him away from the wall.

· · ·

He didn't remember the cage going up. He remembered the collars, blue and then not blue, and losing count of them at nine, and starting again at nine, and losing it again. His hands had stopped being his. His helmet was back on. Aur'k must have put it there.

At the top the boundary module was grey and ordinary, its monitors flat. Kaelin got out of the cage on his knees and stayed on them. The light had come up with him. It lay across the steel floor in long slow bands that moved when his eyes moved, and when he shut them it was still there.

Aur'k stood at the panel by the Drop's head. LOG PAUSED, in amber. BOUNDARY LEAD. His palm hung a hand's width from it.

"Do I resume the log?" he asked.

If the log came back on now, it came back on a night when nobody had gone down. No sign-off. No session. Nothing to tell Ines, or command, or Earth. The onions, the needle of white, the dark leaning in to listen: none of it would have happened anywhere.

He thought about the post he could write. The rock says don't build the light. Reply when you can. He knew the reply. It would come from people who had never stood on the rock, and it would thank him.

Then it would relieve a field lead who went down the Drop off the record and came up seeing lights. After that it would close the Drop to a boundary lead who had paused its log. The sweeps would go on regardless, and there would be nobody left down here who could hear the rock say anything.

"Yes," he said.

Aur'k laid his palm on the panel, and the amber went green.

· · ·

Ines was in the good chair, and she didn't get up.

It was one in the morning, the second night. In his bunk the ceiling had turned into a slow plain. In the galley Funmi's column had gone blue at the edges and begun to swell. Medical was the only place he had found where the light had somewhere to go.

"Sit on the crate," she said. "You'll fall off anything with arms."

He sat on the crate. Petra was asleep under the cap, the deck with the lighthouses squared on the shelf above her.

"She asked for you the night you went down," Ines said. "Round three. Came up out of a swell and asked where you were."

"What did you tell her?"

"The boundary. That's what you told me." Ines kept her eyes on the overlay. "She went back under before I'd finished saying it. It's longer every time now. Last night she was under nearly the whole half hour."

The swell was coming up on the trace. He began to count it, and around eleven he lost his place, because the light had lifted off the overlay and was moving over the blanket, slow, slow.

When he came back, Ines was watching him.

"Your eyes are following things that aren't there," she said. "I've seen it on the feeds, on every keeper who ever came off a lattice. I've never seen it on you."

"I'm tired."

"You've been tired for eight years. This is new." She let it sit. She was very good at letting a thing sit. "Do I want to know where the boundary is these days?"

He could have told her. She'd have believed every word, and made it her problem too.

"No," he said.

Ines nodded slowly. She didn't ask again.

Petra stirred. Her eyes opened, wet and far off, and found him on the crate.

"Oh," she said, pleased. "It's—" Her mouth stayed open on it. Her face went puzzled, and then gentle, as if she were sorry for him. "It's you," she said. "Hello, you."

She slept again.

Kaelin sat with that. Ines reached across without a word and laid her hand flat on his knee, and left it there.

Petra had looked for his name and not found it, and said you.

Aur'k had not had to look. He had laid his palm on the rock in the dark, in a tent he walked to without a lamp, and said one word low, as a man says a name at a door. Then nothing. Cold stone. Kaelin's own pulse. And only after that, the light, and the attention turning to the word as a head turns at its name.

On the overlay, Petra's swell was going down.

Aur'k had said the name before anything spoke. He had known who would answer.

He had been down there before.
