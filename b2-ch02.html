The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

02

12 FebruaryElenaVila Verde, Daniel's cooperativeMars delay 15.8 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE, THE COOPERATIVE / Elena Vázquez — Mars 15.8 min

The node had drowned standing up. Elena took its lid off and poured yesterday's flood out onto the roof, a cup of brown water and a dead gecko. The children below cheered as if she had pulled a rabbit from a hat.

"Don't encourage them," Daniel said. He was crouched at the other end of the roof with the new board between his knees, a soldering iron in one hand and a pastel in the other. "They'll want it every drowned morning."

"They can have it every morning."

"You say that at half past six."

It was half past six, and the air was the only cool thing in Vila Verde. The street below was still wet.

The afternoon rain had filled the low street to the shins again. By morning the water was gone. It had left a brown line on every wall at the height of a child's knee, and a node on a low roof had drunk the lot.

Under the roof's edge the hall's children were rehearsing for Carnival on buckets and tank lids, eleven of them, a bloco of their own with a name nobody would tell her.

The girl with the biggest bucket kept the time and kept losing it to the boy beside her, who played faster when he was happy, and he was always happy. Elena tapped the downbeat on the gutter with her spanner. The girl found the time again. The boy lost it again, and laughed.

Her lace chimed, the small medical chime it made on its own, with no relay to talk to, the only one it still had. It showed her what it was allowed to show her now: her pulse, her temperature, and a column of sleep in pieces. 1 h 52. 0 h 40. 2 h 15. Never a night. Not since June. She pressed it off.

She wrote the node's fault in the notebook in pencil: board shorted at the water line, gecko, seal failed where the lid met the cable.

The notebook had come from the stall by the train, ruled for schoolwork, with a football player on the cover whose name everyone in Vila Verde knew but her. Everything in it was a roof. Roof numbers, faults, parts, the time each one came back up. Seven months of roofs. She liked it more than anything she had written in the Spire.

"Board," she said.

Daniel brought it over on his palm. He had a fresh burn on the side of his thumb and a crumb of pastel on his chin. She fitted the board, and seated the cable, and laid a bead of sealant round the lid as he had taught her, in one pass, not stopping. He watched her do it. He always watched her hands now, as if they were news.

"Bom," he said.

"Bom is all you ever say."

"I'm a man of few words. Most of them are bom." He licked his thumb and ate the crumb off it. "Mãe says you're the best hand on the crew."

"Mãe says I'm too thin."

"She can believe two things."

The node came up. Its little green light blinked once, steadied, and from somewhere down the hill a handheld chirped to say the roof was back. Below them the bloco reached the end of its piece all together for the first time that morning and stopped dead, astonished at itself.

Daniel's mother came out of the hall's door with two cups and held them up without a word. Coffee came up the ladder hand to hand, from the girl with the bucket to the bakery man to Daniel, who held his in both hands.

The hall's screen stood inside the open door, where it had shown the weather since before Elena came. This morning a new line was coming in at the foot of the list, passed on by the roof upstream, a few hours behind the sky. RE-ENTRY. A town's name, a window, a word: cleared.

She did not look at it long. She had the notebook open on her knee.

At the back of the notebook, upside down, so that the roofs ran one way and the other pages ran the other, there were columns she had not shown anyone. They began in November. A date. A town under a window. Where the piece had come down. Fourteen lines.

She closed the notebook on them. She drank the coffee, and kept time for the bloco with her spanner, until the sun came over the ridge and the town went in.

· · ·

The rain came at five past three, late. Elena was in a shop doorway on the low street when it arrived. She watched it walk up the valley over the reservoir, a grey wall with the light gone white behind it. Then it was on the tin roofs like gravel, and the gutters were running before she had counted ten.

The street filled from the top. Water came down it brown and fast, carrying a sandal, a plastic chair, the smell of the drains and wet brick and somebody's pastéis frying behind a shutter. In five minutes it was at her ankles. In ten it was over her boots' tops and cold inside them.

The standpipe node stood on its post at the bottom of the street, a box a metre off the ground: the water rota's oldest node, the one that warned the queue of the pump. Its light had gone out when the water reached it. She had come down to fix it. She stayed under the shop's awning instead, with the notebook on the dry side of her body, and the last page of the upside-down columns open.

In November she had called it a hobby, like keeping a list of birds. In December, the ring getting old: more pieces, more alerts, and Foresight's models getting better at them. She had built half of Foresight's models. That had been the problem with December.

Three days ago the list had brought a block from a power satellite down on Quebec. Trois-Rivières under a shelter window in an ice storm, a hundred and forty thousand people. Then cleared. The block was in the forest north of the river, where the map had nothing.

She had worked the block on Thursday, between roofs, in pencil, from the Signatories' public catalogue of everything in the ring.

The block had burned twice on its last two passes. A dead piece, its satellite torn off it in January's storm, and its thrusters had been woken and fired. Fine. Somebody owned it. Somebody could talk to it.

But a controller who only wanted it down would have burned once and hard, straight into the air, steep enough that the air stopped mattering. These burns were small and late. They had left the block coming in shallow, skimming the top of the air for most of an orbit, and it had still come down on its spot.

But the burns were wrong for anyone who could only see what she could see.

She had worked them back.

To put that block into that forest that way, the second burn had needed to know how thick the upper air would be along its last orbits, a day ahead, the day after a solar storm. And where an ice storm's wind would be at the bottom, for the pieces.

Foresight's best number for the first was a band as wide as the Atlantic. She knew, because she had drawn the band. Nobody on Earth had a better one. The Mandate's people in Beijing published theirs, and it was worse. The Signatories' own traffic office printed its error bars next to every advisory, and the error bars on Trois-Rivières had run three hundred kilometres either way along the St Lawrence.

The burn had not used a band. It had used a number. And the number had been right.

She went down the fourteen lines with her pencil. Every one the same. A town under a window. A burn that knew the weather. A piece in the dark.

Somebody was flying the ring's dead down out of the sky with a forecast better than anything on Earth.

She knew one thing that was. She had found it on a January morning with a white line standing up on a board. It had known a tug's fault three days before the tug's crew. It had waited, and two people had died under the ice because it waited.

The rain drummed on the awning. Under it, as in every rain since June, the fragment played. Bichito. You left me again. Two seconds before her cut. She had not decoded it again. She did not need to. It ran on its own, in her sister's voice and not in her sister's voice, and she had stopped being able to tell which.

She stood in the water with her hand flat on the notebook and listened behind her left ear for a long time. There was nothing there. There had been nothing there since one o'clock on the fourteenth of June. She listened anyway.

The Lantern was cut. Both ends. She had cut it herself, and a man on Mars had cut the other end on her count, and Haven had eleven names on the back of a rota to prove it.

So somebody had built another.

"Elena!" A woman at a window across the street, a bucket in her hand. "A bomba?"

The standpipe's light was still out. The queue would form at five whether it knew the pump was on or not, and it had not known for twenty minutes, because she had been standing under an awning doing sums.

She put the notebook inside her shirt and waded out to it. The fault was easy. A cable joint below the box had been wrapped by somebody in a hurry and had let the rain in. She cut it out and made it again, taped it and bagged the tape, elbows tucked in to keep the joint under the box's lip. The light came back green.

The woman at the window lifted her bucket to her, like a toast.

The water was going down. A woman came out from behind the pastéis shutter with a broom and began to push the street downhill, and a man joined her with another, and then the children, with their hands.

Elena tore a page from the roof end of the notebook, wrote on it in square capitals, and put it in her pocket where the rain couldn't reach it. Then she waded home to tell Daniel, because she had promised she would ask.

· · ·

The sky over the reservoir cleared to a hot orange after the rain and stayed that way far longer than any sunset should. Up on the hall's roof the new panels caught it and turned the colour of a stove.

Daniel was lying on his back on the warm panels with his eyes shut and a cup on his chest. She sat down next to him. She did not lie down. She had found that if she lay down she listened.

She had called Theo's clinic at noon on his old handheld, because it was Sunday. The nurse had said what she said every Sunday: he had had a bath, his trace was level, she had read him the football. His father was in custody in the Polder, waiting for a trial date, and called nobody.

"How was he?"

"Level."

"Bom." He opened his eyes. "You're not tired."

"I'm never tired after the rain."

"You're never tired. That's the problem." He sat up and looked at her, and she knew he had seen the page in her pocket before she took it out. Her pockets had always told him things.

She took it out.

"I've been keeping a list," she said. "Since November."

She told him all of it, from November to the number that had been right, and who had drawn the band it made a fool of.

He listened as he listened to a box that wouldn't come up, his head a little on one side, his hands still.

"Someone's flying them," he said.

"With a forecast nobody on Earth can make."

"Except the one you cut."

"Except that." She smoothed the page on her knee. "It came from Mars, Daniel. It knew things a day early, three days early. That's what it is. That's what it does. And somebody is using it again, and not telling anyone. Fourteen pieces, and every one of them down where the map has nothing."

"Who's up there? In the forest."

"I don't know. Nobody does. That's the list."

He said nothing for a while. Down in the street a fan started, and somebody's forró came up off a roof a little behind its singer.

"What do you need?" he said.

She turned the page round so he could read it. RIDGE MAST. THE DISH.

"I need to hear the Mars band," she said. "If somebody's talking to Mars, I'll know the shape of it. Can the ridge dish do it?"

He was already working it out. She watched it go across his face as a route went across the map.

"The ring relays drop the band to the ground station at the rectenna field," he said slowly. "We're under the edge of that beam. Just. The dish could follow one across, if it had a feed for the band. It's built for weather." He rubbed the burn on his thumb. "The old man's box has one. He built it to steal the football."

"Then we can."

"That's a Charter relay."

"Yes."

"Listening to a Charter relay from a free town."

"Yes."

"You're under inquiry."

"Yes."

He drank from his cup, slowly, and set it down on the panel as he set down everything that could spill. The sky had gone from red to the colour of a bruise.

"Last time you didn't tell me," he said.

"I know."

"This is telling me."

"This is asking you."

"It's not me you're asking." He stood, and held out his hand, and she took it, because she had learned that too. "What does it cost them?"

She told him the truth, because there was nothing else she wanted to tell him any more. "If the network office finds a Charter band on the hall's mesh, they'll come. The hall's already lost one contract over me. They could take the licence for the masts. All of them."

"Then the hall decides," Daniel said, "not me," and went down the ladder.

· · ·

There were nine of them at the rota table by nine o'clock, the night's people, the ones who did the masts and the pump and the radio.

Daniel, the bakery man, two women from the north crew. An old man who had soldered the first box in Vila Verde and still kept the iron. Others whose names Elena knew from the map.

The fan in the doorway turned the air without cooling it. Daniel's mother sat under the map with the marker in her lap.

Elena told it again, shorter, in her Portuguese. It had got better in the mornings and worse at night. Nobody stopped her. When she had finished the bakery man asked what a relay band was, and the old man told him, more or less, and the bakery man said that was what he'd thought.

"They took our contract," said one of the women from the north crew, "for a door she built."

"Yes," Elena said.

"And now she wants another."

"Not a door. An ear."

"Orelha." The woman made a face. "Ears get cut off."

Daniel's mother had not spoken. She was looking at Elena as she had looked at her in June, all the way down, the face and the hands and the boots still wet to the ankle.

"Por quê?" she said.

Elena had a reason ready. It was a good one. It had fourteen lines in it, and a hundred and forty thousand people in Quebec, and the blanks on the map. Under the fan's noise the two seconds played again, in the voice that was her sister's and not, and for one breath the reason she had ready was a different one. She did not give them that one.

"Porque ninguém sabe," she said. "Not the towns under the windows. Not the places where the pieces come down. Nobody is told."

Nobody spoke.

"Como a gente," the old man said. And then, to the table, "We were dark before they called it dark."

Daniel's mother uncapped the marker. She stood and went to the rota. In the empty space at the bottom, under the households and the water, she wrote in thick marker capitals: MASTRO DO MORRO — PARABÓLICA — NOITES. Then the cooperative's name beside it. That was how the hall signed things. She taped the corner down where it had always curled.

"Pronto," she said. "Agora vai dormir."

Elena did not go to sleep.

· · ·

The ridge mast stood above the last houses, where the scrub began, on a concrete foot somebody had poured in the dry years. The dish was older than the mast, white once, now the grey of an old tooth. It had shown the hall its weather for twenty years.

Daniel had put the old man's feed on it at midnight, swearing at the bolts in Portuguese. He turned it with a hand crank and his shoulder. Elena read each pass off the catalogue in pencil by the light of his handheld.

Two relays had gone over since one o'clock, ten minutes each, and given them hiss. The third came up at two minutes to five, a moving point of light over the western ridge, among the others. The ring's stations ran across the dark like a thread of beads. The relay was one of them. The dish followed it, creaking.

The node on the mast had its own green light, and beside it sat a bigger box the old man had built in the eighties. Its screen came up full of hiss. She knew this hiss. She had known it on every night of a year. She sat on the concrete with the handheld on her knees and her boots still damp and watched it go by.

Daniel sat beside her. After a while his head went down onto her shoulder, and she let it.

At 05:04 the node flagged a shape.

A pulse, small and clean, on the calibration band where nothing lived. Then the one before it, which she had let scroll past. She did not need to time them. She had built frames like these by hand in March. She had sent her own questions to Mars in them. She had read the specification of this one forty times, on a review that voted seven ayes.

The Lantern's frame. Its exact shape, to the bit.

The relay went down in the east, toward the reservoir, and the hiss closed over it, and where it had gone the sky was beginning to grey.
