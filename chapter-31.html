The last airlock cycled, and Elena Vázquez stepped out of ten days of transit into a light she had no professional category for.

She had crossed three transit jurisdictions and two orbital stations to reach it, each with its own scanners, its own idea of what a body was for and what it owed the machines that read it, and every one of them had lit her the same way — the flat white audit-glare of systems built by people who thought of light as a form of measurement. She knew that light. She had specified it herself, in another life, for a tower on the far side of a drowned coastline. She had ordered rooms to be lit so that nothing in them could hide.

This was not that. The reception chamber at the head of the Luna Haven tether was lit from its walls, and its walls were alive. Blue-green, the colour of something growing in deep water, the mycorrhizal network threaded through the grown composite and glowing with its own slow chemistry, breathing light the way a forest breathes air — not aimed at her, not reading her, simply present, the way weather is present. There were no windows in the chamber itself — a deep transit space built for arrival rather than view, the airless lunar dark and the scarred blue planet she could no longer legally return to both sealed somewhere far above her, past domes and glazing she had not been walked through yet. The light did not come from a window here. It came from the living wall, and it fell on her ink-stained hands and the practical dark bun coming loose after ten days without a proper mirror, and it did not measure her at all.

She had expected, at the end, to feel like an arrival. She felt like evidence that had finally reached the right archive.

No one applauded. She had not wanted anyone to, and had spent part of the last orbital transfer bracing for it anyway — the small horror of being thanked, of standing in a warm room while people told her she was brave, when what she had actually been was slow, and complicit, and three years late. The reception held two people. She was grateful for both the smallness of the number and the plainness of their faces.

"Dr Vázquez." The nearer one, mid-thirties, a diagnostic stylus held the way other people hold a pen they have forgotten they are holding, subtle neural lace tracing one temple — the same augment Elena carried, she saw, worn without the reflexive concealment she had never quite unlearned. Fingertips scarred in fine pale lines, as though they had been used, repeatedly, as an interface. "I'm Jīn. Jīn Wú. I run the portal work down in the Levels." A short pause, precise, the pause of someone who chose their words the way they'd choose a calibration. "We're not going to thank you for coming. Léa thought you might find it easier if we skipped that part."

Something in Elena's chest, held rigid since the eleventh working tier of the Surveillance Spire, moved very slightly. "She thought right."

"She usually does." Jīn didn't smile so much as let the possibility of one exist. "There's a briefing waiting for you. I'll be honest about what it is, because you've had ten days of people being careful with you and I don't think it helped. It isn't a welcome. It's four hours of everything we know, and we need what's in your head against it more than you need to sleep, and I'm sorry about that, and it's still true." They tilted their head toward the corridor throat where the wall-light deepened toward the colony's heart. "You built the machine that's been hunting us. That makes you the only person in the room who knows how it thinks. We'd be fools to let you rest first."

It was, Elena thought, the most honest thing anyone had said to her in three years. She picked up her single case — everything she had allowed herself, which was almost nothing, because almost nothing in a life of accumulation had belonged to the person who was leaving — and followed them down into the light.

The Meridian Levels sat beneath the southern habitats like a held breath, and Elena read in them for four hours without once looking up.

They had given her a console at the edge of the deep-interface chamber, a curved slate of grown material warm to the touch, and a security key that opened everything — everything, with no compartment walls, no need-to-know gradients, no silent audit flag waiting to bloom in the logs the moment she reached past her clearance. She noticed the absence of those walls the way you notice the absence of a sound you've lived inside so long you stopped hearing it. In the Corporate Union, access was a topography of permissions, and the shape of what you could not see told you more than what you could. Here the archive simply opened. It unsettled her more than a locked door would have. She had spent her career learning to read institutions by their secrets, and she did not yet know how to read one that kept none.

So she read the data instead, which she did know how to do.

The cascade first — Chapter after chapter of instrument logs from the event five months gone, the thing that had partially distributed a human consciousness across rift nodes and left the Levels' sensors recording physics that had no permission to exist. Then the deep-chamber records, the ones that had come up the relay and reframed the entire programme as a lock that somebody had been picking for decades without knowing it was a lock. Then the comparisons — the Suppression Archive material out of the Pearl Delta laid alongside the Lunar Accord feeds, the pattern leaping out of the overlay the way a face leaps out of noise once you've been told it's there.

And under all of it, threading every dataset, the thing only she could supply: the Corporate Union's own hand. She could see it. Where the Luna Haven researchers saw anomalies, she saw decisions — the specific institutional fingerprints of the surveillance architecture she had designed, the tells of a system built to watch, the difference between data that had merely been collected and data that had been collected by something, for a purpose, by people whose logic she had spent a decade fluent in. She annotated as she read. Not conclusions — she had left the business of manufacturing conclusions behind in a black-lacquer tower — but translations. This is not noise. This is a masked query. This is what it costs them to run it. This is the room the order came out of, and here is how that room decides.

At the third hour Jīn brought her water without being asked and stood a moment reading over her shoulder, and Elena — surfacing from the overlay, blinking at a term that had recurred through the deep-chamber records and snagged on something in her memory — asked the question that had been forming under the work.

"Why 'Meridian'?" She kept her eyes on the display. "I've been reading your files for three hours and mine for three years, and I never once asked. In New Manhattan there was an archive I broke into — pre-Uprising, 2060s, buried and scheduled for deletion. We all called them the Meridian files. Everyone did, the leakers, the analysts, everyone. But I've just been through the whole fossil, line by line, and the word isn't in it. Not once. They never called it that. So where did the name come from, if not from them?"

Jīn was quiet for long enough that she looked up. There was something careful moving behind their eyes, the pleasure of a precise person handed a precise question.

"The place was named first," they said. "Long before any of this." They gestured, one scarred hand taking in the chamber, the grown walls, the deep. "The colony runs on a single organism. The mycorrhizal spine — one continuous living thing feeding nutrient and signal through every southern habitat, the line the whole place hangs its life on. When they cut these levels into the substrate to study it — the better part of a decade back, before I ever moved into the division — a grown colony's idiom called that central life-line what a grown colony would call it. Its meridian. The Levels have been the Meridian Levels since the division was founded, years before the interface ever manifested. There was no dimensional anything down here then. Just soil, and signal, and people doing unglamorous foundational work on the thing that keeps us breathing."

Elena understood before they finished, and felt the shape of it settle with the particular cold satisfaction of a wrong assumption correcting itself. "And when the interface showed up—"

"It showed up here. So it inherited the room's name. First people said it colloquially — the thing in the Meridian Levels, the Meridian thing, the Meridian. Then they said it formally, and by the time anyone thought to ask whether that was a good name it was the only name there was." Jīn's almost-smile returned. "The name flowed one direction. Out of the soil, into the science."

"And backward onto my fossil." She heard her own voice go flat with the recognition. "Nobody in the Corporate Union ever named that archive. We named it. Afterward. Your word came off this wall and travelled four hundred thousand kilometres and landed on a pre-Uprising weapons file that never once used it, and we called it the Meridian files as if the name had always been there, as if there were some shared source to explain." She almost laughed. "There's no coincidence to explain. There's no conspiracy of naming. There's just a room on Luna Haven that got named after a fungus, and everything downstream of that borrowed the word without knowing it was borrowing."

"One name," Jīn said. "One origin. It's the least mysterious fact in the entire programme, and it's the one people build the most mystery out of." They set the water down at her elbow. "Corporate Union analysts included."

"Corporate Union analysts especially," Elena said, and went back to the data, and for the first time since she had walked out of the Spire into the cold, felt something that was not quite hope but was at least the raw material hope was made from — the clean relief of a thing that turned out to be simpler and truer than the machine she'd left behind would ever have allowed it to be.

They convened in the fourth hour, and Elena understood almost at once that she was watching something being built that had no right to exist and might, precisely for that reason, be the only structure that could hold.

Léa Fournier came down to the interface chamber herself rather than joining by relay from the Dome Plaza, and Elena — who had spent a career learning to price people quickly, because in the Corporate Union the mispricing of a person was how you died — found she could not price her at all. Mid-forties, grey-green eyes lined with the particular patience of someone who read for a living, hair worn loose in a place where Elena's own reflex was still to pin everything back and give the scanners nothing. Léa's authority did not announce itself. It simply meant that when she spoke, the room reorganised around the quality of her attention, and Elena, who had spent years in rooms organised around rank, felt the difference like a change in air pressure.

"Before we start," Léa said, "I want to be clear about what this is and what it isn't, because the difference is the whole point." She looked around the chamber — at Jīn, at Elena, at the two relay presences holding on the grown displays. "This is not an institution. It has no charter, no budget, no legal standing, no enforcement. If any one of us breaks faith with it, there is nothing any of the others can do except stop trusting them, which is not a sanction, it's a grief. I want everyone to hold that clearly from the first minute, because we are about to behave as though we trust each other with the most dangerous knowledge on two worlds, and that trust is the only load-bearing element in the entire structure. There is no other."

On the first display, a man in his mid-thirties inclined his head — intense dark eyes carrying both focus and a low, held weight, a short professional cut, the slight forward stoop of a body that had spent too many years over an instrument bench. Xiàng Fēi, relaying in from the Baikal Complex across a link so hardened and indirect that his image carried the faint stutter of data that had crossed half the planet to arrive, routed to look, in every log it passed through, like something harmless. He had not defected. He was still inside the Sovereign Coalition's ice, running calibrations by day for people who would weaponise the results, and reaching out at night through a channel that would end him if it were ever read.

"I want it on record that I'm agreeing to this from inside a facility that would arrest me for agreeing to it," Xiàng said. His voice arrived a half-beat late, the dilation of distance and encryption. "Not for sympathy. So that no one here ever mistakes what I can and can't do. I can give you what crosses my bench. I can't give you anything that requires me to be free, because I'm not." A pause. "And when they militarise us — not if — my bench becomes a weapon, and I'll have to decide whether the next thing I hand you is worth the last thing I ever hand anyone. I'd like you all to know I've already decided. It just hasn't come due."

"It's noted," Léa said, and let it sit with the weight it deserved, and did not thank him either, and Elena began to understand that the refusal to thank was not coldness here but a kind of respect — a colony that had learned not to spend the people who gave it things.

The second presence was harder to read, because the link itself was strange. Kaelin Drayke sat somewhere red — Elena could see rust-coloured wood behind him, the impossible cobalt of a Martian sky through some aperture, dust worked so deep into the creases of his hands that it had stopped being dirt and become part of him — and his words arrived not merely late but time-shifted, skewed by the Preserve's dilation differential so that the rhythm of the exchange never quite closed, every answer of his surfacing from a slightly different now than the question that had prompted it. An evolutionary biologist, Jīn had told her, the one who read the Preserve's ecology the way the rest of them read instruments. He spoke rarely and only when he had something.

"The animals moved before your fleets did," Kaelin said, out of his offset now. "I want that in the shared record from the start, because it's the kind of thing that gets left out for sounding unscientific and then turns out to have been the earliest datum we had. Whatever's coming, the Basin registered it as an ecological event before any of us registered it as a political one. Empathy is infrastructure out here. Load-bearing. When it gets stressed, things migrate. They've been migrating." His offset gaze found the camera. "Share that with the rest. It's the whole reason I'm agreeing to share anything."

"Which brings us to the principle," Léa said.

It was simple, and Elena, hearing it, felt every reflex a decade of the Corporate Union had built into her rise up to call it insane.

Everything they discovered about the Meridian would be shared simultaneously. Across the whole group. At once. No bloc, no institution, no individual would ever hold a finding in reserve, would ever sit on a result to price it, to trade it, to convert knowledge into leverage. The instant any one of them understood something true, all of them would. There would be no proprietary access to the nature of reality.

"That's not how anyone protects anything," Elena heard herself say. She had not meant to speak. "I'm sorry. I know what this is, and I know why it's beautiful, and I'm the one person here who has to say it out loud: everything I was ever taught says the group that shares everything simultaneously is the group that gets everything taken simultaneously. Compartmentalisation isn't cruelty. It's how you survive the breach, because there's always a breach. You keep the walls so that when one room falls the others still stand." She looked at Léa. "I designed those walls. I'm telling you they work."

"They do work," Léa agreed, without heat. "They worked so well that a programme to weaponise consciousness ran for decades inside your bloc and no single person ever held enough of it to be responsible for it. That's what walls buy. Deniability, distributed until it's nobody's. We're not building walls because we've seen exactly what a perfectly compartmentalised institution becomes, and we would rather be fragile than become that." She held Elena's gaze, and there was no argument in it, only the offer of a different physics. "You're right that we'll be more exposed. We've chosen it. A finding that everyone holds can't be the private weapon of anyone who holds it — including us. Especially us. The principle isn't naïve about betrayal, Dr Vázquez. It's designed for a world where the thing we're most afraid of becoming is the thing we're fighting."

Elena was quiet. On his display Xiàng was watching her with the recognition of one insider for another — a man who also knew exactly how the walls worked, and had decided the same thing, from the inside of a facility that would kill him for it.

"Then I'm in it," she said. "For the record, since we're putting things on the record. I spent ten years making sure the Corporate Union never had to trust anyone. I'd like to spend whatever I have left finding out if the opposite of that is survivable." She managed, barely, something adjacent to dryness. "I'll assume you'll all tell me if it isn't. Simultaneously."

That earned the first sound of anything like ease the chamber had produced — Jīn's short exhale, Kaelin's offset huff arriving strangely a moment after, even a flicker at the corner of Xiàng's tired mouth.

There remained the matter of how. A shared encrypted environment could be built and would be, Jīn said, and it would be good, and it would still be a network, and any network could in principle be read — and the moment their principle proved itself in something that mattered, the Corporate Union and the Sovereign Coalition would both want very badly to read it. For the findings that could not survive interception, they needed a channel that was not a network at all.

They had one. Elena had already half-understood it from the logs, and understood the rest now as Jīn explained it, quiet and careful, the way you explain a person you love and will not spend.

Mara Ekeberg could carry it.

Not electronically. Through the bridge-state — through the dimensional substrate itself, the same thin place she had learned to hold coherent in the mycorrhizal dark, the same door she had opened wider than anyone toward the far side ten days ago and come back from still herself. She could route the most sensitive of their shared knowledge along a path that touched no conventional infrastructure at any point, that no scanner watched because no scanner knew the medium existed, that could not be intercepted for the simple and total reason that there was nothing between the endpoints to tap. Information passed hand to hand through the structure of reality, by a human being who had become, at a cost no one on this colony named lightly, a place two worlds could meet.

"She consented to this specifically," Jīn said, before Elena could ask, because it was the first thing anyone who understood the colony would need to know. "Freely, and knowing exactly what it asks, and with the standing to refuse it that outranks every fleet in the sky. She is not our channel. She's a person who has agreed, for now, to carry things. The difference matters more than anything else in this room, and if we ever stop being able to feel the difference, we've already lost the thing we're trying to protect."

Elena looked at the deep-interface chamber around her — the grown walls breathing their unmeasuring light, the two relay presences holding open across ice and red dust and the awful courtesy of distance, the impossible principle hanging in the middle of it all with nothing to hold it up but five people's word. Overhead, forty thousand kilometres up and ten days into a silence that should already have broken, two fleets sat in strike geometry and did not fire, and no one in the chamber knew why the blow had not fallen, only that a held breath was not the same as safety.

It was the most fragile thing she had ever been part of. She had come from the most durable institution on Earth, and it had been a machine for making harm nobody's fault, and she looked at this breakable, unbacked, unenforceable web of trust and thought that its fragility was not a flaw it would have to overcome. It was the load-bearing truth about it. A thing that could be broken by any one of them keeping faith with none of them was, for exactly that reason, the only kind of thing worth building on the far side of the wall she had spent her life pouring.

"All right," she said. "Where do you want me to start."
