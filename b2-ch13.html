The Narrow Gate · Chapter 13

The great wave

The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

13

10 AprilRachelthe BatteryMars delay 18.9 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY / Rachel Iverson — Mars 18.9 min

"Thursday," said Joaquín. "Number four Bowling Green. Thursday noon, dry to the skirting."

He had the grease pencil and the flood glass, and his coat was back on, the camel one, because it was April again and eleven degrees in the street and nobody could stop him. Down the glass, where the hurricane pool would go in June, he had drawn the Polder's south tip in six lines and the lobbies that had gone under on the second, and against each one a day and a name.

"Number four has a basement," said Moss, from chair three.

"Number four has a man with a fan. I've met him."

"That's not a bet," said the night lead. "That's inside information."

"I'm from the Shallows. All our information comes through the wall." He held out the pencil to Rachel. "Jefa. One lobby. You're the only one on this floor who's never paid me."

"I've never lost."

"Not yet, you haven't."

She took the pencil. The pump under the floor hummed up through her shoes. Eight days since the wave had come over the ferry steps, and the south tip still smelled of wet plaster and diesel from the pumps, and the floor had started laughing again. She liked it laughing. A floor that laughed was a floor that had slept.

She wrote IVERSON — NO. 9 STATE ST — SUNDAY and gave him back the pencil.

"Sunday," Joaquín said. "Bold."

Behind her the map wall changed colour.

· · ·

Nothing flashed. A tag on the ring plot went from white to amber, and then the amber ran, as ink runs on wet paper, into a string of tags along one orbit, thirty, forty, more, strung out like a fishing line with the hooks still on it.

"Santos," said the night lead. She had stopped writing her own clocks. "Power satellite. São Paulo's. Took a piece of the spine on the second. We had it logged as damaged and holding." She touched her screen. "It isn't holding."

"Read it."

"Breakup. The sail's in pieces and the pieces are coming down together. Saturday the fourteenth. We get them one orbit out, eleven thirty-two our time, and we steer till twelve thirty. They land in the last four minutes. Every track crosses the metropolis." A pause. "All of it. Twenty-two million."

"Their own satellite."

"Biggest of their sisters. Went up when the dams stopped turning." The night lead put the rectenna field up in a corner of the wall, kilometres of fenced mesh east of the city, shimmering in somebody's afternoon. "It's coming home."

Rachel was at the rail. Down the long wall the forecast filled in São Paulo, address by address, a city she had never seen, laid over its hills. A fine red brush, district by district. Fifty-eight minutes of it.

"The floor will want a name for it," said Moss.

"The floor's got one," said Joaquín, and he had: he had already written it at the top of the glass, above the lobbies. THE WAVE. "Like the second. Only it's theirs."

"Who do we need?"

"Everybody," said the night lead. "Four chairs and the reader, fifty-eight minutes. The big piece is the transmitter core. Its thrusters are dead. Nobody turns that without the reader at the head of the row."

Rachel took the pencil off its string. Four days. She wrote SP 14 on the inside of her wrist, under the tide times, and turned to the rota on the night lead's second screen to put her reader at the head of Saturday.

Saturday's head of the row was blank.

AUR'K — READER — LEAVE, 13–14 APRIL. FILED 02:51.

· · ·

He came at twenty to eleven, at the end of his block, and stood in her doorway because her room had no space for him to stand anywhere else.

Her room was hot. The Gate's racks were behind her wall, and they ran warm all year, whatever the street was doing. With Aur'k in the doorway it was hotter. His plating ticked as it cooled, a sound like a kettle left on a stove, and the air by the door had the warm metal smell of a car in a car park in August.

"Sit down," she said. "Before you melt my floor."

"Your chair has a weight limit. I've read it." He set his feet wide on the boards instead. "You've read the rota."

His override key hung on its chain at his chest plate, the only one on the floor, a plain steel blade the length of a finger, ticking against the plating when he vented heat.

Grit came off his shoulders in a fine red skin onto her boards, Mars still working its way out of the seams after five months. It always did when he ran hot. Nobody on the floor swept it up any more. They stepped round it, as you step round a sleeping dog.

"Leave. Thirteenth and fourteenth."

"Elena Vázquez has put my name on a ship." He said it in his reading voice, every word set down square. "The window opens on the fourteenth. Six in the morning, the launch field outside São Paulo. She wants a guide to the rock. I've been asked by name, and I've been priced. Usually I'm only priced."

"And you said yes."

"I haven't said anything. I've filed leave. That keeps a door open."

She had known since the request came across her desk that Elena would go to Mars. She had signed the clearance so Elena would. She had not thought about who would take her down into the Preserve.

"You said you'd stay. Through the Fall."

"I did. If I go, I break it. I'm telling you first." He turned his helm toward her, the whole of it, as he did with people he was listening to. "I'd like to ask someone at the rock whether what answers on your floor after I've gone home is me."

The fan on her camp bed turned its face across them and away.

"It's residual," she said.

It was the audit's word. She had never given it to him. She heard herself give it to him now, and so did he: his helm stayed on her, and went still.

"You have a word for it," he said. "You say it as if it were a place. I'd like to know who lives there."

"I need you on the fourteenth."

"I know. I've seen the plot."

"I can hold your clearance. Contract work in an emergency. I've done it before."

"Then hold it. And on the fourteenth you'll have a reader who's been held." He said it without heat. That was worse. "I'm a contractor, Director. You told me so, on paper. If I withdraw, I withdraw."

She looked at him, and thought of the forty-one calls he had printed and brought to this room in March, and looked at the clock over the door. Four days. Twenty-two million. A rule she had written herself: forty minutes on, twenty out.

There was no lever in the room. She went and bought one.

"Window desk," she said, to her slate. "Signatories. On my name."

The desk answered in two seconds, flat and polite, as the Signatories always were when they were about to charge you. "Window desk."

"Mars transit, the fourteenth, the field outside São Paulo. Move it two days."

"The sixteenth is sold, Director. Freight lift to the ring."

"Move the freight."

"The freight goes to the back of the list. The next free day for it is the twelfth of May." A pause while somebody somewhere did a sum. "That's a month of cargo. And two days later, Mars is further off. The burn is longer. The fuel contract with Luna Haven was for the fourteenth. It will have to be sold again."

"Do it. Iverson. Civic director. Log the cost to me."

"Reason for the change, Director?"

"Civic."

"Logged."

She did not tell the desk what was coming down on the field's city on Saturday. The Gate's forecasts weren't the Signatories' to read. The ship would have gone up six hours before it, through a sky full of a breaking sail, and now it wouldn't, and nobody at the desk would ever know they owed her for that.

She put the slate down on the desk, face down.

"Fly the fourteenth," she said. "Then take the night vac south. The ship waits for you."

Aur'k was still a long time. On the floor beyond the glass the afternoon block was coupling, and the medic said coupled four times, and the harbour pressed green against the windows.

"You've bought me," he said.

"Two days of you."

"Most people who buy me get a receipt." His helm tipped, a degree. "I'll fly the fourteenth."

He went. The boards he had stood on were warm when she crossed them, and she stood on them a moment anyway.

· · ·

Haven called at four.

It came through on the director's line as Luna Haven, Commons, and when she put it on the screen it was not the fuel office. It was a tall woman in a crawler crew's dust-grey coverall with the speaker's band on her sleeve, standing in front of a skylight with the Earth in it. Cropped white-blonde hair. A long Haven face, made for a sixth of this weight.

Behind her a crawler shift was coming in off the plain through the Commons, low voices and boots on rock and the hiss of a lock, nobody hurrying, everybody grey to the knee.

"Director Iverson." The Moon put its small pause in, between Rachel's breath and the answer, short enough to forget and too long to talk over. "Halla Strand. I speak for the crawler crews."

"Speaker."

"You've moved a ship. Two days later, the burn's longer. The longer burn is ours." Halla spoke low, as everyone on Haven did, as if the air in the room had a price on it, and it did. "We mine it a few tonnes a year, out of the ground, a breath at a time. So I'm going to tell you what it costs, once, and then the Commons will vote, and that'll be that."

"Tell me."

The pause. "Your Charter names Haven's eleven. In public. On its own record. Our dead, as its dead. That's the price of the sixteenth."

Rachel had the list. Haven's thirty, in pencil, in the order Mara had read them off the wall on go-live night, and the eleven dead among them marked since July. One of the eleven was STRAND. She had not known, until this second, whose.

"I'll read them," she said. "Tonight. On the Battery's record, under my name. Every one."

Halla didn't answer at once, and it wasn't the Moon.

"You held a door," she said.

"I did."

"For the cut. Nine minutes. The whole of Earth heard about it." Halla lifted one hand, and counted on it, finger by finger, without looking down. "Eskil was on the wall at one o'clock. Mara had every hand off it at a minute to. He didn't hear her. He was further in than the others. He always went further. He liked the warm."

Her hand closed. "So no. Not you. The Charter that built the thing he was on. Its name."

"The Charter won't."

"I know it won't." The pause, and nothing in it. "Mara will stand up for your ship in the Commons on Sunday. She'll say Haven doesn't sell air to people and hold back fuel from people, and she'll be right, and she'll lose. I've counted."

"Count again."

Halla almost smiled. "I always do. Full air, Director."

On Haven it meant all was well. She said it as you'd close a door.

The screen went to the Commons' crest and then to nothing.

Out on the floor Joaquín was in chair four with his coat over his knees and his clock steady at eleven. On the glass above him somebody had written THE WAVE bigger, and under it, in another hand, SAT.

Rachel sat in her hot room in her shirtsleeves and picked up a pencil, and found she had nowhere on her wrist to put it that wasn't already somebody.

· · ·

The plan came at twenty to eight.

The night lead brought it on paper, because Rachel had asked for every plan on paper since the Malé audit, and laid it on the desk and stood back. Outside on the floor the evening block was flying a storm off Bermuda that would come to nothing, and somebody was laughing at the coffee machine.

"Fifty-eight minutes," said the night lead. "Thirty-one pieces worth flying. The panels burn up on their own. Pods on everything that still answers. That's most of it."

"And the core."

"The core's the problem. Dead thrusters, four hundred tonnes, coming straight down the track over the city. Nothing on it to push with." She turned the page. "So the plan puts a skyhook in its way."

On the second sheet, the skyhook. A long line turning slowly against the Earth, its lower tip dipping to the top of the air.

"Majuro," said the night lead. "Cargo tether. Nobody aboard. The hub's automatic since the solar watch. The plan phases its spin so the lower arm meets the core at twelve twenty, over the sea. The tip's slower than the core. The hit takes the speed off it and it comes down short, in the sea off Santos." A small dry sound in her throat. "The port they named it for."

"And the arm."

"Cut loose. It comes down along Majuro's own track."

Rachel turned to the map wall through the glass. São Paulo filled the middle of it now, hills under a fine red mesh of addresses, the river roads, the towers along the ridges, the rectenna field to the east, and through all of it the thirty-one tracks coming in off the sea from the south-east in a sheaf, each one turned aside at its end by a small white tick.

Thirty ticks. Every one of them a street that would still be there on Sunday. The thirty-first ended short, out over the water off Santos. And from the coast inland, where nothing had been falling that morning, the night lead had laid a new line: Majuro's cut arm, three hundred kilometres of thin red, coming down along its track up the escarpment and across the grey.

It was grey there. The fine red brush that had filled São Paulo address by address that morning stopped short of the city's south edge as if somebody had told it to, and past it the hills were blank. No addresses. No names. Two great reservoirs lay in the grey like spilled water, and the red line crossed the eastern one at the dam.

"What's under it?"

"Billings works. Dam, pump stations, the intake." The night lead didn't look at her paper. "Last full reservoir the south side's got. The districts round it are dark. The map doesn't hold them."

"Who's under it?"

"The map doesn't hold them, Director."

"Drown it," Rachel said, to the grey.

She looked at the straight edge. She had stood under a line like that on the map wall fourteen months ago, with the Rockaways blank past it, and said That's wrong, and sent nine crews past it in a van. There was no van to São Paulo.

She thought of a keeper's key on a water controller in a town the relays didn't serve. ROCHA, D. His family on a hill on the city's east side. She found the hill by the reservoir she knew was under it: grey too, and forty kilometres east of the red line. Saved, on this plan. By distance.

And on Saturday, because of a call she had made at eleven that morning, Elena Vázquez would be on that hill as well, beside Daniel, and not six hours up and gone. Rachel had bought that with the rest.

"Reader," she said. "Forty minutes on."

"He'll be at forty at twelve twelve. By then he's flown the thirty and set the catch. The core's at twelve twenty. After forty, the catch only has to be held."

"Keep the rule."

The night lead waited.

"And if it needs more than holding?"

"Then the line holds it." The night lead said it to the page. "On his model. As it flew Malé. It's in the spec. Nobody ever needed it till you asked."

Rachel picked up the pencil and wrote it in the margin of the second sheet, in her own hand, so that nobody could say afterward they'd misread it. Reader relieved 12:12. Line on reader model to close. She put the pencil down.

Stop it answering in my voice when I've gone, he had said, in this room, with the pages in his hand. She had written it anyway, under his rule. The rule was hers.

The night lead looked at it for a while. Then she went back out to her clocks.

The director's earpiece was on her left, where it always was. At 19:52 it opened.

"It saves more than it costs."

A child's voice, close and hoarse, as if she had been shouting somewhere else for a long time. Rachel didn't move.

"You said. In the gym. To the lady with the clipboard." The child sounded pleased with herself, as children are when they remember a hard word right. "You take the ones you can reach. It saves more than it costs."

For the length of a breath the boards under her chair were varnish, and she was on her knees on them. Wall bars. The list in her hand, the dead radio against her knee, a teacher with a clipboard asking why the little ones by the wall weren't going first, the water already in the car park, and Rachel at twenty-three saying it. Those words. She had believed them. She had said them to get the woman to stop asking.

The earpiece closed. On the slate, under the channel: 19:52. CARRIER. NO TRAFFIC.

Nine years old, Noor had been, and had never heard a word like costs said like that in her life.

It was true, though. Rachel looked at the numbers on the first sheet until they stopped being numbers and became a city on hills, twenty-two million, lit, and then at the grey on the second. It was true. That was the worst of it. It always sent one that was true.

Before anything else she opened the Battery's record on her slate and read Haven's eleven into it, alone in her hot room, family name first, in Mara's order, with nobody asking. Strand, Eskil. It went into the log under her name, where Halla had said it was worth nothing.

Then she took a pen out of the drawer, not the pencil, and signed the plan under her own line. R. Iverson, civic director. Then she pushed back her cuff, and under SP 14, small, where the tide times went, she wrote BILLINGS, and pulled the cuff down over all of them.
