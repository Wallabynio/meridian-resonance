MARTIAN PRESERVE — COBALT BASIN, EMPATHY FLATS / Alia Solís — Preserve, six hours before the session · Luna Haven −70m

The whorls were wrong, and Alia Solís had known it for eleven weeks before she could prove it.

She knelt where the migration corridor bent north around the old stand, in the reinforced growth Kaelin had taught her to read years ago — the tight spiral grain the ground cover threw up at every junction node, the way the wood of a tree thickens around a knot. Healthy whorls wound clockwise for four turns and then released. She had six hundred and forty photographs that said so, and she had gone through all of them twice this month, because she had needed to be certain she was not seeing what she wanted to see.

These released at three turns. Some at two. The spirals came apart early and finished loose, and the moss growing out of them ran a shade paler than the mat around it — not dying, nothing so legible as dying, but simply less committed to the shape it had been making for longer than anyone had been here to watch it, the way a hand that has written the same signature for forty years will one day begin leaving off the last letter without ever deciding to.

She photographed the whorl from four angles and wrote the measurement down before she let herself feel anything about it.

"Alia." Kaelin's voice came off the field link flat and close, the way it did when he had not looked up from something in a while. "Read me the release count on the northern junctions."

"Three. Three. Two. Three." She moved down the line on her knees, camera up. "Two at the gap — the one nearest the Verge. Worst I've measured anywhere."

A pause on the link, the particular one she had learned to leave alone. Kaelin thought in silences and resented being talked through them, and she had stopped mistaking that for coldness somewhere in their second season.

"Send me the timestamps," he said. "Not the dates you wrote in the record — the actual observation times, down to the hour."

She sent them, and went on working the line while he did whatever he did. The Basin's long light climbed the way it always climbed, ochre haze softening the rust-red scrub without ever quite letting go of it, the cobalt above flattening toward the horizon into that colour she still had no word for after four years of trying. A grazer worked the scrub two hundred metres off and did not care about any of it.

"Alia." Different voice. Not flat now.

"Go."

"Every degraded junction you've logged postdates a rift event on the other side by between nine and fourteen hours of our time. Every one. Eleven matched pairs, no unmatched observations in either direction." A sound she could not identify and then understood was Kaelin putting his hand down on something. "I have been looking for a season-long signal. It is not a season-long signal. It is eleven separate afternoons."

Alia sat back on her heels in the moss.

She had known it would be true, which was not remotely the same as hearing it be true, and what arrived in her when it did was not vindication but the specific nausea of a suspicion that has finished being a suspicion: eleven afternoons, each one a room somewhere on the other side of the boundary where people in good tailoring had authorised a test and signed the authorisation and gone home to their evening, and eleven corresponding mornings out here where the ground cover had begun the shape it had made without fail since before there was anyone on either world to name it, and had come up three turns short, and had gone on growing anyway.

"It isn't damage," she said, when she could. "Kaelin, listen to me before you write this up, because you will want to call it damage and it is not. Nothing here is injured. The mat is growing, the grazers are feeding, the substrate under all of it is exactly as old and as stable as it was the day I got here, and if you brought me a stranger and stood them in this moss they would tell you it was the healthiest ground they had ever knelt in. It is keeping less carefully."

The link held a long moment.

"Say that again," Kaelin said. "Slower. I want the words."

"It is keeping less carefully. The shape is a relationship. Something out there stopped holding up its end eleven times, and the ground has begun — not deciding, I'm not saying deciding — the ground has begun finishing the gesture early." She pressed her palm flat into the moss beside the loose whorl, which told her nothing, and did it anyway. "You spend six years learning what a thing does when it trusts the conditions. Then one season you watch it stop bothering."

"That goes in exactly as you said it, with the photographs and the timestamps." A breath. "Alia — the small ones came back up the scree at first light. All of them at once. I want your eyes before the session, because I cannot decide whether I am looking at an animal or at somebody."

She was up and moving before she had chosen to be.

They were on the western scree, where the Basin's floor broke into rust-dark talus: nine of them, perhaps a metre tall, the forelimbs longer than the hind and terminating in something honest enough to call a hand. She had seen them perhaps thirty times in four years, always as a footnote in her own record — small arboreal-analogue fauna, western margins, no observed interaction with corridor — and never once doing what they were doing now, which was standing in an arrangement: not clustered and not herded but arranged, a loose open crescent facing down-slope toward the corridor's northern bend, each animal a deliberate distance from the two beside it, and above the whole crescent that soft trailing distortion in the air she had spent four years photographing over the backs of migrating megafauna and had never once seen over anything else.

"Tell me what you see," Kaelin said. He was twenty metres off with his notebook out and his hand not moving on it.

"Nine individuals, open crescent, oriented on the junction that measured two." She raised the camera and made herself narrate rather than conclude. "Spacing even to within about a third of a metre. Shimmer over the whole group, not over individuals. They are not feeding, they are not grooming, they are not watching us, and I have been standing here ninety seconds and not one of them has changed position."

"Now the other thing."

"I don't want to say the other thing."

"Say it anyway. That's what I'm out here for."

Alia lowered the camera. "The crescent is pointed at the worst junction on the northern run. Not the nearest. The worst. And I did not know it was the worst until an hour ago."

Kaelin wrote for a long time. When he finished he looked up, and she saw on his face the expression she had seen there twice before — the one that arrived when the wrong name for something finally became unbearable to him.

"Full documentation," he said. "Spacing, orientation, duration, the correlation with the junction survey, every photograph you have. And then I want to stop."

"Stop where?"

"At the edge of what we measured." He closed the notebook on his thumb. "Because the next word after directed changes what these animals are owed, and I am not going to be the man who says it on the strength of one morning, and I am not going to be the man who fails to write the morning down either. It costs a colony nothing to wait for that sentence. It costs these nine everything if we get it wrong in either direction."

The crescent held for another eleven minutes. Then, without any signal Alia could photograph, all nine turned at once and went back up the scree, and the shimmer over them thinned into ordinary air, and the Basin's light went on not quite finishing its arrival.

MARTIAN PRESERVE — COUNCIL GROVE, ANCIENT GROVE / Kythara — Preserve, one hour before the session · Luna Haven −12m

Under the canopy the light arrived in its own filtered order, and Kythara composed a sentence.

This was not, for it, an ordinary act. It had spoken to the humans many times, but all of it had been speech between parties already standing in the same weather, and what it prepared now had to survive being carried through a person, into a room, past several habits of hearing, and arrive still meaning what it had meant when it left. The difficulty was not vocabulary. The difficulty was that the humans possessed only two shapes for a sentence of this sort, and the true shape was neither.

The first shape was a threat — stop, or we will close the door — and every word of it was wrong, because there was no we: the Guardians did not hold the mechanism, had never held it in 3.8 billion years of standing beside it, and would be sealed inside the result exactly as thoroughly as the herds and the water and the nine small animals currently arranging themselves on a scree slope forty kilometres west. A demand implied a hand that could be stayed. There was no hand.

The second shape was a forecast, a thing said about the weather — regrettable, impersonal, nobody's business to answer — and that was wrong in the other direction, because the weather did not have eleven afternoons in it, and eleven rooms had made this, and every one of those rooms had emptied at the end of the day and let its occupants go home, and a sentence that let them go on going home was a lie of a different grammar from the first one but a lie all the same.

What remained was the accurate shape, which no human language Kythara had studied in 3.8 billion years of watching this species make languages had ever comfortably held: this is what happens, and you are doing it, and no one is deciding.

It had the words, and had had them for some time. It turned them over once more under the canopy and found them adequate — and found also that it was carrying a second thing into the session, and that the second thing would not be said.

Vaera's finding was eleven hours old by the Grove's reckoning, the Grove had convened out of order and had not yet finished, and the presence at the northern run remained small and growing and understood by nobody well enough to be described to people who would immediately act on the description. Kythara had argued for the delay itself, and still held the argument to be correct: a report that smuggled a guess into a fact would set every human decision downstream of it on ground that no one had verified, and the humans in that room were four days from doing something irreversible with anything at all that they were handed.

Correct, and still a withholding. Kythara noted both, because noting both was its function, and because the records worth keeping had always been the ones that declined to tidy themselves.

Mara Ekeberg, it said, across the thin place, into the colony where the woman it needed was already sitting down. I am ready when you are. Take what you need first. I have waited a long time and I can wait through your breathing.

LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, MERIDIAN LEVELS / Mara Ekeberg — session 1h08m · Preserve +5h50m

Mara went out to meet it the way she had learned to go anywhere now, which was by stopping trying to go.

The body sat cross-legged on the interface floor with its hands open on its knees and Jīn a careful arm's length to the left, and she could feel all of that the way she felt a coat. Courier work she had done a dozen times, and carrying was easy, because a carried thing sat still and let itself be carried; this was the other kind, the kind where another mind spoke in the near part of her while the far part held the channel open across the thin place, and it had a cost she had measured once and did not intend to describe to the room.

"Whenever you're ready," Jīn said aloud, to the body.

Across the chamber the grown displays held the rest of them: Léa with her hands folded, Elena two seats along with a pen she had not yet used, Xiàng and Eddy shoulder to shoulder over a resonance map neither was looking at, Zara standing because Zara always stood. And on the two Preserve relays — arriving with that faint stutter the boundary put into everything — Kaelin with basin dust to the second knuckle, Alia beside him, camera still in her hand.

Kaelin spoke for nineteen minutes and used no adjective Mara could later remember: the junction survey first, six seasons of it, then the eleven matched pairs, then Alia's photographs of a spiral that released at three turns instead of four. Alia said her sentence about keeping less carefully, and said it exactly the way she had evidently said it once already that morning, because it came out of her with the smoothness of something that had been true before it was spoken. Elena's pen started moving at that. Xiàng put his hand flat on the table and left it there.

"To be precise about the limits," Kaelin finished, "we have correlation across eleven events, no counterexamples in either direction, and no mechanism. I can tell you what stopped. I cannot tell you what it costs."

"Noted," Léa said. "Thank you for the limit."

And then the room turned toward the interface floor, and Mara felt the turn arrive as a change in the field around her, and opened the channel the rest of the way.

Kythara came through with a courtesy so specific it stopped the breath in the distant body.

It did not push. It waited at the edge of her the way it had waited at the edge of a treeline for four years before it let Alia see it standing there, and when she made the room in herself it stepped into that room and no further, occupying exactly the space it had been offered and not one node beyond, and Mara held the door and did not flinch it shut and began, in the sliver of her still sitting on the floor, to give the arriving structure words.

The cost is not injury, Kythara said, and the room heard it in Mara's voice, arriving a half-beat behind her own decision to speak. Your researcher is correct and I will not soften him. Nothing here is being destroyed; the ground beneath this grove has been stable for 3.8 billion years and will be stable for as long again after every one of you and every institution any of you has ever answered to has finished. What is being spent is not the place. It is the agreement between the place and everything that reaches into it.

"Define the agreement," Elena said.

It answers the state of the attention pointed at it. A pause that was not hesitation. Not the equipment. Not the command. The attention. That answering is not a courtesy the ground extends and could withdraw if it chose — it is the structure the ground is made of, the way the arch of a bridge is not a decision the bridge makes each morning, and a structure can be worn. Eleven times in one of your seasons, something reached in wearing the shape of asking and meaning compulsion; each time the ground did what it is built to do, which is to answer; and each time what came back to it was not what the shape had promised. Your botanist has photographed the consequence. A spiral that releases early is a body that has stopped expecting the gesture to be completed.

"And if it stops expecting entirely," Léa said.

Then it closes.

Mara felt Jīn's attention sharpen beside the body and take up more of the thread's weight without being asked.

I want the shape of this understood before anyone in that room decides what to do with it, Kythara said. I am not threatening you. There is no one here to threaten you. My kind do not hold this mechanism and have never held it, and when it closes we are inside it — sealed with the grove and the herds and the water, intact and unreachable and of no further use to anyone, including ourselves. I cannot stay it. I cannot slow it. I am not permitted to negotiate on behalf of a fever.

"Then you're describing weather," said Zara, carefully. "And nobody answers weather."

No. Weather has no cause in a room. The structure arriving through Mara sharpened, and she rendered it as exactly as she could and marked it internally as hers, the rendering, not the thing. This has eleven causes and each of them was authorised by a person who went home afterwards. It is automatic and it is not natural. Those are different words and your languages keep wanting to collapse them. What closes the ground is not a judgement about what you deserve. It is the same thing a body does when an inflammation will not stop: not anger — closure, no more negotiable than fever, and no more interested in your reasons.

"Where's the threshold," Elena said.

I do not know.

"You've watched it for—"

I have watched it for longer than your species has had a word for watching, and I do not know, and I will not estimate for you, because an estimate is a number you would build a schedule against. Something in the structure that Mara had no word for at all and rendered, finally, as the flattest sentence she could find. An immune function does not publish its triggers. That is not a gap in my record. That is the property that makes it work.

The room held still.

"The two blocs doing the reaching aren't in this meeting," Eddy said at last. "Do we tell them?"

"We'll spend the rest of tonight on that question, and I'd like nobody to answer it in the first minute." Léa's voice did not rise. "I'll say only this much now, so we start from the honest place. I have read four of Rachel Iverson's internal papers. If we hand her a warning she cannot verify, delivered by a voice she cannot subpoena, through a woman she would describe in a memorandum as an unreliable interface — she will not hear an ecology. She will hear an asset declaring a capability. And then the thing we told her in order to stop her becomes the reason she accelerates."

Nobody argued. Mara, spread wide and holding the door, felt the room arrive at it together, the way she felt weather arrive now — the specific quiet of several competent people understanding in the same half-second that they had just been handed something true, and load-bearing, and almost certainly untransmittable to the only two parties on either world whose behaviour it needed to change.

There is one more entry, Kaelin said from the Preserve relay, and it took Mara a moment to notice the voice had come from outside her.

He gave them the crescent. Nine animals, even spacing, eleven minutes, oriented on the worst junction on the northern run — and the fact that neither he nor Alia had known it was the worst until an hour before the animals arranged themselves in front of it.

"Say what you think it is," Zara said.

"No."

"Kaelin—"

"I'll say what it did. It held a formation, it held it on a target it had no means of surveying, and it dispersed without a signal I could record." He set the notebook down. "The word you want from me changes what is owed to them, and I'm not paying that word out of one morning's data in either direction. It goes into the record undecided, in full, with the plain sentence that we cannot resolve it. If I'm wrong about them I'd rather be wrong on paper where somebody can find it."

Léa looked at him for a moment on the stuttering relay. "Enter it undecided," she said. "And flag it, so that nobody six months from now mistakes our not knowing for our not having noticed."

Mara held the channel through the rest of it, and the rest of it was eighteen minutes of people being careful. Then Kythara withdrew — not closing, only lifting the hand, the way it had the first time — and she gathered herself home through the fractal dark, node by node, until she was mostly, almost, sitting on the interface floor again.

She opened the body's eyes. Jīn was there, level and specific, holding the last of the floor.

"You're shaking," they said.

"I know. It'll stop." She flexed her hands and watched them not quite obey yet. "It's not the speaking. It's the two of us being one shape for an hour. I'd like to do it again and I'd like it to be a decision every time."

"It will be."

She sat a moment longer, and in that moment — before the field drew fully in, while she was still half-spread and still able to feel the shape of a thing as a change in the air around it — she found the place where Kythara had been, and found it not quite empty.

Something had come with the sentence and had not been said. She could not read it. It had the texture of a thing deliberately carried rather than a thing hidden, a distinction she trusted an ancient intelligence to be able to make and did not, sitting on a cold floor with her hands not yet steady, entirely enjoy.

Kythara had told them the ground would close if it was pushed. It had not told them anything about the ground.

Mara let the thread come in the rest of the way, said nothing about it to the room, and filed it where she filed everything she had learned to carry: in the part of her that was still, reliably, a single person, and that would be there tomorrow to be asked.
