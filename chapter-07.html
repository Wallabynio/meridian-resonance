LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, MERIDIAN LEVELS / Jīn Wú

Late-cycle in the Meridian Levels meant the readings went quiet before the corridors above did — the deep-interface chamber settling into its overnight baseline a half-hour or so ahead of the Habitat Corridors' own dimming, as if the chamber kept a private clock one step ahead of the colony it served. Jīn Wú had come to like the hour for exactly that reason. Whatever moved through the substrate at this depth moved without an audience, and eleven years of watching had taught them that anomalies, like everything else worth watching closely, behaved differently when they thought no one was looking.

"Baseline confirmed, all channels," the wall said — warm and unhurried, the register every Luna Haven system defaulted to whether the hour was ordinary or not. "Would you like the extended report?"

"Not yet."

Jīn stood at the chamber's centre with the diagnostic stylus loose in one hand, doing the part of the work that never showed up in any report: waiting, attentively, for nothing in particular to happen. The deep-interface chamber was built for exactly this kind of patience — a sunken, unornamented space three levels below the lowest residential tier, its walls a dark basalt-toned composite that swallowed light rather than reflecting it, a suspended ring of sensor arrays hanging motionless above a floor kept scrupulously, deliberately empty. Nothing here was decorative. The one indulgence the architects had allowed themselves was the quality of the dark itself, calibrated so that anything the instruments caught would have nowhere to hide.

Léa Fournier sat at the observation console along the chamber's northern wall, grey-green eyes moving between three passive feeds with the unhurried thoroughness she brought to everything, her loose hair catching what little the ring-lights gave off. She had not spoken in eleven minutes. Jīn had stopped finding her silences companionable and started finding them necessary sometime in their second year working together, and neither fact had changed since.

It was Jīn who saw it first, because Jīn was the one standing close enough to the chamber's dead centre to be looking at exactly the right patch of nothing.

A region of space that should have contained nothing visible contained, instead, a slow and organised distortion — not a flare, not a lens artefact, not the kind of instrument noise that a recalibration cycle usually explained away by morning. It had geometry. It moved the way water moves when something beneath the surface is turning, a spiral logic to it that had no business belonging to empty air, faint traceries of light-that-wasn't-quite-light folding over each other and unfolding again, patient, deliberate, entirely without a source.

Jīn did not move. Did not call out. Did not reach for the stylus's active-scan function, though every year of training said that was the correct next action. They simply watched, for a count they were aware of running somewhere in the back of their mind without ever quite letting it become the whole of their attention — forty seconds, near enough — logging the shape of it the only way it seemed right to log something that was, whatever else it turned out to be, not yet fully in the habit of existing.

"That's new," Jīn said, quietly.

Léa was already crossing the chamber floor by the time the words finished leaving them, her instrument-careful hands rising to the passive array's manual controls without once taking her eyes off the distortion itself, as though looking away from it, even to steady her own equipment, risked losing something that would not offer itself twice.

"Confirmed on three channels," the wall reported. "Source undetermined. Recommend immediate active scan."

"No," Jīn and Léa said, at almost the same moment, and did not look at each other about it.

Léa's readings scrolled past her in a column of numbers that told her everything and nothing — resonance frequencies with no reference match, a spatial signature that folded back on itself in ways her instruments weren't built to parse, temperature and radiation both sitting exactly, suspiciously, at ambient. She opened a session note, cursor waiting in the log field, and sat with her hand above the keys for longer than the moment strictly required. First stable manifestation, deep-interface chamber — she typed that much, then stopped, thumb hovering over the next key, because the sentence that wanted to follow it was a claim, and eleven seconds of spiral geometry, however extraordinary, was not yet evidence for a claim. She deleted nothing. She simply did not finish the sentence, and moved instead to recording only what the instruments had actually shown her, in the flat exact language she had spent twenty years training herself to use instead of the one that wanted to come first.

The distortion held its shape for perhaps ninety seconds, then began, gradually, to lose it — the organised spiral logic fraying at its edges into something closer to static, the way a held note goes ragged when the breath behind it starts to run out.

"It's destabilising," Léa said.

"I know." Jīn did not move closer. Some instinct, not yet backed by any data either of them could have named, told them that closing the distance themselves would not help. "Pull the deep-soil correlation. See if anything upstream is—"

The wall answered before Jīn finished the sentence. "Deep-soil resonance in Habitat Corridors sector eleven shows a matching frequency signature, timed to within four seconds of first manifestation here. Routing alert to M. Ekeberg, currently on shift, sector eleven."

Jīn looked at the fraying shimmer, and then, for the first time since it had appeared, at Léa.

"She's going to want to see this," Jīn said.

Léa did not look up from the console, but something in her posture — the particular stillness of a scientist choosing, deliberately, not to reach for a word before it had been earned — answered for her more clearly than agreement would have.

LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, MERIDIAN LEVELS / Mara Ekeberg

The pulse came through as a small, insistent warmth against Mara's wrist, cutting straight across the deep, listening quiet she carried out of every mycorrhizal shift — a quiet that usually took the better part of an hour to shake off, and that she shook off now in the time it took to read four words off her handheld and start moving.

Levels. Now. Bring yourself.

Jīn never wrote bring yourself. Jīn wrote coordinates, timestamps, frequency ranges — precise, undramatic, exactly as much language as a fact required and not one word more. Whatever was waiting three levels down had already talked Jīn into something that wasn't quite procedure, and that alone had Mara moving at something close to a run through corridors that pulsed their slow blue-green around her, unbothered, indifferent to the fact that she was, for once, in a hurry.

She came into the deep-interface chamber breathing hard and found Jīn and Léa exactly where she'd have expected them, standing at a careful, deliberate distance from the chamber's centre, where a shape hung in the air that had no right to be there.

It was fainter than it must have been. She could see that much even without being told — the fraying, unravelling edge of something that had been more coherent a few minutes earlier and was losing the argument for its own existence a little more with every second she watched it. And yet, even fraying, even half-lost, it did something the moment she crossed the threshold that made her stop dead just inside the door.

It leaned toward her.

Not dramatically. Not the way a held object falls toward a stronger source of gravity. It was subtler than that, and worse for being subtle — a shift in the distortion's drifting geometry, faint but unmistakably real, oriented now a few degrees off its previous axis, toward the exact point in the room where she stood.

"It did that when you came in," Jīn said, very quietly, watching her rather than it. "Just now. I want to be clear I'm not imagining it, and I want you to tell me if you think I am."

"I don't think you're imagining it." Mara's voice came out steadier than she felt. She had spent three years learning to read a living system through skin before she trusted it through instruments, and every part of that training was telling her, insistently, that whatever this was, it had noticed her the way the mycorrhizal substrate noticed a hand pressed flat against a wall — not as an object in a room, but as something with interior weather worth attending to.

She stepped closer.

The shimmer's fraying edges did not resolve. If anything, closing the distance made it worse — the spiral logic that had held for ninety seconds fracturing further, jittering, patterns breaking apart and reforming and breaking apart again in a rhythm that had none of the earlier stillness in it. It was reaching for her, or seemed to be, and finding, each time, that it could not quite hold the shape of the reach.

"It's worse with just me," Mara said, forcing the observation flat, the way Jīn would have. "Fragmented. Whatever this is, it doesn't stabilise around me alone."

Jīn was already moving, unhurried, to stand beside her — not touching the console, not raising the stylus, doing nothing that either of them could have pointed to afterward as a technical intervention. What Jīn did instead was harder to name and, Mara understood without being told, entirely deliberate: a quality of attention brought fully and without reservation to bear on the shimmer, the specific, sustained regard of someone treating a thing not as an instrument reading to be classified but as something that merited being considered on its own terms.

The change was immediate, and unmistakable.

The fraying edges gathered. The jittering pattern found, within two or three seconds, a rhythm that held rather than broke — resolving, smoothing, drawing itself back into the same organised spiral Jīn had first seen alone, except brighter now, more defined, a slow coherent turning that Mara felt, standing this close, not only with her eyes but somewhere lower and older than her eyes, a resonance she recognised without being able to say from where.

"Eleven," Léa said from the console, her voice pitched low, counting. "Twelve. Thirteen."

It held for what Léa would later log as eleven seconds exactly, before it began, gently this time, without the earlier fraying violence, to fade — not collapsing, not fleeing, simply completing whatever it had been doing and withdrawing from a stage it no longer needed.

The chamber went dark and ordinary around them, the ring-lights the only light left, and none of the three of them spoke for a long moment.

"It responded to you being here," Jīn said finally, to Mara, in the same careful, undramatic register they used for everything that mattered too much to dress up. "And it only held together once I was paying attention to it the way I'd pay attention to a person. Not an instrument."

"That's not a mechanism," Léa said, from the console, already back to the flat exact language that was hers. "That's an observation. I want to be careful about the difference tonight, of all nights."

"I know." Jīn looked at the space where the shimmer had been, empty now, giving nothing back. "I'm not claiming a mechanism. I'm telling you what I saw."

Mara said nothing. She stood very still in the space the shimmer had leaned toward, one hand pressed instinctively flat against her own sternum, and thought, without yet having any language steady enough to trust with the thought, that something in that fading spiral had felt, for eleven seconds, less like being observed than like being recognised.

CORPORATE UNION — SÃO VERDE, VERDANT CITADEL / Daniel Cruz

Nine thousand kilometres from a chamber he would never see, Daniel Cruz sat at the terrace-side terminal with a blank page open and Elena's data — three weeks old now, worn soft in his memory the way a document gets worn soft from being read too many times — arranged in three columns on the display beside it, and found that the thing he'd been putting off for three weeks was, now that he'd finally sat down to do it, harder than the leak itself had been.

The irrigation cycle had finished an hour ago. The Citadel's forty storeys of engineered terraces held the particular hush that settled after the pumps went quiet, fertiliser and warm soil and, underneath it, the faint ozone tang of hardware that had no business running behind a monoculture facade — a smell Daniel had stopped noticing as strange years ago and had never quite stopped noticing as home.

He had read the drift figures enough times to have them by heart: the entangled-channel telemetry, the resonance signatures that matched nothing in any Corporate Union or Sovereign Coalition archive he'd ever had access to, the shape of something enormous happening four thousand kilometres away in a Lunar Accord research chamber that treated the discovery, as far as he could tell, as a fact to be studied rather than a weapon to be built. That last part was the part that had kept him up three nights running. Not the physics. The politics of the physics — the fact that whatever this was, every bloc that learned of it would immediately understand it as leverage, and the first bloc to claim it outright would own more than territory. It would own the argument about what consciousness itself was allowed to be used for.

Nobody owns this, he wrote, and then crossed it out, because a resistance document that opened with a demand had never once in his experience changed anyone's mind who wasn't already convinced.

He tried again. This document exists because the alternative — silence, until someone with the resources to weaponise a discovery gets there first — is worse than an imperfect draft written by someone who has never set foot in the room where the discovery happened.

That was closer. Ink stayed under his nails these days, an old habit from the parallel record he kept on paper for nothing that mattered and everything that did, and he found himself reaching for the physical logbook now, out of some instinct that this particular document deserved to exist in both forms from its very first draft — the network copy that could travel in three seconds to three continents, and the ink-and-paper copy that no compliance sweep would ever find because it had never once touched a network to begin with.

He wrote for two hours. What emerged was rough — he knew it was rough, could feel every seam in it, every place where he was reasoning from Elena's fragmentary data and his own years of watching what centralised power did with anything it could centralise — but it existed, four principles instead of the fourteen he'd started with, stripped down to the load-bearing ones: that no single bloc or institution would hold proprietary access to the phenomenon; that whatever was discovered would be shared, in full, with every party capable of using it responsibly; that consent, not capability, would govern who was permitted contact with it; that documentation of everything — the discoveries and the decisions both — would be kept in a form no single point of failure could erase.

"Draft saved. Local storage only," the terminal said, in the clipped procedural register every piece of Corporate Union-descended hardware defaulted to, whether or not the moment deserved something warmer.

Daniel sat back, rubbed the ink from the side of his hand out of habit, and looked at the four lines on the display without yet feeling the satisfaction he'd expected to feel. It wasn't finished. It wasn't even close to finished — a draft with no legal standing, drawn from secondhand data, written by a man who had never once stood in the room where any of it was true. But it was the first thing that had existed, anywhere, built specifically to keep whatever was happening in that Luna Haven chamber from becoming the exclusive property of whoever got to it with the most force first.

Outside, the terraces held their after-hours stillness, forty storeys of something genuinely good grown over something the city had never quite finished fixing, and Daniel Cruz closed the physical logbook, sealed it back behind the false panel where it belonged, and sat a while longer in the dark before going to find sleep he didn't expect to come easily.
