Hold: Rachel Iverson's precise blonde bob, pale blue eyes and manicured, never-idle hands, exactly as the attached portrait establishes them; and, of the place itself, the sealed black-lacquer executive chamber with biometric amber accents and no physical windows, exactly as the attached plate establishes it.

Change: Rachel stands at the head of the chamber mid-presentation, one hand indicating a point on a display panel, addressing a small board audience seated just out of frame. Her composure is total and deliberate — precise data, precisely sequenced, delivered with the confidence of someone who has already anticipated every objection in the room.

Bound: no board members are rendered as identifiable figures; at most their presence is implied at the frame's edge, unindividuated. No legible text on any display. Nothing about the chamber's own architecture is invented beyond the attached plate.

The Mirror Bastion never quite let you forget you were being watched, which was, Rachel Iverson had long since decided, the entire point of building it in black lacquer and biometric amber instead of anything softer. Every surface in the chamber returned some version of your own reflection back at you — warped faintly in the lacquered walls, sharpened in the polished table, caught and held for half a second in the amber light strip that ran the room's perimeter reading vitals nobody in the room had consented to have read. It was a room designed to make its occupants feel observed even when, as now, the only thing actually watching them was the building itself.

She had built her presentation to survive that scrutiny. That was the discipline of it.

"Thermal and resonance signatures across all four Habitat Corridor sectors," she said, and the wall behind her resolved the requested overlay without needing to be asked twice, "cross-referenced against the mycorrhizal power draw Luna Haven itself reports to the Lunar Accord's own public energy ledger." She let the numbers sit a moment before she said anything else. Numbers, presented plainly enough, did most of an argument's work before a person ever needed to raise their voice. "The draw has increased sevenfold in eleven months. Luna Haven's public statements characterise this as expanded agricultural output. The output figures do not match the draw."

Six board members sat around the table's long curve, backlit by the amber strip so that their faces read, from where Rachel stood, as more silhouette than expression. She had chosen this particular six for a reason: enough seniority in the room to matter, not enough unity among them yet to ask the question she most wanted deferred, which was what, precisely, she intended anyone to do about it.

"You're describing an energy discrepancy," said the board member nearest the door — a woman Rachel had crossed paths with twice before, both times over budget allocations neither of them had cared about personally. "Discrepancies happen. Colonies under-report for tax reasons as often as they over-report for propaganda ones."

"They do," Rachel agreed, and meant it, because agreeing with the objections she hadn't yet defeated was cheaper than pretending they didn't exist. "Which is why I didn't stop at the discrepancy." She advanced the display. A second dataset layered itself beneath the first — resonance-frequency traces pulled, she did not specify how, from a research programme Luna Haven had never formally disclosed to any Corporate Union oversight body, and did not need to, under the treaty language currently governing dimensional research disclosure. That omission was, in its own quiet way, the most useful fact in the room. "This is what the power is actually doing. It isn't feeding crop yield. It's sustaining a sustained-duration resonance field, consistent in signature with directed dimensional manipulation."

"Dimensional manipulation," the same woman repeated, careful, testing the phrase's weight before she'd let herself commit to reacting to it.

"Post-scarcity idealism is a framing," Rachel said. "It is not, on its own, a safety guarantee. Luna Haven has spent four decades building a public identity around openness and abundance. I am not disputing that identity is sincerely held by most of the people living inside it." She let that land — the small, precise concession that made everything that followed sound more careful, not less. "I am telling you what four decades of sincere openness looks like from the outside, when the power draw underneath it stops matching the story being told about it. It looks exactly like every early-stage weapons programme this board has ever been shown, run by people who may not yet know that's what they're building."

Nobody at the table said anything for a moment. That silence, too, was doing work — she had learned, across a career built on rooms exactly like this one, that a room's silence after the right sentence was worth more than its noise after the wrong one.

"You're proposing what, specifically," said the man at the table's head, and his tone carried the particular flatness of someone who had sat through a hundred briefings that ended in a request for money or authority and had long since stopped being impressed by either.

"A classified intervention framework," Rachel said. "Not action. A framework — contingency architecture, held in reserve, reviewed at successive escalation thresholds, requiring separate board authorisation at each one. I am asking this room to open a file. I am not asking it to act on anything in that file today."

It was, she knew, the single most important sentence of the entire presentation, and she had built the rest of the hour to make this exact one feel small by comparison. Opening a file cost the board nothing they could be held to later. It felt, to everyone in this room, like caution rather than escalation — a hedge against a possibility rather than a commitment to a course. Rachel had learned this a long time ago, in rooms smaller than this one, arguing points that mattered less: the decision that actually determined an outcome was never the dramatic one. It was the quiet administrative one nobody in the room believed themselves to be making.

The man at the table's head glanced left and right along the curve, reading the room the way Rachel had spent the last forty minutes teaching him to read it, and said, "Open the file. Classified, board-eyes-only, quarterly review." He did not phrase it as a question. Nobody in the room treated it as one.

"Understood," Rachel said, and did not let her face show what she felt, which was not triumph, not exactly — something closer to the particular satisfaction of watching a mechanism she had built operate precisely as designed. She gathered her tablet, thanked the room with the flat professional warmth the room expected of her, and walked out past the amber light strip, which read her vitals one last time on her way through the door and, as always, said nothing about what it found.

The file was open. Everything after this was administration. Rachel had built her entire career on knowing the difference, and on being the only person in most rooms who did.

Edit the three attached images — Wáng Sù's own portrait, Tán Měi's own portrait, and the Broadcast Chamber's own plate — into one new scene. Do not regenerate any reference; composite them into a single frame, full-width 16:9 cinematic landscape, photorealistic film still.

Hold: Wáng Sù's haunted eyes, retreating hairline and steady console hands, exactly as his attached portrait establishes them; Tán Měi's severe hair, disciplined bearing and military tailoring, exactly as her attached portrait establishes them; and, of the place itself, the state propaganda production chamber, airless, designed to erase the individual, exactly as the attached plate establishes it.

Change: Wáng Sù sits at the review console mid-script-review, hands steady on the controls, fully absorbed in the technical review of a propaganda package. Tán Měi stands at the edge of his official circle, present for the technical review of the scientific claims, saying nothing, her attention giving nothing away.

Bound: exactly these two figures, no additional named characters. No legible text on any script or display. Nothing about the chamber's own architecture is invented beyond the attached plate.

"Reframe the second sequence," Wáng Sù said. "Less colony, more infrastructure. I want the viewer looking at machinery, not people."

"Adjusting," the production system answered, clipped and immediate, the register every Beijing Command system defaulted to whether the request mattered or not. "Sequence two re-cut. Runtime unchanged."

Wáng Sù watched the footage resolve on the Broadcast Chamber's wraparound wall — real footage, that was the useful part, always real footage, because audiences believed real footage in a way they never quite believed a rendering, and Luna Haven's own Habitat Corridor monitoring gave him more than enough real footage to work with. The resonance anomaly that Luna Haven's own researchers had, according to the intercepted data his analysts had annotated for him, not yet fully explained to themselves became, recut against a slower frame rate and a colder colour grade, something that looked unmistakably like a machine warming up for a test it did not want observed. The instrument traces scrolling along the frame's lower third were real too. Their attribution — evidence consistent with directed-energy weapons testing — was not, but nineteen years in this chamber had taught Wáng Sù that a caption's confidence mattered more to most viewers than its provenance.

"Good," he said. "Run the full package once more from the top."

He had built this particular campaign over six weeks, in careful coordination with a Corporate Union channel that existed nowhere in any official record — a channel Rachel Iverson had opened and he had accepted, both of them understanding without needing to say it aloud that neither bloc wanted its fingerprints alone on what was about to be broadcast to every Lunar Accord member state simultaneously. The package was technically excellent. He allowed himself that assessment without embarrassment. He had been writing technically excellent scripts for nineteen years, and whatever else was true of the work, the craft of it was not something he had ever let himself do carelessly.

Around him, the Chamber performed its usual choreography: the LED wall's slow shift through state-sanctioned colour temperatures, the production team moving with the unhurried precision of people who had done this often enough that urgency would have looked like a mistake, his own tailoring — dark, precise, cut to read as authority on camera without reading as vanity — settling into the posture the role required of him the way it always did, the way it had for nineteen years, as naturally as breathing.

He became aware, without quite looking up to confirm it, of the officer standing at the edge of his working circle.

She had been introduced to him three weeks earlier as a technical liaison — a military-technology officer assigned to review the propaganda package's scientific claims for accuracy before broadcast, a formality more than a function, since nobody had ever meaningfully corrected anything Wáng Sù's team produced. Tán Měi stood exactly where a liaison ought to stand: close enough to the review table to see the data scrolling past, far enough back that she was never quite in anyone's direct line of address. Severe black hair pulled into a bun that had not moved in three weeks of observing him. Gloved hands, folded, still.

"The resonance figures in sequence two," she said, when he finally glanced her way. "Confirmed within acceptable variance for broadcast purposes."

It was, Wáng Sù noted, exactly the sentence her role required of her and not one word more. She had said versions of that sentence in every review session for three weeks — precise, correct within the narrow bounds of what she'd been asked to confirm, entirely unrevealing of anything she might privately think about the figures themselves, if she thought anything about them at all. He had worked with technical liaisons before who used the role as a small stage for their own opinions, a raised eyebrow here, a pointed silence there. Tán Měi did none of that. She simply confirmed, in the flattest possible register, exactly what had been asked, and then she watched — the review table, the footage, the room — with an attention that Wáng Sù, on a different day, in a different mood, might have found himself wondering about. Today he had a broadcast to finish. He filed the thought nowhere and returned to the frame.

"Run it again," he said. "I want the transition into sequence three tightened by half a second."

"Adjusting," the system said.

Behind him, at the edge of his circle, Tán Měi made a small notation on the tablet she carried for exactly this purpose — a notation Wáng Sù did not see, because he had no reason to look for it, and would not have thought much of it if he had. It was the kind of note a diligent liaison made constantly: timestamps, confirmations, the small procedural residue of a job done correctly. She had been making notes like it, in this exact posture, at the edge of exactly this room, for three weeks. She would go on making them, Wáng Sù assumed without ever quite forming the thought consciously, for as long as the review assignment required her presence and nothing more.

He did not know what she noted, that day or any other. He did not ask. It was not, as far as his role required him to understand it, any of his business what a liaison chose to write down, so long as the figures she confirmed for broadcast were the figures the broadcast needed. The package ran clean on the final pass. Wáng Sù signed off on it at 1740 hours, the LED wall shifted back to its resting neutral, and the Broadcast Chamber emptied around him in the same unhurried order it always did, technically excellent, exactly as designed, by a man who had been building rooms like this one for nineteen years and had never once, until this campaign, felt the need to wonder who else was standing quietly at its edges.

Edit the two attached images — Jīn Wú's own portrait and the Habitat Corridors' own plate — into one new scene. Do not regenerate either reference; composite them into a single frame, full-width 16:9 cinematic landscape, photorealistic film still.

Hold: Jīn Wú's face, subtle neural lace and lattice-scarred fingertips, exactly as the attached portrait establishes them; and, of the place itself, the bioluminescent living walls with the proximity sensors glowing where the mycorrhizal network runs close to the surface, exactly as the attached plate establishes it.

Change: Jīn stands partway down the corridor with a portable display held up, running a correlation analysis between two overlaid data traces — one drawn from the colony's soil resonance, one from an external feed. Their expression is focused rather than alarmed: a pattern is emerging that they do not yet have a theory for.

Bound: Jīn is alone; no second person appears. No legible text, graph label or value on the display — pattern and overlay only. Nothing about the corridor's own bioluminescence changes beyond the attached plate.

The correlation had been sitting in Jīn's working file for four days before they let themselves look at it directly, which was, they privately acknowledged, exactly as long as it took for the discomfort of not looking at it to outweigh the discomfort of being wrong about what it showed.

"Pull the propaganda-broadcast timing log again," Jīn said. "Public feed, all Corporate Union and Sovereign Coalition channels, past ninety days."

"Compiled," the wall answered, warm and precise, the register every Luna Haven system used with the people who worked inside it. "Cross-reference against deep-soil resonance readings, Habitat Corridors sectors nine through fourteen?"

"Yes. Same window."

The two datasets resolved side by side on the corridor's interface panel, and Jīn stood with the diagnostic stylus loose in one hand and did the thing they had trained themselves, over eleven years, to do before reaching for any conclusion: they looked at the shape of the thing for longer than felt strictly necessary, resisting the urge to name it before the data had earned a name.

The shape, when they let themselves see it, was this. Every time the Corporate Union or Sovereign Coalition propaganda networks intensified — reach widening, tone sharpening, broadcast frequency climbing across a forty-eight-hour window — the deep-soil resonance running through the mycorrhizal substrate beneath sectors nine through fourteen shifted, within a margin too tight to be coincidence and too imprecise to be proof, toward a frequency band the Levels' own environmental monitoring flagged, in its flattest technical language, as consistent with organismal distress response.

It was not a perfect correlation. Jīn was careful, even alone in a corridor with no one to perform carefulness for, to hold that qualification in mind rather than let the shape of the pattern talk them into more certainty than it had earned. Two of the fourteen broadcast spikes in the window showed no matching resonance shift at all. But the other twelve did, tracking close enough in both timing and magnitude that Jīn's hand went still on the tablet's edge the same way it had, years ago, the first time a reading told them something they weren't yet ready to believe.

"Define distress response," Jīn said.

"Frequency pattern consistent with mycorrhizal network states observed during localised habitat stress events — resource scarcity, structural damage, sustained external interference," the wall said. "This classification does not distinguish cause. Confidence in classification: moderate."

Jīn exhaled slowly — not frustration, the small private ritual of making room for a thought before deciding what to do with it. The substrate had no way of knowing what a broadcast was. It had no receiver for propaganda, no faculty for language, nothing in its biology that should, by any model Jīn had ever worked from, register a script recorded four thousand kilometres away in a chamber it would never sense directly. And yet the pattern sat there on the panel, twelve matches out of fourteen, refusing to resolve into the coincidence Jīn kept trying, out of professional caution, to let it be.

Footsteps, unhurried, a weight and rhythm Jīn didn't need to turn around to place.

"You're doing the thing again," Mara said, crossing the last few metres of corridor to stand beside them. She glanced at the overlay, then at Jīn's face, and whatever she read there made her go quiet in a way that wasn't quite alarm.

"I don't have a mechanism," Jīn said, before she could ask. "I want to be clear about that. I have a correlation. I don't have a reason the substrate would care what's being broadcast on the other side of the planet."

"But you have twelve out of fourteen."

"I have twelve out of fourteen." Jīn turned the stylus over once in their fingers, a habit they'd never quite broken. "The two that don't match are both low-intensity broadcasts — narrow distribution, short runtime. Whatever this is responding to, it looks like it's responding to scale. Reach, not content."

Mara set her palm flat against the living wall beside the interface strip, the way she always did when she wanted to read a thing through skin as well as instrument, and was quiet for a long moment. "That's worse," she said finally, "not better."

"I know."

"If it's reach and not content, it isn't the propaganda itself doing anything to the network directly. It's — " She stopped, visibly unwilling to finish the sentence before she'd earned the right to.

"It's the attention," Jīn said, because someone had to say it, and it was easier coming from the person who'd built the discipline of saying only what the data actually supported. "Something about the scale of coordinated attention aimed at this colony, from outside it, registers in the substrate as pressure. Not the words. The weight of that many minds being turned toward the same false idea about this place at the same time."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. The corridor's bioluminescence held its slow blue-green pulse around them, unbothered, the same as it had been the day Jīn first logged an anomaly here that neither of them had yet found a name steady enough to trust.

"Log it clean," Mara said eventually. "No interpretation. Not yet."

"Already drafting it that way." Jīn's tone held the same careful flatness they gave every reading that mattered more than it should. Correlation observed between external broadcast-reach metrics and deep-soil resonance distress markers, sectors nine through fourteen. Mechanism undetermined. Twelve of fourteen sampled events show timing correspondence within acceptable margin. Two do not. Recommend continued monitoring; no causal claim supported by current data.

"You know where this goes if it's real," Mara said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know where it goes if I say it's real before I can prove it." Jīn flagged the file for the next scheduled research council review — the ordinary channel, the one built to put an anomaly in front of the people whose job it was to weigh it, no faster and no slower than the system was designed to move. "That's the part I can control. What it means once someone with more data looks at it — that's not mine to decide from here."

The wall chimed, soft and unhurried, log confirmation registering on the interface panel. Outside sector eleven, the substrate kept doing whatever it was doing, patient and wordless, registering a pressure it had no way to name any more than Jīn did — a whole colony's worth of quiet, honest work sitting directly beneath a story being told about it, four thousand kilometres away, by people who had never once set foot in the corridors they were describing.
