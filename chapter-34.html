The living walls had hardened to a colder, harder blue-green by the time the security-planning division finished making its case, and Léa Fournier sat in the tiered ring and listened to the best argument for becoming her enemy that she had ever heard.

It was a good argument. That was the part she had made herself accept before she came down: that Tomasz Nowak was not a frightened man reaching for a weapon, and that treating him as one would lose the room inside a minute. He stood at the speaking floor with his slate live at his side, square-built and close-cropped and unhurried in the way of a man who had spent twenty years being the calmest person in every emergency he had ever attended, and he laid the proposal out the way he laid out a perimeter — cleanly, in the order the threat would come.

"A defensive override protocol," he said. "Narrow, conditional, and dormant until a strike is confirmed inbound. The Meridian research systems in the Levels beneath us are the most sensitive resonance instruments that have ever existed. Right now they can only read. The proposal is that we build the capacity — held offline, behind a triple authorisation this council would hold the keys to — for them to also act: to generate a coercive resonance field across the approaching assault the moment it commits, in the eleven minutes we would have between commitment and impact." He let the number sit; everyone in the ring knew where eleven minutes had come from. "I am not asking you to use it. I am asking you to have it, so that the choice exists at all. A colony that cannot defend itself does not get to keep having debates like this one. It gets to have this debate once."

The hardened light moved on the faces around the ring, and Léa watched the argument do its work, because it was working. She had spent the two nights since the security division circulated the brief lying awake assembling her answer, and the hardest thing about the answer was that Tomasz was right about the danger and right about the timeline and right that eleven minutes was not enough time to convene anyone, and that all of it was true and none of it was the point.

The floor came to her. It did not have to; the Dome Plaza was not a parliament and no one was obliged to speak. She rose anyway, because the thing she had to say was not an objection to a clause. It was an objection to a direction.

"I want to start by agreeing with Tomasz," she said, and felt the security tier brace for the but, and did not give it to them yet. "Everything he described is real. The fleet is real, the eleven minutes are real, and the instinct to close the gap between what we can do and what is being done to us is not cowardice or panic. It is the most natural response a threatened body has. I felt it myself, reading the brief. I want you to know I felt it, because I am not going to stand here and pretend that the people who want this are weak and I am strong. They are not weak. I am not strong. We are all equally frightened, and the proposal is what fear looks like when it is competent." She let that land. "Which is exactly why I think we have to refuse it."

She had rehearsed the next part until it was smooth, and now, standing in it, she slowed down instead, because smooth was how you smuggled a conclusion past a council that deserved to arrive at its own.

"Ask what Luna Haven actually has that the Corporate Union and the Sovereign Coalition do not. It is not our fleet; we barely have one. It is not our numbers or our reach. What we have — the only thing we have that they cannot manufacture — is that we are visibly, demonstrably a different kind of thing than they are. Everything this colony is worth was bought with that difference. Our authority in every consultation, every treaty channel, every argument we will ever make to a frightened Earth about what the Meridian is for — all of it rests on one fact: that we do not use consciousness as a weapon. That we found a responsive intelligence and chose to listen to it instead of aiming it." She turned, slowly, taking in the ring. "The override does not cost us a battle. It costs us that. The instant we build a system that reaches into the Meridian and makes it do harm on our command, we are no longer a different kind of thing. We are the Mirror Bastion with better lighting. We become exactly the argument Wáng Sù has been making about us for two years — that Luna Haven is a weapons programme wearing the language of ethics — and we make it true, and we hand it to him signed."

"With respect," Tomasz said — and it was respect; he did not perform it — "a principle you cannot survive to hold is not a principle. It is an epitaph. If the strike lands and this colony is gone, our demonstrated difference goes with it. There is no one left to be different at."

"I know." She had known he would say it, because it was the true objection, the one that could not be waved off. "And if the only way to survive is to become the thing we exist to refuse, then I think we have to be willing to look at not surviving, and mean it, or the refusal was never real to begin with." The words cost her more, out loud, than they had in the dark. "I am not being romantic about this. I have a list in my head of the people in this colony I am proposing to risk, and it is not an abstraction, it has faces on it. But there is a difference between dying because you were attacked and dying because you disarmed yourself, and I am not offering us the second one. I am saying there are other defences — Sienna's logistics, the treaty channels a fleet cannot legally cross, the Guardians on the far side of a door we are, right now, trying to open — and I would rather bet the colony on all of those together than buy eleven minutes of tactical advantage with the one asset that makes the colony worth defending."

The ring was quiet. The walls burned hard and cold above them, patient in their own chemical way, and Léa understood, the way you understand a thing you cannot yet prove, that she had made the room think but had not yet made it decide, and that the distance between the two was where the colony would be won or lost.

Then Zara AlHāshimī asked for the floor, and the argument stopped being about what Luna Haven should choose and became about what it was permitted to.

Zara did not stand. She rarely did; she conducted argument the way she conducted everything, seated and precise, dark hijab framing a face that gave away only attention, hands open on the rail in front of her in the deliberate way that meant she had already decided and was now merely going to show her work.

"I want to move us off the ground we are standing on," she said, "because I think it is the wrong ground, and I think fighting on it is how we lose. Léa has made a moral argument, and Tomasz has answered it with a survival argument, and both of them are serious, and if this council decides the question on which of those two feelings is stronger tonight, under a fleet, we will get it wrong, because fear is a thumb on that scale and everyone in this ring can feel it pressing." She paused, exactly one second, the pause of someone who has learned that the law lands harder when the room is leaning in for it. "So let me tell you why the proposal in front of us is not a decision this council actually gets to make."

She turned her open hand toward the floor where Tomasz still stood.

"The override protocol requires the Meridian research systems to generate a coercive resonance field. Strip the engineering language away and that means: it requires us to reach into a system that has demonstrated responsiveness — a system that answers to the quality of attention, that retracts when we are afraid and extends when we are calm, that Léa has been documenting as a responsive intelligence since the cascade — and compel it to act, at scale, against its structure, without asking it, because asking it is not a step the protocol contains and could not be, because a weapon that has to request consent from its target is not a weapon." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "That is non-consensual access to a responsive system. And under the rights framework already in force in this colony — the provisional principles every one of us is operating under while the formal document is still being drafted — non-consensual access to a responsive system is not a hard case or a grey area. It is the specific thing the framework exists to prohibit. It is the line the whole architecture is built around."

"The framework is provisional," someone said from the security tier — not Tomasz; he was watching Zara with the still attention of a man recalculating.

"It is provisional in the sense that we have not finished writing it down," Zara said. "It is not provisional in the sense that we get to ignore it when it becomes inconvenient. That is precisely backward. A consent framework you honour only when honouring it is cheap is not a framework, it is a mood. Its entire value — the reason it is worth anything to anyone watching us from Earth — is that it binds us here, under exactly this pressure, with a fleet forming and eleven minutes on the clock. If we can override it tonight because we are afraid, then it never bound us at all, and everyone will know that, and they will be right." She let her hands close, once, deliberately. "The proposal is not permissible. Not because it is wrong, though I believe it is. Because it is unlawful under the law we have already chosen to be governed by, and this council does not have the authority to suspend that law by majority in a bad week. If it did, it would not be a right. It would be a privilege we extend to ourselves, and I have read enough history to know what those are worth when the sky is falling."

The silence after that was a different quality of silence. Léa had made the room feel the stakes; Zara had removed the room's permission to trade them away. Between the two of them, the question had been closed from both ends — the thing they should not do, and the thing they could not — and the hardened walls kept their vigil over a council that had, without anyone yet saying so, already turned.

Tomasz spoke last, which she respected him for.

"I withdraw the proposal," he said, "before we vote it down, because I would rather it be withdrawn than defeated. I still believe the danger is what I said it was. I want that in the record." He looked, for a moment, not at the ring but at the floor beneath it, through it, toward the Levels three tiers down where the instruments he had wanted to arm sat reading the dark. "But I built the security architecture of this place, and I am hearing tonight that the architecture I know how to build is the wrong shape for what we are actually protecting. I don't yet know what the right shape is." A short, honest silence. "I'd like help finding out. Not tonight."

"Not tonight," Léa agreed, gently, and meant it as the beginning of something rather than the end of an argument.

The council did what the council did. It consented, slowly, visibly, without the mercy of speed, to remain a thing that could be destroyed. No one pretended it was a victory. The hardened light held on all their faces, and Léa sat in it and let herself feel the full weight of what they had just chosen, which was not safety, and was not even the hope of safety. It was only the refusal to become the other thing, preserved intact, at exactly the moment it would have been easiest to spend.

Afterward she went down to the Levels, because she needed to stand next to the thing they had refused to weaponise and make sure she still believed it was worth what she had just risked the colony to protect.

Three tiers of hardened corridor took her out of the hard light and into the deeper calm, the same blue-green softened now, the mycorrhizal glow breathing its slow luminescence along the living walls the way it had before any of this, before the fleets, before the cascade, before she had learned that the future she had helped build was fragile in ways she had not been warned about. The interface chamber opened ahead of her, quiet at this hour, the great resonance instruments standing in their patient ranks — not weapons, not tonight, not ever if the council held to what it had just decided. Only listeners. Machines built to pay attention, doing the one thing she had spent her career insisting was harder and rarer and more valuable than the thing everyone else wanted them to do.

Near the interface floor, the fragments from the cascade sat in their monitored cradle — rust-red wood and cobalt soil, living material from a world she had never stood on — and as she came in, calm now, the drop of the vote's adrenaline leaving her, she watched them do the thing that still stopped her breath every time. They extended. Fine root structures reached, slowly, toward the nearest mycorrhizal interface, the way they did when the people near them were not afraid. The intelligence in the room read her, and answered, and reached back, exactly as it had been reaching back since the day she first understood that it was reaching at all.

She stood there a long time.

This, she thought — not in words, quite, but close to them — is the thing. This was what non-consensual access meant, made physical: reaching into that patient, answering system and making it into a fist. She had defended the abstraction in the ring with a rehearsed argument, and here was the argument with soil on it, extending its roots toward her because she had come in calm, and she knew — the way she had learned, in month after month of consequence arriving before its mechanism, to trust a thing she could feel before she could prove it — that the council had been right, and that being right was going to cost them, and that both of those were simply true at once and neither cancelled the other.

Jīn Wú found her there, an arm's length off, the way they kept a careful distance from anyone standing close to the interface. "It held, then," they said. Not a question. Word travelled fast down three tiers.

"It held. Barely, and only because Zara found the provision. If it had come down to my argument alone, I think we'd have built the thing by morning." She did not take her eyes off the reaching fragments. "Tomasz withdrew it himself. He's not wrong about the danger, Jīn. That's the part I can't put down. We may have just chosen the way this colony dies."

"Or the only way it was ever worth keeping alive." Jīn was quiet a moment. "You know they'll come back to it. Not Tomasz — the pressure. The next time a strike gets close, someone will make the same case, and it'll be a little more reasonable each time, because that's how it works. We didn't settle this tonight. We just refused it once."

"I know." She thought of the hardened walls, of the list of faces in her head, of the roots extending toward her in the blue-green dark. "So we'll refuse it again. And again, as many times as it's asked, for as long as there's a colony left to ask it in." She finally looked away from the fragments, at Jīn, and found that the enormity of it had settled into something she could carry. "That's not a weakness we have to apologise for. It's the whole design. An Accord that could arm itself in eleven minutes wouldn't be an Accord anymore — it would just be a faster oligarchy, and the galaxy already has enough of those. We move at the speed of consent. Even now. Especially now."

Above them, three tiers up, the walls of the Dome Plaza were still hard and cold, still telling the truth about how much danger the colony was in. Down here the light was the softer register, the colour of the thing they had decided to keep being, and the fragments of another world reached patiently toward it in the dark, asking nothing, taking nothing, extended toward the calm because the calm was real.

Léa Fournier stayed until her hands were steady, and then she went back up to help defend, by every means that did not require betraying it, a colony that had just chosen to remain worth defending.
