Change: Elena sits alone at her console deep into the night, reading an old, dense archived document, her posture the stillness of someone absorbing something that changes what they thought they understood. One hand rests flat near the console's edge. The amber telemetry glow is the only light on her face.

Bound: Elena is alone; no second person appears. No legible text on the archived document — dense abstract page-texture only. Nothing about the floor's own architecture is invented beyond the attached plate.

"Archive scheduled for deletion in six minutes."

The console said it the way it said everything: flat, unbothered, a voice built to deliver the end of a forty-year-old research programme in the same register it used to confirm a parking allocation. Elena had pulled the retention queue on a hunch she couldn't have defended in an interview — a routine sweep of the deletion schedule, the kind of housekeeping task that filled the dead hours after midnight — and found, three lines down from a decade of expired maintenance logs, a folder classification she didn't recognise. CU-DIMPHYS-ARCHIVE, restricted, scheduled disposal. Nobody had bothered to renew its retention flag. Nobody, she suspected, remembered it needed one.

"Delay disposal," she said. "Administrative hold, forty-eight hours. Cite retention-audit review."

"Hold applied. Disposal rescheduled." A pause, the kind the system only inserted when a query fell outside its normal traffic. "Note: this classification has not been accessed in nineteen years."

"Noted."

She opened it anyway.

The files were old in a way New Manhattan's architecture never let anything be old — not decayed, just abandoned mid-thought, the way a desk looks when someone stands up in the middle of a sentence and never comes back to finish it. Interferometry logs in a document format three schema revisions out of date. Memoranda addressed to department heads whose names meant nothing to her, cc'd to distribution lists that had been dissolved, reorganised, dissolved again, until whatever institutional memory once explained who these people were had been reorganised out of existence along with them. She read the header on the earliest file twice before she trusted it. 2061. Two years before the Uprising. This programme had been running, quietly, inside the Corporate Union's own research apparatus, while she was still an undergraduate three floors below this tier learning the mathematics that would eventually let her build the systems now reading it back to her.

She pulled her chair closer to the display and read.

The early documents were careful in a way she recognised from her own writing — the specific, hedged, over-qualified language of scientists who expected their work to be read by people who understood it, and who had not yet learned to write for people who wanted only the parts that were useful. Boundary-condition anomaly, class unclassified, recurring at eleven of fourteen monitored resonance sites. Preliminary hypothesis: dimensional adjacency, unconfirmed. Requesting continued funding for baseline mapping. No urgency in it. No weapons framework, no threat-assessment language, no strategic-advantage justification of the kind that filled every funding request she'd ever written herself. Just a small research team, working carefully, treating a genuinely strange phenomenon the way you were supposed to treat one — slowly, sceptically, one confirmed data point at a time.

Then the Uprising happened, in the files the way it had happened everywhere else: as a hard edge with nothing readable on either side of it for eleven months. When the correspondence resumed, it wasn't the same programme. The names had changed. The language had changed. Dimensional adjacency had become asymmetric resonance capability. Baseline mapping had become target-envelope modelling. Somewhere in that eleven-month gap, in the purges and the regime change and the general institutional scramble to prove itself indispensable to whoever now held the budget, a research team studying a strange physical phenomenon had been quietly folded into something that wanted to weaponise it, and the folder never once used a word that would have let a future reader see the seam. That was the part that sat wrong in her chest, worse than the weapons language itself. Nobody had lied. They had simply stopped saying certain things out loud, one memo at a time, until the silence did the work a lie would have had to do more honestly.

She read for three hours. She did not notice the three hours passing until her back told her, sharply, that it had.

By the end she had two facts that hadn't existed in her understanding of the world at midnight and did now, immovably, the way a fact does once you've read its proof. First: the Corporate Union had known about Meridian-adjacent physics for over twenty years — not suspected, not theorised, known, with instruments and confirmed readings and a paper trail that traced in an unbroken line to the anomaly she'd flagged nine months ago as though it were new. Second, and this was the one she kept returning to, turning it the way she'd turned her pulse over in her hands in this same chair nine months earlier: the programme had not started as a weapon. It had been made into one. The fossil in front of her was not evidence of an institution's original sin. It was evidence of a transformation — of what a handful of careful people had built, and what a different set of people, later, with different incentives, had made of their work without asking them.

She thought about her own threshold filters, tuned tight enough to catch exactly the right signal and nothing smaller. She thought about who might, in twenty years, read the memos she wrote this month and find the same seam in them that she'd just found here.

The flag was still sitting in the corner of her display where she'd left it that first night — a standing amber dot, dimmed but not cleared, exactly where "no action" had left it four minutes into a night that felt, from here, like it belonged to someone else's career. She pulled it up before she let herself think further about the archive open beside it.

"Status."

"Flag active. Unreviewed. Position in review queue: four thousand one hundred and twelve." The console offered the number without inflection, as if the size of it were not itself an answer. "Queue composition: cosmic-ray bit-flip artifacts, sixty-one percent. Scheduled-maintenance false positives, twenty-two percent. Uncategorised, seventeen percent. Estimated time to manual review at current staffing allocation: exceeds flag expiry."

So no one was coming. No one had been coming for nine months, and no one was going to come in the eighty-one days remaining before the flag aged out on its own — unless, between now and then, something drew a reviewer's eye to exactly this queue at exactly this position, which was precisely the kind of thing she was about to do to this archive's disposal schedule, multiplied by however many automated cross-references her own retention hold might trigger. She did not like leaving a live variable sitting next to the thing she was about to do. She had built the compliance interview process herself. She knew exactly how good it was at finding threads once someone gave it a reason to start pulling.

She reclassified the flag herself — false positive, resolved, reviewed — and backdated the resolution timestamp to a plausible slot eleven weeks earlier, buried in a week where the log showed she'd cleared forty similar items in a single sitting. It took under a minute. It was, she understood clearly as she did it, a different kind of act than the one she'd performed in this chair nine months ago. That first act had been a leak: information moving somewhere it wasn't supposed to go. This was something else — not evading the system's attention but editing it, reaching into the machinery that watched her and making a small, precise, entirely deniable correction to what it believed had already happened. She sat with that difference for a moment. It felt less like crossing a line than like discovering the line had a door in it she hadn't previously known how to open.

She turned back to the archive.

The routing took longer to decide than to execute. Amita's channel at the Gulf Crescent had carried small things before — fragments, single anomalies dressed as routine cargo data, nothing that would have justified more than a shrug if intercepted. This was different in scale: the full fossil record, twenty years of documentation, tagged and compressed and dressed as a scientific-instrumentation update bound for Lunar Accord humanitarian logistics review, the kind of unglamorous administrative traffic that moved between blocs a thousand times a day and drew exactly as much attention as its label suggested it should. She built the manifest wrapper the way she'd have built any other piece of infrastructure she wanted to survive contact with the world: plainly, without ornament, nothing about it inviting a second look.

"Sending," she told the console, the same word she'd used nine months ago, because there wasn't a better one.

"Manifest queued. Transit estimate: nine minutes."

She counted them, more or less. Not out of anxiety this time — she noticed the absence of the old tightness in her chest and filed it as data, the way she filed everything — but because nine minutes felt like a length of time that deserved to be witnessed rather than simply survived. Somewhere below the Spire, cargo drones she would never see were routing a package through customs corridors that had no idea what they were carrying. Somewhere past that, an orbital transfer window she hadn't checked but trusted Amita to have already confirmed was opening on schedule, the way it had opened on schedule for six months of shipments neither of them had ever discussed directly.

"Transit complete."

She closed the archive. The display went dark and returned her own reflection to her, dark hair, the ink stain that had never quite left the side of her hand no matter how many years separated her from the pen that put it there. She stood, crossed the tier, and looked out through the reinforced window at the Atlantic Financial Spine holding its cold, patient glow across the water, that glow throwing itself back off the adaptive flood walls in long unsteady sheets, silver breaking to gold and back to silver as the walls flexed against a tide that never fully stopped testing them. Beyond the walls, at low ebb, the drowned reef of the old district lay black under the scanners' slow amber sweep, the ruin and the tower trading light across the same stretch of water they'd been trading it across since long before she'd had a reason to notice.

The leak was not in this building anymore. It was not, technically, anywhere she could point to — no longer a file, no longer a decision she could still unmake by closing a window. It was in the network now, moving through customs corridors and orbital manifests and a chain of institutional inattention she had just spent the last hour both exploiting and repairing, and there was nothing left for her to do here tonight except notice, with the same flat professional part of her mind that logged everything else, that she felt something closer to relief than to fear. She had expected fear. She stood at the window a while longer, watching the walls hold, and let herself be mildly surprised by its absence.

Fourteen thousand kilometres east, in the upper levels of the retasked Opera House where the old sail-shaped roofline gave way to instrument decks nobody outside the Ring ever saw, Sienna TeAriki had her hands in a conduit housing that had no business needing her hands at all.

"You could let the diagnostic run," she said, not looking up, cement dust from an earlier job still pale along her knuckles where it hadn't washed clean, "instead of making me find this by feel."

"Diagnostic in progress," the Orbital Station's system said, warm and unhurried in the way every Lunar Accord system tried to be, whether or not the moment called for warmth. "Estimated completion: four minutes. You are already faster."

"I know I am." She found the loose junction by touch, seated it, and felt the relay's tension settle under her fingers the way a repaired thing always did — a small, physical agreement between metal and pressure that no diagnostic readout ever quite captured for her the way her own hands did. She'd spent sixteen years learning to trust that agreement over a display, and nothing about the Ring's newest instrumentation had yet talked her out of the habit.

She straightened, wiped her hands on the cloth she kept for exactly this, and turned back to the launch-coordination deck proper — six wide displays curved around a central tracking table, orbital paths threading and re-threading themselves in slow gold arcs above cislunar space, the whole room lit by screen glow and, past the deck's outer windows, the last low gold of evening lying across the harbour three hundred metres below. The lattice roof caught that light the way it always did at this hour, veined and faintly translucent, grown rather than built over a steel frame a century older than anyone currently working under it. She had stopped finding the contrast strange years ago — old bones, new skin, the whole Hub a single argument for the proposition that nothing here needed to choose between what it had been and what it had become.

The perturbation showed up on the tracking table as a flicker she almost dismissed before she'd finished seeing it.

"Flag that," she said, already leaning in. "Orbital envelope, Luna Haven approach corridor. Run it again."

"Confirmed. Gravitational perturbation, orbital envelope above Luna Haven. Magnitude: within background variance tolerance at ninety-one percent of monitored instances. This instance: ninety-four percent." The system's tone didn't change, but Sienna had worked this deck long enough to hear the shape of a genuine anomaly inside even the flattest confirmation. "Classification: technically deniable at current confidence threshold."

Small. That was the honest word for it — smaller than the anomaly readings she'd chased through this same table nine months ago, smaller than anything that would justify waking a duty officer at this hour. But she'd been managing this deck long enough to know that small and nothing were different categories, and that the gap between them was usually where the real work started. She pulled the timestamp and sat with it a moment, turning it the way she turned a suspect weld before deciding whether to trust it.

Nothing here told her what had caused it. She had no channel into whatever New Manhattan's Surveillance Spire was or wasn't doing at this hour, no reason to connect a fourteen-thousand-kilometre-distant data transfer to a gravitational flicker above a colony she'd never once needed to think about in the same breath as the Corporate Union's internal traffic. The correlation, if it was one, sat entirely outside anything her instruments were built to see. What she had was a number, a timestamp, and sixteen years of trained instinct telling her that a precisely timed anomaly was a different animal from a random one even when nothing in front of her could yet say why.

"Route to Dr. Fournier's research channel," she said. "Flag as data point, unclassified priority. Note the timing precision — I want it in the record even if it turns out to be nothing."

"Routed. Delivery confirmed to Meridian Levels research archive." A pause, the system's version of consideration. "Would you like a follow-up review scheduled?"

"Seventy-two hours." She looked back at the tracking table, where the flicker had already smoothed itself back into the calm gold weave of a hundred ordinary orbital paths, indistinguishable now from everything around it. "If it happens again before then, wake me."

"Understood."

She went back to the conduit housing, because the relay still needed closing up properly and the diagnostic, for all its patience, wasn't going to do that part for her. Below the deck's windows, the harbour kept its evening changeover running on schedule — skiffs docking, the terraced shore catching its last direct light, the whole Hub doing, at ground level, exactly what it had been built to do regardless of what a single flicker on an instrument three hundred metres up might or might not turn out to mean. Sienna worked the housing closed with hands that knew the shape of the job without needing to be told, and did not think again, that evening, about the four minutes it had taken a piece of paperwork somewhere on the other side of the planet to become the network's problem instead of one woman's alone.
