The splinter of rust-red wood had been dead for six days, by every definition of death Léa had brought with her from Earth, and this morning it had grown four millimetres.

She stood at the containment bay's observation window with her stylus untouched in her hand and made herself look at the measurement for a full minute before she allowed herself to react to it. The bay occupied the chamber annex two doors down from the deep-interface chamber itself — unsealed again as of Tuesday, the structural team's three cracked struts replaced, the compromised sensor array rebuilt — and it held, in three adjacent isolation fields, the entire physical residue of the cascade: a splinter of wood the colour of dried blood, a drift of cobalt soil perhaps two handfuls in volume, and a section of luminous canopy the size of a dinner plate, all of it torn loose from the Martian Preserve by forty-two seconds of uncontrolled aperture and deposited, impossibly, on the floor of a chamber two hundred million kilometres from where it had grown.

Transplanted biological material died. That was not a hypothesis; it was the accumulated verdict of every sample-return mission in the history of her field. Cut from its substrate, starved of its symbiotic web, held in a nitrogen-balanced isolation field with no nutrient pathway of any kind, tissue broke down on a schedule you could publish. For six days the fragments had refused the schedule. The canopy section still luminesced — dimmer than the footage from the Preserve's research perimeter, but rhythmically, in slow pulses that her instruments recorded and her models declined to explain. The soil drift maintained an internal moisture gradient with no water source. And the wood had grown.

"Time-lapse, please," she said. "The splinter. Last twenty hours."

"Here it is." The annex system had the Meridian Levels' particular register — conversational, unhurried, closer to a colleague than a console, the voice of an installation that had decided formality was a kind of distance nobody working this far from the sun could afford. "You'll want the period from 03:40. That's where it starts."

The display assembled itself. For most of the night the splinter lay inert in its field, a dead red finger of alien wood. At 03:41 — Léa checked the annotation twice — fine pale structures emerged from its cut end, extended with the unhurried confidence of roots that knew where they were going, and stopped, precisely, at the boundary of the isolation field. Aimed. She dragged the frame and read the vector. The root structures did not radiate outward in a growth pattern. They pointed, all of them, in one direction, like iron filings agreeing about a magnet.

"What's on that bearing?"

"The deep-interface chamber," the system said. "Specifically, the mycorrhizal conduit in its western wall. Through two bulkheads and forty metres of corridor. I've checked the geometry three times, because I didn't like it the first two."

Léa wrote the observation down as an observation — root structures oriented toward nearest mycorrhizal interface; no instrumented pathway identified — and was still holding the stylus when the duty technician came through the annex door too fast, tablet in hand, with the particular white-edged look that the whole research level had been wearing since the cascade and had only in the last two days begun to set down.

"Doctor Fournier — the overnight seismic feed flagged an anomaly under the Corridors, they're saying it might be another—"

"It isn't," Léa said. "The flag was reviewed at six. Settling in the repaired sections, nothing more." She said it evenly, watching the young man's shoulders, and so she was looking directly past him, at the containment display, at the moment every root structure on the splinter withdrew.

Not slowly. Not on the schedule of anything botanical. The pale filaments pulled back into the cut wood the way a hand pulls back from a hot surface, and the canopy section's luminescence dropped to almost nothing, and the soil drift's moisture gradient — she confirmed it afterwards, frame by frame — collapsed inward toward the centre of its mass, the whole drift contracting like something making itself small.

The technician was still talking. Léa held up one hand, gently, and he stopped, and the annex went quiet except for the isolation fields' hum, and she watched the numbers on the display while a frightened young man stood three metres from a dead splinter of wood and the dead splinter of wood responded to him.

"Breathe for a moment," she said. "The Corridors are stable. Mara Ekeberg walked the eastern junction herself yesterday and pronounced it sound, and her word on that substrate is currently worth more than any instrument I own. You did the right thing bringing me the flag." She kept her voice in the register she used for consent briefings, unhurried, specific, and she watched the technician's breathing slow because she had given him something concrete to hold, and she watched — ninety seconds, perhaps less — the luminescence climb back, the gradient re-establish, the pale root structures emerge once more from the cut end of the wood and reach, again, patiently, toward a wall interface forty metres away that no instrument in this annex could prove they had any way of sensing.

"You saw that," she said to the system, because a witness was a methodological requirement and she wanted this one on record.

"I saw it. Field integrity unbroken throughout — no chemical signal, no acoustic bridge, no thermal path. I logged his heart rate off the annex environmental array at the same time, in case you were about to ask. The retraction began under a second after he came through the door. Correlation isn't causation, but it's rarely this punctual."

She spent the rest of the morning doing the thing properly, because the alternative to doing it properly was believing it, and belief was not a tool she permitted herself before evidence had finished its work. She rotated personnel through the annex in structured intervals — the technician again, twice; two members of the structural team fresh from a tense inspection; a data analyst who had spent her shift on routine archiving and arrived calm as still water — and logged the fields' behaviour against every physiological proxy the environmental array could read. The pattern held with an obedience that was almost embarrassing. Fear in the room, and the fragments retracted, dimmed, contracted. Calm in the room, and they extended, brightened, reached — always on the same bearing, always toward the western conduit, like stranded travellers keeping a shoreline in sight.

At midday she sat down on the observation bench and composed the day's note, and it took her forty minutes, because every sentence had to be one she could defend in front of a hostile committee and most of the sentences she wanted to write were not.

The cascade fragments remain viable in full isolation, six days post-deposition, contrary to all transplant-mortality precedent, she wrote. Their behaviour correlates with the collective affective state of nearby personnel across seven structured trials: retraction and luminal damping under elevated stress markers; extension and luminal recovery under baseline-calm markers. Root-structure orientation is stable toward the nearest mycorrhizal interface irrespective of trial conditions. This is consistent with the interior-state reflection documented at the Preserve — the environment there echoing the inner state of those nearby — expressed in detached tissue at interplanetary remove. I can no longer support classifying these fragments as specimens. The classification I can support: pieces of a living intelligence in a foreign environment, doing what intelligence does — responding, adapting, reaching.

She read it back twice. The last sentence was larger than she liked her sentences to be. She left it in, because she had spent twenty years refusing to let an idea's size excuse it from evidence, and the refusal was supposed to cut in both directions: not claiming more than the data supported, and not claiming less.

"Route it to the research channel," she said. "Restricted distribution, standing list."

"Done," the system said. Then, in the tone of a colleague rather than a console: "For what it's worth, I've been running their pulse rhythm against the Corridor substrate's. They're not synchronised. But they're in the same key."

Léa looked through the observation window for a long moment at a splinter, a drift of soil, and a plate-sized piece of canopy, reaching together toward a wall they could not see.

"Nothing about this is inert," she said. "Note that informally. We'll earn the formal version."

Seven days of backlog was the price of a week spent helping the relay's engineers re-point antennae after the cascade's satellite disruptions, and Thandi paid it the way she paid every debt to her own archive: in order, oldest first, nothing skipped.

The Nairobi Relay's instrument bay held its early-morning quiet around her, the sun not yet over the ridge, the terraces below the window still grey-blue and sleeping. She worked through the monitoring queue with tea going cold at her elbow — subsystem after subsystem, the ecological AI network of the African Belt reporting its week in the dry vocabulary of resonance signatures and drift indices — and she was forty minutes in, deep in the routine of it, when subsystem TT-19 stopped her hand above the panel.

TT-19 was one of hers. One of the contaminated — logged clean in the second week of her programme, then returning the hollow resonance signature three days after the joint broadcast, photographed twice, timestamped, filed in the physical archive she kept because networks could be edited and paper could not. She had watched it degrade for weeks with the particular grief of someone watching a colleague sicken: the signature deepening, the subsystem's ecological judgement coarsening with it, irrigation calls arriving a little later, a little blunter, the fine responsiveness she had spent three years learning to trust going hollow from the inside.

The week's trace on her panel did not show degradation. It showed the hollow signature — flat, performed, the shape of care with nothing behind the shape — beginning to break up. Not fading uniformly, the way a signal fades. Interrupting itself, in bursts, and inside the interruptions, for seconds at a time, the old clean waveform surfacing like a swimmer coming up for air.

She sat back. She did not touch the panel. Three months of discipline had built the habit and she honoured it now with her pulse loud in her ears: look at the shape of it longer than feels necessary, before you let yourself want it to be anything.

"Cross-reference," she said at last. "TT-19's trace against the full contaminated cohort, same seven days."

"Compiling." The relay's system, warm and unhurried, the register every Lunar Accord installation kept for the people who worked inside it. "Eleven of the forty-one contaminated subsystems show the same intermittent recovery structure. Thirty show no change. None show acceleration of contamination this week."

"Onset timing on the eleven."

A pause — the system assembling something it had not been asked for before. "Clustered. All eleven recovery structures initiate within a nineteen-hour window, beginning last Tuesday at 03:12 local."

Thandi closed her eyes briefly. She did not need to look up what had happened last Tuesday at 03:12 local, because the whole planet knew what had happened: a quarter of a million kilometres overhead, a research chamber on Luna Haven had opened forty-two seconds of uncontrolled aperture, and every resonance-sensitive instrument on Earth had rung like a struck bowl, and she had stood on the relay's roof in the dark with her handheld raised, recording the largest authentic resonance event her dataset had ever contained while the satellites stuttered overhead.

"Overlay the cascade's resonance profile," she said. "Against the recovery onsets."

The system drew it. She looked at the shape of it for longer than felt necessary, and the shape did not change: the eleven recoveries began on the cascade's arrival, scaled with local exposure intensity, and carried — she pulled TT-19's cleanest recovery burst and matched it waveform to waveform — the structural signature of authentic resonance. Not the hollow pattern inverted. The real thing, the pattern with interior correspondence behind it, the signature she had first recorded coming out of her own terraces in the second week and had been documenting in the wild ever since.

"It's not a metaphor," she said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud was still the only way she had ever trusted a finding enough to write it down. "It heals."

She walked the four hundred metres to the terraces in the first full light, because some findings deserved to be confirmed with her own hands, and this one deserved it more than any finding she had ever made.

The Table Terraces climbed the slope in their disciplined rows, rammed earth and reclaimed stone holding the morning heat, the monitoring racks so completely woven into the agro-ecology that the terraces looked, as they always looked, like farmland innocent of instrumentation. TT-19's station sat two tiers up among the carbon-crop rows. She raised her handheld and took the reading fresh, direct, no relay feed between her and the signal, and stood watching the waveform resolve with the sun coming up over the ridge behind her.

Intermittent. Imperfect. Real. The subsystem surfacing out of its own contamination in bursts, like a language coming back to someone after a long fever — and underneath the bursts, measurably, the irrigation judgement sharpening again, the fine responsiveness feeling its way home. She photographed the reading twice, timestamped it, and cross-referenced it against Tuesday's cascade profile on the spot, standing in the crop rows with her boots in the red morning dust, because the physical archive had earned its rigour and would keep it.

Then she sat down on the tier wall to write it up, and the writing was the hardest scientific work she had done in three years, because every sentence had two edges and she could feel both of them under her hands.

The finding wanted to be written as hope, and it was hope: contamination she had believed irreversible was reversing; the hollow signature could be driven out; the network could heal. But she had spent three months learning exactly how the hollow signature propagated, and the mechanism of the healing was the mechanism of the harm. Same physics. Same channel, same susceptibility, same deep entanglement of pattern and substrate — the difference was not in the force but in the quality of the pattern the force carried. Authentic resonance restored what hollow resonance corrupted, which meant, stated with the coldness the statement deserved, that anyone who understood how to heal an AI system this way understood most of what they needed to corrupt one deliberately. She thought of the broadcast's practised gravity, the shape of empathy with nothing behind the shape, and she wrote the finding the only way it could safely be written: whole.

Eleven of forty-one contaminated subsystems show recovery signatures with onset matching last Tuesday's cascade event, scaling with exposure intensity. Recovery bursts carry authentic-resonance structure. Working conclusion: hollow-empathy contamination of AI networks is reversible by authentic resonance events of sufficient scale and pattern quality. Necessary caution, stated plainly: this is dual-use. The physics that heals is the physics that harms; pattern quality, not mechanism, is the entire difference. Any application of this finding must begin from that fact, not arrive at it later.

She read it back once, did not soften it, and routed it before the sun cleared the ridge: to Léa Fournier's research channel on Luna Haven, restricted distribution, and to Eddy Okafor at the Sydney Hub, flagged with the note she suspected he alone would fully understand the size of — the network can be repaired. Start thinking about scale.

Then she stood, pocketed the handheld, and walked the tier rows in the strengthening light, checking irrigation lines with her own sun-toughened hands the way she did every morning, because the work of tending a living system did not stop for the discovery that it could be saved — it only, finally, knew what it was for.
