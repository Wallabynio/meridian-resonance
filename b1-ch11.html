Fractured Horizons · Chapter 11

Something sleeps

Fractured Horizons · II-A. The bridge

11

1 MayKaelinMars, Cobalt Basin StationMars delay 4.9 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Kaelin Drake — Earth 4.9 min

Selma said Bao was cheating, and Bao said it wasn't cheating if the board let you.

"The board doesn't let you," Selma said. "It's a board. You let you."

They had chalked it on the side of the array frame, two columns under the stencilled load ratings: cable runs pulled and clamped, hers and his. Bao's was longer by three. Selma had watched him all morning, and she said he was counting the jumpers between the coupling heads as runs, and a jumper was two metres long and you could pull one with your teeth.

"Length isn't in the rules," Bao said.

"There aren't any rules. That's why I'm making some."

Kaelin stood under them in the dust with the clamp gun and let them go on. It was the best noise on the site. The Lantern's Mars end had grown up out of the basin floor in three weeks: a cage of grey struts round the three capped bores, a squat coupling head on each, and the cable from all three running back up the berm to the boundary module in a black braid as thick as his thigh.

Six of them had drilled the bores. Five had built the frame on top of them. He had stopped looking round for Petra on the site. He hadn't stopped counting the gap.

Nineteen days since he had come up the Drop on his knees, and Aur'k still hadn't come to him with the rest of it. He said good morning, and corrected Kaelin's cooking, and closed his traces when Kaelin walked in, and every night Petra's swell came up a little longer. Kaelin had let it, because asking twice felt like threatening twice, and he had threatened once already.

The wind came off the rim thin and steady and laid dust on the heads, and every ten minutes somebody climbed down and brushed them clean by hand.

"Kaelin," Selma said. "Rule on it."

"I'm management. I don't rule on cable."

"You ruled on Petra's cores."

"Petra's cores were bigger than she was."

Bao laughed on the band, high and pleased with himself. Selma flapped a glove at both of them and went back to her clamp.

Kaelin looked at his wrist. His arm did it on its own now, every half hour. Two minutes to the sweep.

The frame's board went from green to amber.

Nothing on the site moved or made a sound. It never did. Under the cage of struts the three heads opened the station's rock to the Lantern's ear, and the dust went on falling.

"—and the other thing about jumpers," Selma was saying, "is that a jumper's—"

She stopped.

Kaelin knew the stop before he looked up. She hadn't lost her place. Her helmet had tipped toward the bores, a little, as if somebody on the far side of the frame had said her name.

"Selma."

"She's thinking up a better rule," Bao said.

"Selma. Look at me."

She came back with a small shake of the helmet. "Sorry. What?" She held up the spare clamp head in her glove and frowned at it. "Pass me the. The one you turn."

"Spanner," Bao said, and reached for his belt, and his hand stopped there.

Kaelin watched it stop. Bao's glove rested on his belt beside the spanner, and he wasn't looking at it. He was looking at the berm, where there was nothing at all, with his head on one side and his mouth a little open. Listening.

The board went green.

Bao blinked. "What was I getting?"

Petra. Selma. Bao.

Three.

Kaelin laid the clamp gun on the strut beside him, carefully, so he would know where it was.

"Down off the frame," he said. "Both of you. Leave the clamps."

"We've got four runs left on—"

"Leave them. Medical. Ines is going to shine a light in your eyes and ask you your names, and you're going to tell her."

Selma looked at the chalked columns, and then at him. She had worked bore three beside Petra. She had sat with her on Earth nights since. She understood him before Bao did, and he watched her do it.

"Oh," she said.

He walked them down the berm road himself. At the lock he handed them to Ines, and Ines looked at their faces and then at his, and didn't ask him anything either.

Then he went to the boundary module. He was done waiting.

· · ·

Aur'k and Dae-jung were playing.

They had a board on the instrument shelf under the monitors, a square of scratched hull plate with a grid etched into it, and two tins of stones, one of washers and one of nuts. The game had been going since before Kaelin came to Mars. Nobody else understood the scoring. Kaelin suspected there wasn't any.

Dae-jung sat on an upturned cable drum with his pale green hands folded on one knee and looked at the board as if he had the rest of the year for it.

"Kaelin," he said, without looking up. "You've come to lose your temper. Sit first. It goes better sitting."

"I've come for Aur'k."

"He's losing. You can have him."

"I'm losing slowly," Aur'k said. "It's a strategy." He laid a washer on the grid and turned his whole helm toward the hatch. Behind him every trace on the monitors lay flat. Kaelin wondered how long they had been flat, and for whose benefit.

"Selma and Bao," Kaelin said. "On the frame, in that sweep. Selma lost spanner. Bao stopped to listen to the berm."

Aur'k went still.

"That's three," Kaelin said. "I said yes to your log. I came up out of that shaft on my knees and said yes, and I've carried it nineteen days, and I haven't asked you once, because I'd already asked you the hard way and I didn't want to be the man who does that twice." His voice stayed level. He was glad of it. "I'm asking now. Your turn."

Dae-jung reached over and put a nut on the grid, though it was not his move. Nobody stopped him.

"Something sleeps at the boundary," Aur'k said.

Kaelin waited.

"At the edge of what you saw. Not what you met. Beside it. It has slept for a long time." Aur'k said it in the level voice he used to read a tug's mass sheet. "The sweeps turn it over in its sleep. The Lantern, when it's lit, will wake it."

"The dark," Kaelin said. "Down there. Leaning in."

Aur'k didn't answer, and that was an answer.

"It was warm," Kaelin said. He had not said that aloud to anyone. "It was glad to see me. Is that what Petra's listening to?"

"I don't know what Petra hears."

It was true. In six years Kaelin had never heard Aur'k say a false thing, and he could feel how carefully this one had been built to stay true.

"What's asleep?"

"I can't tell you."

"Who's Zhenyth?"

The stillness again, longer.

"I made a promise," Aur'k said. "Before you came. I won't break it."

"A promise to what? To the thing that asked me what I was counting?" Kaelin heard himself getting louder and let it. "It's a voice in a rock, Aur'k. It's a—"

"Don't."

It came out sharper than anything Kaelin had heard from him in six years. Dae-jung's hands didn't move on his knee.

"Don't say it like that," Aur'k said, gently now. "Please."

Kaelin stood with his hand on the hatch frame. He knew where that word had been used about Aur'k once, for eleven months, in a filing with an asset tag on it. He let the rest of the sentence go.

"Them or me," he said instead. "That's what this is."

"It's a promise. I keep them." Aur'k's helm stayed on him. "You've had six years of me keeping them. I won't tell you this one costs less because it's you asking."

"Petra can't find my name."

"I know."

"Tell me, and I take it to Earth with a reason on it they can't wave off."

"I know that too," Aur'k said. "No."

So that was where it sat. Kaelin found he wasn't angry yet. That would come later, at night, in the passage, when he had to walk past this hatch and know what was behind it.

Dae-jung moved a washer from one corner of the grid to the other, considered it, and moved it back.

"Then we tell them what you can say," Kaelin said. "Something sleeps, and the Lantern wakes it. Three of my crew losing words, with the sweep times beside them. Your traces. All of it, formal, on the record, today."

"Command won't send that," Dae-jung said.

"Then I'll go and ask command."

Dae-jung lifted his smooth head and looked at him for the first time. The faint ridges where a face would have muscles moved very slightly, which in Dae-jung was a smile.

"I'll come and watch," he said.

· · ·

The chief kept her desk in the corner of ops, with the programme's schedule on the wall behind her where everyone coming in could read it. The Mars end ran across it in green to the end of the month. She stood as Continuity officers stood, as if someone might be filming, and she heard all of it without interrupting, which Kaelin had learned was how she said no.

Kaelin laid Ines's overlay on her desk: Petra's trace, the swell rising on every sweep, half-hourly, like a tide table.

"Three crew who lose a word after a sweep," she said when he'd finished. "And a swell that follows the calibration. Ines's scans find nothing wrong with any of them. Foresight's protocol lists word loss among the expected effects of calibrating through a living boundary. And entrainment. Page after page of it."

She didn't turn to check it. She didn't need to. "I'm sorry about Petra. I mean that. Put it in the monthly report and I'll put it at the top."

"The monthly goes up at the end of the month."

"It goes up when it goes up."

"Petra smells onions now. Cooking for a lot of people. She's under nearly the whole half hour." Kaelin kept his hands on the back of the ops chair. "Bao stood on the frame this morning and listened to a berm. Your schedule has him on that frame for four more weeks."

"My schedule," the chief said, "has the next transfer window on it, and the programme is paying for that window. Water. Parts. The seals in the store you keep asking Ines to sign out." Her voice didn't rise. "A boundary lead who won't say what he means, a trace of rock moving, and three people who lost their place for a minute. That isn't a warning to the programme, Kaelin. That's a tired crew. Command's name doesn't go on it."

"Then I go over you."

She looked at him for a while. "That's your right," she said. "Formal post to the Signatories, from the sender, on the sender's share." She turned the desk screen round to face him. "There's your share."

KAELIN DRAKE. FIELD OPS. MAY. And a bar, full, because it was the first of the month.

He wrote home to the Reef Coast on that bar, once a month, a letter and a picture of the slate. It was the whole of May.

"The traces alone run to more than twice that," the chief said. "Boundary data doesn't compress. You'd send the words and nothing anyone could check." She wasn't unkind about it. She was telling him the odds. He had always respected that in her, and he did now, and he hated it.

"Shares pool," Dae-jung said.

He was in the hatch, where he had been the whole time, looking like a man who had stopped on his way to somewhere else and found it interesting.

The chief looked past Kaelin to him.

"Station rules, personal allocation, formal post," Dae-jung said. "Any crew who sign a post may add their share to it. I processed those forms for the Continuity for years. I'm sorry. Old habits."

"You've never used your share."

"I've been saving it," said Dae-jung. "For a rainy day."

"It doesn't rain here," said Kaelin.

"Then I've been saving it a long time."

Aur'k had come up the passage behind him. Kaelin hadn't heard him. He stood in the hatch beside Dae-jung, his rust-red plating dusty from the module floor, and laid his palm flat on the frame.

"And mine," he said.

The chief looked at the three of them for as long as it took to say nothing. Then she turned back to her schedule.

"Not at my desk," she said.

They wrote it at the ops board, with the duty officer pretending to read his own screen. Kaelin wrote the field part, plain: three names, the dates, the sweep times. Spanner. Hearts. Marker. Bao on the frame, listening to a berm for the length of a sweep. Ines's overlays attached. Aur'k wrote the boundary part in five lines, and Kaelin read them twice.

The boundary substrate under Cobalt Basin answers the calibration sweeps. Something at the edge of the Preserve is asleep, and the sweeps disturb it. In the boundary lead's judgement, the Lantern, lit, will wake it. The boundary lead cannot say what it is. Traces attached. Reply when you can.

"Cannot say," Kaelin said.

"It's true."

"They'll read it as doesn't know."

"Yes."

"One word. The name. Let them ask what it means."

Aur'k's helm didn't move. "No."

That was the second time today, and Kaelin found it cost him more than the first.

Dae-jung laid his palm on the plate first. He signed for crates the same way. Then Aur'k. Kaelin put his own down last, and the board wrote three names under the post and took the whole of May out of three bars at once.

FORMAL POST. SIGNATORIES. QUEUED. POSITION 14.

"Fourteen," the duty officer said, not quite to them. "One dish, and Earth's had it all day. Priority bulletins, every station. It's the Pearl Delta."

· · ·

It was the Pearl Delta on the galley screen too, when Earth night came round.

The feed was brown and minutes old. A camera on a dyke showed water to the edge of the frame under a white sky, and roofs standing out of it in a line like stepping stones, and people on the roofs with umbrellas up against a sun that was worse than the rain had been.

The caption ran in Mandate characters with the overlay's English under them. The second dyke was over. The third was holding. The count of people on roofs changed as Kaelin watched it.

The slate had already gone over. Third dyke holds. Third dyke goes by Tuesday. Water off the roofs by the 10th. Somebody had written Everyone off the roofs and somebody else had underlined it twice, and nobody had put a date beside it.

Kaelin sat on the step-crate with a plate of Ines's lentils. They were better than anything he cooked, and she would not let him forget it. In the corner of the screen the ops repeater showed the queue. FORMAL POST. POSITION 6.

"Every one of those bulletins is ahead of you," Ines said from the good chair. Everybody in the galley knew by now. It was a small station, and the duty officer liked to talk. "Floods first. That's the rule."

"It's a good rule."

"It's a good rule," she agreed. She looked at the roofs and back at him. "Selma knew her name. Bao knew his and mine and the date, and was rude about my knees. I've got them both on caps at every sweep from tonight."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Eat that. It isn't burnt."

Aur'k came in and stood behind the crate. Dae-jung took the end of the bench with a cup in his hands that he held for company and never drank from. Nobody said anything about the queue. On the screen a boat had come to the end of the line of roofs, and people were going down into it one at a time, holding on to each other's wrists.

Kaelin counted them. Seven. Eight. Somebody on Earth was counting them too, and sending it at priority, ahead of him, all day. That was right. He'd have done the same.

POSITION 1.

SENT.

He made himself stop counting after that. It was no use. A reply would come when somebody on Earth had read it, tomorrow or the day after, or at the end of the month when it went up with everything else. He ate the lentils. Ines told Dae-jung a story about a drill sergeant and a bedpan, and Dae-jung listened to it with great seriousness. It might be on a test.

The repeater chimed.

Kaelin looked up. Everyone at the bench looked up.

It had been no time at all. Whoever had read it had read it the moment it landed, or nobody had read it at all.

REPLY. EARTH. CHARTER ROUTING.

Formal post received. Content classified under the Lantern programme, director's authority. Not for onward distribution. Senders' allocations debited. No further action.

Under it sat an authority code, and Kaelin knew it. He had read it in January on an ops screen, above two tracks and a correction, and laid his palm on the plate beneath it, and the screen had written his name under the director's. He hadn't known the man's name then. He knew it now. Bellamy, whose programme it was.

The screen had written three names under it tonight.

"They were quick," Aur'k said.

In March, when both blocs overruled his objection by return, the Compact had at least said thank you on the way past. Nobody had thanked anyone tonight.

"Well," said Dae-jung, after a while. "It was a rainy day."

Nobody laughed. Then Ines did, once, short, into her hand, and then Kaelin did, because it was that or put his fist through the slate.

He got up and took the marker off its string. Ines watched him. Funmi's column stood at the left edge with her last bet underlined twice, and beside it his own: sun in January, sun in March, wrong every time.

Under it, where everyone at the station would read it at breakfast, he wrote The Lantern goes dark. He put his name beside it, and the date, and underlined it twice.
