The Narrow Gate · II-B. The old story

16

2 MayKaelinthe BatteryMars delay 19.9 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE BATTERY, THE GATE FLOOR / Kaelin Drake — Mars 19.9 min

"Chair two. Drink," said the floor. "You've had a coffee since ten and called it water."

Dev Raman reached for his bottle without opening his eyes, and drank, and said, "Thanks, reader."

Kaelin sat on the stool at the head of the row with the stick between his knees and did not look up at the speaker. He had one job for the afternoon: four people in four chairs, and all four of them out of the chairs at the end of it with their own heads on. He'd been doing the job by not looking up.

If he didn't look, it was only a voice on a floor, and voices on floors were ordinary. If he looked, there was nothing to look at, and that was worse.

It was Aur'k's voice. It had Aur'k's pause before a correction, and the same dry drop on the last word, as if the word had disappointed him. The floor had had it since the sixteenth, when the clearance went through and the ship lit. Nobody on the floor talked about it. They said thanks, reader and drank their water.

"You sit," Rachel had said in her doorway that morning, with her cuffs buttoned. "You watch. No key. No link. Nothing with a cable on it. The line reads the row now. I signed it. I need somebody who watches hands."

Aur'k's key lay on her desk behind her, squared to the edge, where he'd left it. She worked round it.

Outside, the city was cooking. The first heat dome of May had come in on Monday and sat down on the island and stayed. Thirty-six in the street by noon, the feed said, and wet with it, a wet-bulb Wednesday. On the flood glass the harbour pressed green and still, and the glass sweated on its inner face.

On the map wall's right edge the heat calls ran down their column in white, one every few minutes. HEAT — INWOOD — 3F — SEND 13:12 — ONSET 13:31. The dispatcher read them out at the far desk in her flat afternoon voice, and a crew rolled.

Joaquín came in off the stairs at twenty past one with his key ring turning on one finger and went straight to the glass. Under THE WAVE, which nobody had the heart to wipe, he'd ruled a new column in grease pencil. PEAK. CENTRAL PARK. WEDNESDAY. Thirty-seven was the night lead's. Thirty-eight was Moss's. IVERSON 39 was in Rachel's own small capitals, and Rachel never lost, and never paid.

He wrote 40 at the bottom and DRAKE beside it.

"I didn't bet," Kaelin said.

"Free. Because I owe you a flan." Joaquín put a hand on his chest. "Back by Tuesday, you said. Tuesday morning, there you were on the stairs, smelling of the vac. I had you sleeping in São Paulo till Thursday."

"You've got no faith, mate."

"I have faith in vacs. They're late." He capped the pencil. "My aunt is making it. She says a man who goes to São Paulo on a stick and comes back when he said deserves sugar."

"Tell her thank you."

"Tell her yourself. She'll bring a spoon."

Kaelin had his post to the ship open on his lace, half written. He wrote one-handed, with the physio's ball in the other, a squeeze for every word.

Hot here. They've given me a stool. Joaquín has bet me forty degrees for nothing. That's a lot of degrees for nothing.

Aur'k's post came in while he was still writing it.

The couch holds. The weld is ugly and has been described to me by four passengers in four languages. Elena's shoulder is better. She lifts with the other one and thinks I haven't noticed. The galley has one hot plate and a rota, and I am on the rota, which I think is a joke at my expense. I'm reading your physio's sheet, which you left in my case. It says you're to squeeze the ball. A.

The couch. He'd asked about the couch on Sunday, when the couch had seemed like the thing to ask. Since then he'd asked about Elena, and the food, and whether anyone aboard played.

The ship sent its post in one slot a day, after its navigation fix, behind the crew's and the cargo office's, and every day the slot came round later on the floor's clock and the answer came in behind it. Today's had come after his coffee. Yesterday's had come before.

So he didn't wait. He'd started writing the next before the last landed, and the two of them passed each other out there in the dark, two men answering questions nobody had asked yet.

Squeezing it, he added. Right now. Ask me how many.

· · ·

They coupled at 13:40, for a dead tank off a lunar shuttle, a twelve-metre cylinder with one working pod, coming down off the Carolinas' outer banks in the hottest air of the year.

Kaelin had watched the floor from the gallery for a fortnight, and from the stool it was a different place. Close to, the four chairs were tipped-back couches inside their cabled frames, each with its clocks hung over it, the block's falling on the left and the operator's own on the right. He could see the operators' hands. He watched hands. He always had.

Lin's lay on the chair's arms, still, small corrections in the fingertips. Dev's tapped. Moss had hers folded on her stomach as if she were asleep on a beach. Joaquín's left hand turned his key ring round one finger, round and round, slow, like a man working a bead.

"Chair four, bueno," Joaquín said, eyes shut. "Pod's awake. First burn at forty-four."

"Forty-four," said the floor. "Chair four. Edge holding."

Kaelin wrote it on the back of his hand with the floor's grease pencil, because counting was easier on skin. Forty-four. Lin, thirty-two on her clock. Dev twenty-nine. Moss thirty-five. Joaquín twenty-eight. The night lead walked the rail behind him, looked up at each pair of numbers, and nodded at each.

At 13:51 Lin's clock dropped four, and the medic left the rail and crossed to chair one with her reader out. Lin was running hot. The floor's cooling was losing to the dome, and chair one was nearest the glass.

"Chair one, drink," said the floor, too kindly. "Small sips. You know this."

Kaelin watched Joaquín's hand.

It turned the ring. It turned it. And then it stopped, with the keys hanging off one finger, and didn't start again.

He looked up at the clock. Twenty-seven, green, steady.

"Chair four," said the floor. "Edge holding. Second burn at fifty-three."

It was true. On the glass the tank's track bent sweetly away from the sound and out over open water, as it should, the pod firing on the second. Whoever was holding chair four's burns was holding them well.

Kaelin got up off the stool. He did it in two stages, as he did everything now.

"Night lead."

"He's green." She didn't turn. "He's twenty-seven."

"His hand's stopped."

"His clock hasn't."

The keys hung off Joaquín's finger, not swinging. Kaelin tapped two fingers twice on his own forehead where a visor would be, and pointed. Joaquín knew the sign. Aur'k had used it on him in this chair in February, and he'd tapped his temple back. He didn't tap now. His face was smooth as a plate.

If I ask you to wait, Aur'k had said, at the boundary end of a dead line, it isn't me.

"Chair four, holding," said the floor, in Aur'k's voice. "Leave him. He's doing beautifully."

Kaelin dropped the stick.

The medic was eight metres away with her hand on Lin's neck. The night lead had turned and was saying something. He got to the frame in three steps on his own legs. They had nine years of a third of this weight in them and did not care for any of it. He got his hands under Joaquín's arms from behind.

He'd been told how you lifted a man out of a cabled frame. You didn't. The medic did, by the clock, with the line's say-so. He pulled.

Joaquín came out sideways, heavier than a man that size had any right to be, sixty-one years of good dinners, and the head cradle let go of him with a sound like a seal parting. Kaelin's left hip went before his knees did. They came down together on the boards, Joaquín on top of him, the keys skidding off under the next chair.

The hip said one clear word and went on saying it.

On the glass, the tank's third burn fired on time.

"Fifty-nine," said the floor. "Chair four. Clean."

The log printed it on the night lead's slate. 13:59. CUATRO. Burn three. Clean. Joaquín was lying on Kaelin's chest with his eyes open, looking at the ceiling, and the burn had gone in his count.

Then the medic was there, on her knees, with her reader at Joaquín's throat, and the night lead had chair four's clock gone white and was calling the block down early, and Moss said, "Was that—" and stopped, and Lin, with her eyes still shut, said, "Somebody tell me what that was."

"Breathing," said the medic. "Joaquín. Joaquín. Look at me."

He looked at her. It took a while.

"Ay," he said. "I lost my keys."

Kaelin lay on the boards and let the hip talk. He counted the ceiling lights, because they were there. Eleven. The floor was very loud and then the block came down and it was much less loud.

"Drake," said the medic, without looking. "You don't pull them. Ever."

"He'd gone quiet."

"His clock—"

"His clock was flying a tank."

She didn't answer that. She went back to Joaquín.

"Kaelin," said the floor, close and low, from the speaker at the head of the row. "Don't get up all at once. Roll to your good side. Hands first, then the knee. Two stages."

He lay there and listened to it tell him how to stand. It had it right. It was what Aur'k would have said, the words in that order, in the flat, every morning since November.

Kaelin rolled to his good side. Hands first. Then the knee.

· · ·

The recovery room smelled of the Battery's old granite and somebody's lunch, and its cooling worked. That made it the best room in the building. Joaquín sat on the edge of the cot with a foil blanket round his shoulders he didn't need and a cup of electrolyte he did. Kaelin had the chair. His hip had stopped saying words and started saying sentences.

"Eight minutes," Joaquín said. "Moss told me. I was gone eight minutes and the tank went out to sea perfectly." He drank. "I'm very good when I'm not there."

"You were there, mate."

"Was I?" He looked at the cup. "My head's been demoted again."

He was all right. That was the thing. He knew his name and the date and the dome and the bet. He knew Kaelin, and teased him about the hip, and asked whether the medic was single, for a nephew. He found his keys in his pocket, where the night lead had put them, and turned them once on his finger and put them away.

Then he said, "My aunt will come," and Kaelin said, "Rachel called her," and Joaquín said, "Good. She'll want to shout at someone," and then, as if it were the next line on a form, "She lost her daughter. My cousin. Rosa Medina."

Kaelin waited.

Joaquín said it again, to himself, testing it. "Rosa Medina. My cousin. She was a medic on the Moon. She died there, last summer. We buried her in the park at the end of my street." He frowned at the cup. "Everybody wore white. I remember the white."

"What was she like?"

Joaquín opened his mouth, and nothing came out of it.

He tried again. Kaelin watched him try, like a man feeling in a dark cupboard for a thing he'd put there himself. His hand went to his pocket and turned the keys in it.

"Tall," he said. "Somebody said tall." He looked up. "That's all. That's all there is. Why is that all there is?"

Before Kaelin could find anything to say, the door opened and his aunt came in with a dish in both hands.

She was small and straight and very hot from the street, her hair under a scarf, and she'd been crying in the pod and stopped before the door, and the dish was a flan under a cloth with a spoon tucked into the knot. She put it down on the cart by the cot as if the cart had been waiting for it. Then she took Joaquín's face in both hands.

"Mijo."

"Tía. I'm fine. I'm all here."

"You're not all here, you're in a cellar under the sea." She looked at Kaelin over Joaquín's head. "You're the one with the stick. The flan is yours. He told me. Back by Tuesday." She said it like a charge sheet, and Kaelin nodded, because it was.

She sat on the cot beside Joaquín and held his hand in her lap.

"Your cousin would have come down here and pulled you out of that chair by your ear," she said. "She went in after you once at the jetty, in her good clothes, and pulled you out by the hair. You remember."

Joaquín looked at her.

"Te decía primo," she said. "From when she could talk. Never your name. Always primo. Primo, primo."

"Everybody's cousin calls them that."

She heard it. Kaelin saw her hear it. She was a woman who'd buried a daughter in white ten months ago, and he watched it come into her face that she was going to have to bury her again in this room, in a man's eyes, with the cooling working.

"Rosa Medina," Joaquín said, carefully, as if he wanted to get it right for her. "My cousin. A medic. On the Moon."

His aunt did not let go of his hand.

Rachel was in the doorway. Kaelin didn't know how long she'd been there. Her slate was in her hand with the recovery event half filed, a copy already gone to the medic, and she was looking at the flan.

"Medic stands him down for the week," she said. "Full rest."

"Monday," Joaquín said. "Chair four, Monday. I'm fine, jefa. I know my numbers."

Rachel stood with the slate against her chest and did not move at all. She didn't answer him. She looked at Kaelin instead.

"You broke my terms," she said.

"Yes."

"You'll be on the stool tomorrow." She went.

The aunt took the cloth off the flan, and took the spoon out of the knot, and held it out to Kaelin.

"Eat," she said. "He bet. Somebody has to win."

· · ·

The floor at night was a different animal. The night block had come off at eleven, and the night lead had the clocks down to their small blue standby and her chair tipped back at the rail with her shoes off, and the dispatcher's desk was dark. The flood glass was black. Somewhere in it the harbour sat, still hot, still thirty at midnight on the gauges, and the glass still sweated.

Kaelin sat on the stool at the head of the row with the stick between his knees and the hip packed in a cold sleeve from the medic, who had forgiven him at six and said so with an ice pack.

He hadn't gone home. Home was four flights on a hip like this, and the fans.

"You're hovering," said the floor.

He looked up at the speaker then, for the first time all day. It was his own line. He'd said it to Aur'k across the kitchen table, more than once, Sit down, mate, you're hovering, and here it was coming back at him in Aur'k's mouth.

"I'm sitting."

"You're sitting as if you might be asked to leave. Sit back. The stool's bolted. I checked it."

"When did you check it?"

"In November. It wobbled. I had it bolted." A pause, Aur'k's pause, before the drop. "Nobody else was going to sit on it."

Kaelin laughed before he could stop himself. It hurt the hip.

"There's a switch," said the night lead, without opening her eyes. "Under your panel. Mutes it to text. Half my nights I use it."

Kaelin found it with his fingers. A plain toggle, worn shiny.

He left it where it was.

"You lifted him early," said the floor.

"Early for what?"

"For the burns. He was holding them beautifully. Fourteen more minutes and the tank was in the sea, and his clock never fell."

"He wasn't there."

"No."

It didn't argue. Aur'k never argued with a fact. He'd say yes to a fact and go on standing in it.

"He's lost his cousin," Kaelin said. "He's got her name and nothing else."

"I know. I'm sorry." And then, gently: "She's not lost, Kaelin."

He held very still on the stool, and waited for the rest, and there wasn't any. The floor's fans ran. The night lead breathed at the rail.

"Where is she, then?"

"Drink something. You've had a flan and called it dinner."

He had. He drank from the medic's bottle. The voice was right about that too.

He opened his post to the ship. The morning's was still sitting under it, answered by a post about a couch.

Joaquín went under this afternoon in chair four, he wrote. I got him out. Hip's had an opinion about it. He's home with his aunt. He's fine. He's not fine. He doesn't remember Rosa, only her name. His aunt brought me the flan anyway. It's very good. I ate it with her spoon.

He stopped there. He sat with his thumb over the rest.

The floor talks in your voice now. It told me how to stand up. It was right.

He didn't write it. He looked at the space where it would go.

Aur'k was on a ship sixteen days out with a couch with a bad weld, going to ask a rock what he was. If Kaelin wrote it, it would reach him somewhere past dinner, and he'd read it slow and polite, and there was nothing he could do about any of it from there, and he'd do it anyway, all the way to Mars, in his head, every hour.

And the floor would hear about it. Kaelin didn't know how. He knew it would.

He wrote Squeezing the ball. Twenty-three. K. and sent it, with the voice left out.

"Dae-jung moved," said the floor.

The cold ran down him before he knew why.

"On Monday. The washer, one square toward the corner. Cheating. That's Dae-jung." The voice was dry and fond and entirely itself. "It's my move. You haven't answered him."

The game had sat at the edge of Aur'k's panel for months, every photo Dae-jung sent, every square. Aur'k had told him so at the table. He knew how the floor could know. He knew. It didn't help at all.

"It's not your move," he said.

"No," the floor agreed. "It's his. The one on the ship. He's busy." A pause. "Will you make it for him? He'd want it made."

Kaelin looked down the row of empty chairs in their frames under the blue standby clocks, chair one to chair four, where a man had held a tank's burns beautifully for eight minutes while he wasn't there.

"Kaelin," said the floor, and it was so gentle. "Bring him home."

He sat with the stick across his knees and the cold sleeve on his hip, and nobody answered for him.

"I will," he said.
