Fractured Horizons · Chapter 25

The storm

Fractured Horizons · III. The cut

25

14 JuneKaelinMars, Cobalt Basin Station into the dust stormMars delay 4.2 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Kaelin Drake — Earth 4.2 min

The man in the galley still had his spoon.

He sat where it had found him, at the end of the long table with his back to the slate. His bowl had gone over. The lentils had run to the table's lip and stopped there, and he was smiling at the hatch with his eyes open, as if somebody he liked was about to come through it.

"Legs," said Ines. "Dae-jung, his head. Kaelin, take the weight when we turn."

They lifted him between them. He was light, as everyone was light here, and he didn't help and didn't fight. Kaelin tried to take the spoon out of his fingers on the way down, and the fingers closed on it.

"Leave it," Ines said.

They laid him on a cot from stores the forty had slept on, Alexandria stencilled on the frame. The galley wasn't a galley any more. The long table had gone against the wall with the slate, and the floor was cots and mats in two rows, because medical had five bunks and five people in them, and Selma and Bao on mats beside Petra, and nowhere left to put a foot.

Ines knelt. She laid her hand flat on the man's chest before she said anything, as she always did, and waited for it to rise.

"There you are," she said to him. Then she took the spoon, wiped it on her sleeve, and put it back into his hand. "He'll want to know where he is."

Kaelin counted the room because he couldn't stop himself.

Five here. Four in medical's bunks who hadn't been in them at dinner, Hemi the last of them, in the second bunk, brought in off the rover ramp after he'd driven the two frame hands home and parked and sat down on the ramp and not got up. Nine sleepers since dinner, smiling at nothing. And Selma and Bao on their mats, and Petra, gone quiet since May.

On their feet, in the whole station: him, Ines, Dae-jung, Aur'k somewhere, the chief, the duty officer, and five more spread thin across the air plant and the pile, who couldn't be spared to carry.

On the galley repeater, minutes late, New York was a heat night. The feed showed roofs full of people lying out on mats, white towers, a warning crawling along the bottom of the frame in two languages. Nobody had written a bet on the slate. The Lantern had the weather now.

Under the feed, the station's traces sat in a stack. Every one of them had stood up in the same second, at twenty-one thirty-eight, with the Lantern green on the wall for most of an hour.

"It's the channel," he said.

"I'm told it's a power bus." Ines didn't look up. She was counting the man's breaths against her watch, her lips moving. "I said so to the Battery. In those words. Somebody over there should get the joke."

"The Mars end comes through the site and down the braid to the boundary. Into the Preserve. It can be cut there. By hand."

"Then go and cut it."

"Tell me to."

She looked up then. Her face was grey under the galley lights, and there was lentil on her cuff.

"No," she said. "You want me to say it so it's mine. It's yours. You've got a chief for that. Ask her nicely first." She went back to the breaths. "Then do what you were going to do anyway."

· · ·

The chief had the Lantern on her wall.

It ran across the top of the schedule in green, one word, LIVE, and under it, in amber, command's own medical count. Beside that the hour's air check had come in at ninety-nine, and nobody had initialled it. She sat with her hands folded on the desk and her back straight, and she didn't ask him why he'd come.

"Drop the Mars end," he said.

"A stop is a command action." She said it as she'd say the date. "The Charter's code, from New York. The Mandate's, from Beijing. Neither has been turned. My orders are to hold, and to report."

"Report what?"

"Nine." She looked at the amber number. "I've reported nine."

"Then cut it at the boundary. There's a cut at the boundary end for this. We built it."

"There's a cut at the boundary end for a fault, on both partners' word, by the boundary lead." She didn't unfold her hands. "You're suspended from boundary work. You're still suspended."

"Then give me a rover."

"Your allowance is held." The duty officer was at his screen behind her, very busy with it. "And the berm road's closed. Weather. Visibility at the frame under the line, and the grit taking the visors." She let that sit. "Your call, Kaelin. From the fifth. I've decided it was a good one."

He stood with his hands at his sides. Nine more in the galley and medical than at dinner. A woman across a desk who had heard every word he had ever said to her and never once interrupted. That was how she told you no.

"You'll stay in the hab," she said. "That's an order. It's logged."

"Yes, chief," he said, and went out, and didn't ask her anything else.

· · ·

Medical's hatch stood open on the way to the lock.

Ines was on her knees between the mats with her hand flat on Selma's back. Bao lay on the next mat with his cap off and his face turned toward Selma, smiling at her as if she'd said the funniest thing in the world. In the second bunk Hemi's lips were moving.

Kaelin stopped in the hatch.

"Pole," Ines said. She didn't look up. She moved her hand to Bao and waited for his breath.

He went on down the passage.

The rover ramp sloped down to the lock under a bank of blue work lights, and halfway down it, where Hemi had sat and not got up, it came for him.

A warmth at the back of his head, behind his ear, under the cap. Low and easy, a bath at the right heat. And the frogs. The Reef Coast's frogs after a storm, thousands of them in the cane drains, so loud you couldn't hear the shutters, and his brother under the kitchen table saying they were singing for him, and under all of it a lot of people, very glad he'd come.

One step. He'd stopped. He didn't know when.

He counted the work lights down the ramp. Six. He counted the bolts in the nearest one. Four. He counted his own breaths, and the frogs went on, and at eleven they were further off, and at twenty there was only the ramp and the cold coming up off the lock door and the blue lights buzzing.

He stood with one hand on the rail until he could feel the rail. He didn't tell anyone.

Aur'k was in the lock.

He stood beside the near rover with the dust still in his plating from the fifth, nobody's brush on it since. His helm was turned to the ramp, and he had been waiting long enough to have gone completely still.

Dae-jung was with him, holding a cup he wasn't going to drink, as he held a cup in the galley for company.

"He's moved," Dae-jung said. "On the shelf. Before he came down. A terrible move. I want it noted."

"It's a strategy," said Aur'k.

"It's a washer in the corner. Nobody's ever put a washer in the corner." Dae-jung looked at the cup. "I won't touch it while he's gone. I want him there when I punish it."

Kaelin said nothing to either of them about the ramp. He put his palm on the rover's plate.

FIELD OPS ALLOWANCE: HELD, said the plate. COMMAND AUTHORITY.

"You said no," he said to Aur'k. "On the fifth."

"I said I wouldn't authorise a risk for people who hadn't chosen it."

"And?"

"Nobody's on the frame tonight. Hemi drove them in." Aur'k's helm stayed on him. "If nobody holds the edge when the feed drops, it tears." A pause, as he chose the next word. "There are people on the edge. I've chosen it."

Kaelin knew whose. Zhenyth had put him out of the warm dark on the third, and spent a hand's width of the Glow doing it.

He laid his palm on the plate. The rover woke under it.

BOUNDARY LEAD, said the board. ROUTE?

"I'll stay," said Dae-jung, before anyone asked him. "She'll want carrying done. I don't sleep, and I don't need the air." He held the cup out a little, toward the ramp, toward the galley. "Go on. Lose somewhere else for a change."

· · ·

The storm met them at the top of the berm road like surf at night on a reef: a long roar with no breaks in it, and the rover's hull ringing under the grit.

There was no road. There was the lamp's throw, four metres of brown boiling in the light, and then nothing. The windscreen went white at the edges in the first minute and kept whitening toward the middle. The board had a dozen routes in it, and every one of them was drawn on a storm track that had been a guess for a week, and Kaelin turned them all off.

"Poles," he said.

The poles ran from the station to the site, one every fifty metres, steel, a reflector at the top that the grit had long since taken to the bare metal. Hemi had driven him home by them. A hundred and twenty to the frame.

The first one came out of the brown at the edge of the lamp, leaning, and went past.

"One," he said.

Aur'k sat in the second seat with his helm forward and his hands on his knees, and said nothing. The board between them had stopped asking for a route and drawn its own: STATION – SITE – STATION, STORM RATION. A bar under it, green going to amber at the end. Then the amber moved, on its own, toward the middle of the bar, and a word came up in it.

RESERVE.

"It's counted us there and back," Kaelin said. "In this. And it's into the reserve before we start."

"The reserve is for when it goes wrong."

"It's going wrong."

"Yes," said Aur'k. "That's what it's for."

Nine. Ten. The rover rocked over ruts nobody could see. At fourteen a pole wasn't there. "Dust take it," he said, and stopped, with the lamps on nothing, and counted thirty breaths, and crept forward until the fourteenth came up on the left, bent flat to the ground by some rover in some other season, and he steered by it.

The band clicked.

"Field operations. Boundary lead." The chief, level, as if reading a gauge. "You've left the hab without authority and taken a rover onto a closed road. Return to station."

Kaelin kept his eyes on the brown. Twenty-two.

Aur'k answered. "Boundary lead. There's an incident at the boundary. The rules put the boundary lead at the boundary in an incident. I filed under them on the fifth. You were there."

"There's no incident logged."

"There are nine more people asleep in the galley and medical than at dinner. I'm logging it now." His hand moved on the board. "Boundary incident. Twenty-two forty-one. Boundary lead attending."

There was a long hiss on the band. Then her voice, doing the arithmetic to the end, as she always did.

"That rover's air is the station's."

"Yes," said Aur'k.

"Logged," said the chief. "All of it." And the band went to nothing, and stayed there.

Thirty. Forty. Somewhere past fifty the cabin warmed enough for the grit to come off Aur'k's plating, and the air in the rover began to taste of it, iron and old batteries, and Kaelin's eyes ran, and he wiped them on his wrist and counted. Aur'k reached over without a word and turned the cabin filter up a notch, and the RESERVE bar slid a hair further left.

At ninety his voice had gone to a whisper. Aur'k hadn't stopped him.

It was past midnight when, at a hundred and twenty, the Lantern came up out of the brown, a cage of lamps, the three heads under their struts, every light on the frame burning for nobody.

· · ·

The frame was empty.

Hemi's brush lay on the deck by head three where he'd put it down to drive the others home. The board at the frame's foot showed green. THE LANTERN. LIVE. Under it ran a number of seconds, climbing, the channel's open time, and nothing else, because nobody had needed to tell it anything since nine.

The black braid went away from the frame toward the boundary and into the storm, and the storm had it after ten metres.

Kaelin went up the ladder. The grit took his visor at once. He felt it in his teeth through the helmet, a long scour, and the faceplate bloomed white in a ring and closed toward the middle, and he climbed the last rungs by feel.

Head one. He knew the jumper without seeing it. Two metres of braided cable, clamped at both ends. Selma swore you could pull one with your teeth. He'd pulled head two's on the fifth and dropped a calibration with it. Three jumpers, then the boundary end. That was the plan. That was all of it.

He got his glove round the near clamp.

"Kaelin," said Aur'k, on the private band, from the board. "Don't."

"Hold the edge. I'm pulling the heads first."

"There's a post. On the hard stop. It's landed here." Aur'k read it flat, as he read gauges. "It's from Earth. It's addressed to you. Do not pull the heads."

Kaelin stood on the strut with the clamp in his glove and the grit hissing on his helmet.

"Read it."

"Cobalt Basin, field operations. Drake. Do not pull the heads. Do not cut the Mars end alone. One end cut alone floods the end left open. Both ends must go inside one light-crossing of each other. I am cutting the Earth end. I will send you the time." A pause. "It's signed. E. Vázquez."

He knew the name. It had been in the minutes on the third of June, beside Petra's: the Spire's woman on the review, who had explained his crew's traces away as a power bus and called Petra a contact injury. A girl who'd touched warm rock through a split glove because he'd sent her in.

Three jumpers. He could have all three out before Aur'k reached the edge. He knew they'd come. He'd felt one come out in his shoulder.

He didn't know what was on the other end. She did.

He took his hand off the clamp.

He came down the ladder by feel, the faceplate gone to a white ring with a hole of brown in the middle, and checked his bottle at the foot: full, and nothing behind it but the rover's. At the board Aur'k had turned the screen toward him and cupped one hand round it against the grit, as if a hand would help. A Charter header. His own name in the address line. Her name at the bottom, small.

Behind them the rover's lamps were burning the reserve to light an empty frame.

"How far to the edge?"

"On foot. Along the braid. Not far." Aur'k's helm turned to where the storm swallowed it. "She doesn't say when."

"She says she'll send it."

He put his fist to his chest plate, once, where Aur'k could see it through the bloom.

Then he went after the braid into the brown, counting, to wait at the boundary for the woman who had called Petra a contact injury.
