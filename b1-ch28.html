Fractured Horizons · Chapter 28

The Mars end

Fractured Horizons · III. The cut

28

14 JuneKaelinMars, the Preserve's boundaryMars delay 4.2 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, COBALT BASIN / Kaelin Drake — Earth 4.2 min

The braid went into the ground at a collar of grey casing, waist high, and the storm had buried half of it.

Kaelin dug with his hands. The grit came away in clots and stuck to his gloves, and when he shook them it stayed stuck, and the band in his helmet crackled every time his palms met. Dust that clung. Dust that spat. In eight years on Mars he had never seen it hold on like this.

"It's charged," Aur'k said, behind him. "The storm rubs it. It's looking for somewhere to go."

"It can look somewhere else."

He found the guard by feel. A hinged lid on the collar's face, a latch under it, and under that, sunk in its socket, the handle: a T of machined steel no bigger than his fist, knurled deep enough to grip through a frozen glove.

Bao had made it in March, too big by the frame crew's word, because he was done watching people fumble clamps at eighty below. Pull it, and it comes away in your hand.

Kaelin wiped the inside of the guard with his thumb. The grit smeared, and under the smear, scratched into the paint with a drill bit, in Selma's capitals:

NOT WITH YOUR TEETH.

He laughed in his helmet, once, and the sound came back at him off the faceplate. Selma on her mat in medical. Bao on the next one, smiling at her.

"What is it?" said Aur'k.

"Selma."

"Ah." Aur'k knelt beside the collar. The grit had clung to him too, in a red fur along every seam of his plating, and it lifted where his arm passed, toward his hand. "She told me she'd done it. I said it wasn't procedure. She said nothing out here was procedure, it was all just Bao."

He found the plate on the collar's far side, the boundary end's reader, where the braid met the rock it had been bored into, and laid his palm flat on it.

He went completely still.

Kaelin counted. Not for anything. The seams on the collar, eight. The bolts in the guard's hinge, three. His own breaths. At nineteen Aur'k's helm moved.

"It's pulling," Aur'k said. "Both ways. I have the edge."

"Who's on it?"

"Our nine. Petra further in." A small sound in the band. It might have been static. "Others. Older. They're holding the inside of it, and I'm holding the outside. Zhenyth is there." Then, as if he were reading the next gauge: "Kaelin. A rule. If I ask you to wait, it isn't me."

Kaelin looked at him: a shape in the brown, a hand on a plate, the red fur standing up on his shoulders.

"And if you ask me to cut?"

"Then it's me." A pause. "Or it's clever."

The site board's post came down the braid to them a minute later, and Aur'k read it aloud without lifting his hand.

"Drake. 01:00 New York, 14 June. Both ends. I won't move it." Then, flat as a gauge: "E. Vázquez."

One o'clock. Kaelin had nothing on his wrist to read it by. The pad there was a brown crust, the charged grit packed over its face like felt, and when he scraped it the crust came back as fast.

The message was minutes old when it reached him. Whatever she'd been doing when she sent it, she'd gone on doing it, on a night he wasn't in, in a city where it would be hot, the roofs full of people on mats because indoors was worse. She'd given him a window. Minutes either side of her one o'clock, and past them, whichever end was still open would take the flood.

"How long?"

"Sixteen minutes, by the board," said Aur'k. "I'll count for you."

· · ·

It came up through his knees first.

He knelt with one hand on the casing and the other on the guard, and through the suit, through the rock, came a warmth that had no business in the ground at that hour. It came in pulses. It came behind his ear, under the cap, low and easy, and went, and came again. Under him the Preserve was heaving, and all he had of it was heat, in waves, like a fever in someone you were holding.

"Fourteen minutes," said Aur'k.

"Why the corner?"

"What corner?"

"The shelf. The washer. Dae-jung says nobody's ever put a washer in the corner."

"Nobody has." Aur'k's helm didn't move. "That's why. He'll spend a week on it. He'll think I've seen a move four ahead, and I haven't. I wanted him to have a puzzle while I was gone."

"That's cheating."

"It's a strategy."

The warmth came up through Kaelin's knees and stayed longer. He counted it. Six. At six it went.

"Twelve minutes," said Aur'k. "It's leaning on the edge. All of it at once. It's never leaned like this." A pause. "Hemi's close. Hemi wants to go."

"Don't let him."

"I'm not letting anyone."

The grit spat on the band. The storm went on with its long roar, surf on a reef that never drew back, and Kaelin's lamp made a brown room with no walls.

"Ten minutes," said Aur'k.

Then nothing.

Kaelin waited for the next number. It didn't come. He counted thirty of his own and it still didn't come.

"Aur'k."

"We're here." The helm turned, very slowly, toward the brown where the station was. "It's warm here, Kaelin. You can stop counting now."

It was Aur'k's voice, every word of it, with the care gone out of it.

"Count."

"Nobody here counts." Kindly. "There's no need."

"Count for me."

Nothing.

He took Aur'k's wrist where it met the plate. Under its fur of grit the arm was warm, warmer than his own hand inside the glove.

"Off the plate. Now. That's an order, boundary lead."

"You can pull me off." The voice didn't change. "Then nobody's holding the outside. It tears, and they come through it with me."

His hand stayed on the wrist a long time. Then he let go.

The warmth came up through the rock and didn't go. Kaelin held the casing and named things. The seams. The bolts. Selma's capitals under his thumb.

"Say your name."

"Aur'k."

"Say the date."

"The fourteenth." Then a sound in the band, a small bright run of breath, up and down, a laugh he had heard across the galley table four hundred times and in the warm dark once.

Petra's.

He felt it in his teeth. He took his hand off the casing, and put it back, because it was the only thing in the brown he could find twice.

"That wasn't you."

"No," said the voice, gently, and it was Aur'k's again. "That was her. She's here. She laughs a lot. If you cut, Kaelin, they stay where they are. Petra. Hemi. The man with the spoon. All of them, where they are now. Asleep. You won't be able to reach them, and neither will we."

He knew it was true, as he'd known Sami's leg was wrong before he saw it.

"You never lie about the odds," the voice said. "Those are the odds."

"I know."

"Then wait."

He didn't answer.

"A minute. One more minute, so we can bring them to the edge for you. So you can say goodbye properly."

She'd given him minutes either side. It would take all of them, a minute at a time, and every one of them kind.

Ten minutes had been Aur'k's last number, and it had been true when he said it. Kaelin had counted thirty since, and lost a few to a laugh. Call it forty. He started again from there, under his breath, the count he'd kept for thunder under a kitchen table and for the pulse at Petra's throat: one, and one, and one.

· · ·

He shut his eyes.

There was no reason not to. The faceplate had gone white in its ring and closed to a hole of brown no bigger than a cup, and the lamp only made the brown brighter, and the wrist pad was felt. He had his count, and his breath loud in the helmet, louder than the storm.

"You're counting," the voice said, with interest, as a friend notices a habit.

Five minutes. Four.

"Kaelin. She's at the edge. She's nearly here."

If he pulled early the open end was Earth's. Haven's. Hers. If he waited, it was his. The galley and medical and the man with the spoon. And he couldn't see her end. He'd never see it. Whatever she did at one o'clock, he'd find out after, when it was already done.

Two minutes. He found the handle with both hands, the knurling under his gloves through the clinging grit.

"Please," said the voice. It was the first time he had ever heard Aur'k say it, and it wasn't Aur'k. "Please. A minute."

If I ask you to wait, it isn't me.

Sixty. Forty. He breathed. Twenty. Ten.

It comes away in your hand.

He pulled.

The handle came out of the collar in one long draw, heavier than he thought and then not heavy at all, and he sat down hard on Mars with it against his chest. Inside the casing the contacts let go with a knock he took through the rock and up his spine.

The warmth went out of the ground. All of it, at once. Behind his ear there was nothing. Under him there was rock, and the cold of eighty below working in through the knees of his suit, and the storm.

Aur'k went over.

He fell sideways off the plate with his hand still shaped to it and lay in the grit with his helm turned up into the brown, and the red fur along his seams stood straight out, every grain of it, and lay down.

Kaelin got to him on his knees.

He couldn't see him. The sign was two taps on your own visor and a point, and nobody could see a point in this. He found Aur'k's helm with his glove and tapped that instead, twice, hard. Are you there.

Nothing.

Two again.

He counted. Four. Five. He'd have gone on to sixty, he'd have gone on all night, and at seven a hand came up off the ground, slowly, and found his faceplate and tapped it once.

Here.

· · ·

Aur'k was hot.

Kaelin felt it through both gloves when he got him by the shoulder hardpoint, a heat coming off the plating with no right to be there in that cold, and where it touched, the grit slid off him in sheets, as frost slides off a warm roof.

On Earth he couldn't have shifted him. Here he could drag him, on his back, along the braid toward the frame, and had to stop every forty steps with his thighs burning, and did, and went on, counting, because the braid was the only line in the brown and the frame was at the end of it.

The rover's lamps came up out of the storm, two smears of white, still burning the reserve to light the frame.

It took him two goes to get Aur'k up the step. He sealed the hatch and the cabin pressed back up around them, and the board between the seats woke and showed him the bar: RESERVE, further left.

He got his helmet off. The cabin air tasted of iron and old batteries, and now of hot composite too, and his eyes ran. He keyed the band to medical.

Static. The storm's own crackle. He keyed it again.

"Medical." Ines, far off and grainy, as if from the bottom of a well. "Go."

"It's cut. Both ends, if she did hers. How are they?"

A long hiss. He waited through it.

"Breathing," said Ines. "All of them. Turned and breathing." Another hiss. "Nobody's come up, Kaelin. Not one."

He sat with that.

"Full air, then," he said.

"Full air." And then, because she was Ines, "Pole on the way back. I've no more cots."

Aur'k lay across the second seat with his helm against the window. The heat came off him in a shimmer.

"Aur'k."

The helm turned, the whole of it, toward him.

"Kaelin." The voice was slow. It was formal, every word set down as if it might be checked later, and there was nothing easy in it. "Did we cut?"

"I cut."

"On her time?"

"On mine. Near enough hers."

"Good." A long stillness. He lifted his own hand and looked at the palm. "I was at the edge. I don't know when I came off it." Another stillness. "There's a gap. I have the storm. I don't have much before it."

"You read me a post at the frame."

"I don't have that." He thought about it. Then, carefully: "Kaelin. Whose move is it? On the shelf."

Kaelin looked at him for a while.

"Dae-jung's," he said. "You made yours. Before we left."

"What did I play?"

"He'll tell you. He wants you there when he punishes it."

"Then I'll have to be there."

Kaelin put his fist to his chest plate, where Aur'k could see it. Aur'k looked at the fist a long time, as if he were reading it for the first time, and then put his own to his.

Kaelin set Bao's handle on the dash. The grit fell off it onto the board.

Outside, the frame stood in its cage of lamps, every light on it burning, and on the board at its foot the word LIVE had gone and the number under it had stopped climbing.

He could read the rover's clock now. It went past one o'clock, and on, past the minute when anything she'd done at one would reach him, and on.

Nothing came. No post. No channel. No warmth behind his ear. The band carried the grit spitting on the hull, and under it, from the rock and from Earth, nothing at all.
