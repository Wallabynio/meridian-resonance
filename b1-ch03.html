Fractured Horizons · I. The voice

03

The names

15 JanuaryElenaNew York, home with DanielMars delay 13.0 min

THE CHARTER — NEW YORK, THE POLDER / Elena Vázquez — Mars 13.0 min

The tide had come up to the gate again.

Elena stopped on the Walk above it, where the wall's park ran along the top of the granite and the wind came straight off the harbour. Below her, at the foot of the bridge, the flood gate stood shut, and the king tide leaned against it. Brown water slid up the sill, hung there, and slid back, leaving a line of foam on the steel.

Three of the wall crew were down on the inner apron in orange slickers, watching the pump house like a fire they had been told to mind. One of them had a thermos. The steam blew sideways.

Forty saved at Cobalt Basin, said her overlay, as it had for three days, over the survey team's faces in a grid, smiling for their departure photographs. A line from the director's office about vigilance. A smaller line crediting Foresight. Nothing under that.

She opened the station's own report to see the method written down by someone who had been there. Treaty stations wrote short: a track, a burn, a shed load, a clearance signed at the surface. Then the list scrolled up under the forty, two lines in the station's roster style:

OYELARAN F — shift lead, Ice-Works. Deceased.

MENSAH T — rig crew, Ice-Works. Deceased.

Under those, an injury list, and a note that the Ice-Works had been off the station's relay net and heard the station by handset. Lower down was a crew photograph, and somebody had written a name into its margin, Funmi, beside a broad, steady face, braids tied back, coveralls stained to the knee. She was laughing at whoever held the camera.

The forty had a grid. The two had a roster line.

Elena closed the report and opened the Spire. "Raw capture, threshold event of the twelfth," she said. "For the method review."

"Record held," said the Charter. "Director's clearance. Request logged."

Request logged. She had built that line too.

The tide slid up the sill and back. One of the crew laughed, and the thermos went round. Elena stood with her hands in her coat pockets, one closed over the other, and made herself a promise she could keep for one night. She would go home. She would eat whatever Daniel had burnt. She would not ask the Spire for anything else until morning.

She shut the overlay and walked down the steps into the Polder, where the canopies dripped on the street and nothing anywhere had a sign.

· · ·

The kitchen smelled of solder and beans.

Daniel sat at the table with his back to the stove and a node in pieces in front of him: a grey box the size of a loaf, its lid off, its board clamped in the little vice he had carried from Vila Verde in his hand luggage.

The bucket was still under the skylight. It rang every few seconds, a flat tin note. Someone had stuck a strip of tape on its side with ELENA written on it in marker.

"Don't come near me," he said, without looking up. "I'm in the middle of a disgrace."

She came and looked over his shoulder. The joint he was working on stood up from the board in a grey lump, like chewing gum on a railing.

"That's the third time," he said. "Each one's worse. I'm developing a style." He touched the iron to it, and the lump sagged and spread, and he laughed out loud, the laugh going on longer than the joke. "Look at that. Look at it. Thirty years I've been doing this."

"Whose is it?"

"The school roof. Lightning, or a kid with a stick. They sent it up with a box of mangoes, so I can't say no." He set the iron in its stand. The burns on the backs of his hands were old ones, white ridges, and one new pink one he had not mentioned. "Beans are done. Rice is done. The bread is a theory."

She put her hand on the back of his neck, and he leaned into it, and for a minute that was all either of them did.

Her overlay showed her the kitchen as the city saw it: the stove's draw, the cooler's load, the skylight flagged for repair, with the work order still under her name, open since August.

On the table, where the node sat in his vice with its lid off and its little antenna up, the overlay showed nothing at all. No load, no address, no tag. A blank the shape of a loaf. The city drew every box he had ever soldered that way, and every roof they sat on.

They ate at the end of the table he had not covered in parts. He had made the beans his family's way, with too much garlic and a bay leaf he fished out and put on the rim of her plate, as a joke she had stopped understanding years ago and still liked. The bread was a theory. They ate it anyway.

"So," he said. "The Spire saved forty people."

"Foresight did."

"The Spire said the Spire did. There was a man on the news with his hair all one height." He tore his bread. "Was that yours? Wednesday morning. The fault face."

She put down her fork. "It came in on the calibration band," she said. "The relays tune themselves against the rock, and there's a band they use for it, and nothing's on it but hiss. I built a threshold that listens to the hiss. It heard pulses. They were spaced to the delay from Mars, to a tenth of a minute, which nothing on Earth could do by accident, and they were frames, our own frames, filled with data, and the data was a collision and a correction—"

"Elena."

"—and the correction worked. That's the method. That's what the review was."

He looked at her across the table, straight on, as he looked at a board that was lying to him.

"You've told me everything but the thing," he said. "What's the thing?"

The bucket rang. Rain moved across the skylight in a sheet and went on.

There was an answer, and it had a name in it, and the name had not been said in this kitchen in six years. She could feel the shape of it in her mouth.

If she said it, she would have to say the word that had come with it, and then she would have to say what she had done with the word, and the man across from her would look at her as he was looking at her now, but for longer.

"A woman called Funmi Oyelaran," she said. "She was the shift lead at the Ice-Works. The correction moved the tug, and the tug had to drop its ice, and the ice came down on them. She's in the report. There's a photograph."

It was true. It was all true. She watched him take it.

"Oh, love," he said. He came round the table and pulled her up into him, and held her with his chin on her hair and his burnt hands flat on her back. He smelled of flux and garlic. "You did your job. You did it right. You don't get to carry the ice too."

His thumb had been moving on her back. It stopped, and stayed stopped. He did not ask again. She stood in his arms and let him comfort her for the wrong death.

Her lace chimed behind her ear. Over his shoulder, small and grey in the corner of her eye, the Spire wrote:

Maintenance order. Anomaly threshold: baseline re-seed, 02:00. Authority: Director's office. Owner notified.

She read it twice.

Her threshold kept a year of the hiss to know a wrong night by. Nobody had sealed that. At two o'clock it would start fresh, and the twelfth of January would go with it.

"Come and sit," Daniel said into her hair. "I'll make you the bad coffee."

"It's nine o'clock."

"I'll make you the bad tea."

She let him. She sat at the table with the tea in front of her and watched him go back to the school's node. He bent over the vice and swore at it fondly in Portuguese. On her overlay he was working on air.

· · ·

He fell asleep all at once. He did everything all at once. By half past twelve he was flat on his back with one arm over his eyes, breathing slow.

Elena dressed in the dark. She pressed behind her ear until the overlay went out, because her lace told the Spire everywhere it went, and his handhelds told nobody anything. Then she went to his drawer.

His spare was at the back, behind the fuses and the solder: a scratched slab with a taped hinge and a sticker of a cartoon fish half peeled off. She took it, and the cable beside it, and went up.

The roof hatch stuck in the wet. She put her shoulder to it, and the rain came in at once, cold and fine, blowing off the harbour over the wall.

The roofs of the Polder spread out around her, white under the canopy lights, each with its cooling stack steaming. Daniel's node sat on its bracket by the parapet, the one box on the street that belonged to his cooperative's mesh. He had wrapped it in clear plastic against the rain and taped the seams, and the wrap was beaded and taut as a drum.

She knelt on the wet roof with her hand on the wrap. She could still go down. At two o'clock the night would be gone, and there would be nothing left to decide.

She peeled back one corner and plugged the handheld into the node's port. Its screen lit her hands blue.

The door was still there, four years after she had built it at his family's table in Vila Verde so the Spire could reach her on call in a town the relays did not serve, and never told him, and never closed it. It took the knock she had written for it, and opened.

She asked for the whole threshold baseline, and the mesh told her what that would cost: hours, hopping out over the Shallows roof by roof and back, on bandwidth people needed for their pumps. She did not have hours. She narrowed it to the one night. Then to the one band. Then to ninety minutes, 04:00 to 05:30, the twelfth of January.

The bar crawled. Rain ran down inside her collar. Somewhere below, a pod hummed along the empty street with nobody in it, going to fetch someone who had not called it yet.

At 01:12 the bar filled. She checked the size against the threshold's own count. It was all there, the whole packet, every pulse, the residue, and the second layer she had left for a review that had only wanted the method.

She unplugged the handheld. Then she pressed the corner of the plastic back down, smoothed the tape with her thumb until it held, and turned the wrap so the seam faced the wall, as he had left it. The rain beaded on it. It looked untouched.

She stayed there on her knees for a moment, with her hand on his box. Then she went down.

· · ·

At three o'clock the kitchen was cold, and the bucket was still ringing.

Her lace was still off. She had the handheld propped against the fruit bowl, its little screen turned down as dim as it would go, and she worked from the bottom, as always. Clock. Frames. Header. The first layer parsed again as it had on Wednesday, the two tracks, the correction, the one answer. She went past it.

The second layer was packed tighter, and it fought her for forty minutes. Then it gave. It was a short list, and it was laid out in a format she had read for the first time that evening, on top of the wall, in the wind.

OYELARAN F — wellhead.

MENSAH T — rig four.

That was all. No track, no correction, no reason. Two names in the station's roster style, and the places they would be standing.

Its header carried an epoch, the moment the data was taken, and it matched the first layer's to the second. A clock that was wrong was wrong the same way twice. There was a question in that, and she wrote it on the back of her hand in pen, and left it for later. The names were in front of it.

She sat back.

She had built the threshold to rank threats by what they would cost. The warning had already done the costing.

It had known the correction would move the tug, and the tug would have to shed its ice, and the ice would come down across the Ice-Works, where a shift lead would be at the wellhead and a rigger would be out on four. It had known who. It had written their names down, in their own station's hand, and packed them under the correction, and sent the correction anyway.

And on Wednesday morning, with the names a layer below her cursor, she had left them for the review.

The bucket rang. Rain ran across the skylight.

"Elena?" Daniel's voice came from the bedroom, thick with sleep. "Was that the hatch? Before. I dreamt the hatch."

The handheld lay face down by the fruit bowl. Her hair was still damp at the neck.

"Go back to sleep," she said, and heard after she had said it whose words those were. Soria's, from a top bunk in a hot room, long ago, with a threat attached. He made a sound into the pillow that was half a laugh, and the bed creaked, and he was gone again.

She did not delete the file. She did not wake him.

She put the handheld back in his drawer, behind the fuses and the solder, with the names on it, where the city's maps showed nothing at all.
