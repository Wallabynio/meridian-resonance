LUNAR ACCORD — AUSTRALASIAN RING, ORBITAL STATION / Sienna TeAriki

The first probe arrived at 0247 local, and it was polite.

A Corporate Union interceptor drifted out of its filed transit corridor by eleven kilometres — a navigation deviation, the kind that happened a hundred times a day across cislunar space, the kind that treaty protocol obliged Luna Haven's outer defence perimeter to acknowledge, hail, and track until corrected. The interceptor corrected. It apologised, in the automated grammar ships used with each other, and resumed its corridor, and by 0253 it was exactly where its filing said it should be.

At 0311 a Sovereign Coalition survey platform did the same thing at a different bearing. At 0348, two more.

Sienna TeAriki stood at the launch-coordination floor's main display, high in the upper systems of the retasked Opera House, and watched the fourth deviation resolve itself with the same courteous precision as the first three, and understood that nobody, anywhere in that pattern, had made a navigation error tonight.

"They're not testing whether we'll shoot," she said. "They know we won't shoot."

"Then what's being tested?" The Orbital Station's voice kept its register, warm and unhurried, a colleague who happened to be a building.

"The clock." She pulled the perimeter log across the display and laid the four events side by side. "Deviation to first hail: nineteen seconds, twenty-two, eighteen, twenty-one. Hail to sensor lock. Sensor lock to escalation notice. They're timing us. Four bearings, four response chains, and somewhere on a depot with forty per cent extra radiator load, someone is building a table: this is how long Luna Haven takes to see, this is how long it takes to decide, this is where the seams between its systems are." She had spent sixteen years reading infrastructure the way other people read faces, and there was no anger in what she felt, looking at the table being built against her; anger was for later, and would be a luxury even then. "It's a survey. We're the coastline."

"Treaty protocol requires us to respond to every deviation. We can't decline to be measured."

"No." Below the coordination floor's long window the harbour lay in its pre-dawn stillness, the grown lattice of the concourse roof holding the first grey light, and beyond the breakwater the drowned suburbs kept their low dark line, the oldest survey result in the city. "But nothing in the treaty says the measurement has to be accurate. Open the perimeter response configuration, my code."

She worked for an hour, and what she built was a lie made entirely of true things. Every hail still went out inside protocol limits — but now on a fixed cadence, flattened to the slowest compliant interval, so that the perimeter answered a drifting interceptor with the same drowsy regularity it would have shown a lost cargo skiff. The fast reflexes, the layered sensor handoffs, the response chains she had spent months tightening — those she pulled back behind the displayed behaviour, dark capacity, held out of the survey the way a seawall's foundations were held out of sight of the sea that meant to test it.

"Logged as a standardisation pass," the Station said. "Their table will show a perimeter that is compliant, uniform, and slightly slow."

"Their table will show what we chose to put on it." She opened a second workspace. "Now the real one. Every probe, every bearing, every timing interval — full sensor takes, pattern analysis, the lot. They're mapping our responses; the probes themselves map their doctrine. Approach geometries, coordination lags between Union and Coalition assets, which platforms move first and which wait for permission. Package it continuous, two addressees: Léa Fournier's research channel, Meridian Levels. Tomasz Nowak, security planning."

"They're building a map of us," the Station said, "and we're building a better one of them, because they don't know we know we're being surveyed."

"They know a survey is deniable and an attack isn't. That's the whole shape of tonight." She signed the configuration and stood a moment with her hand on the console's worn edge, moko lines at her jaw caught faintly in the display light, feeling the size of it the way she allowed herself to feel such things: briefly, precisely, and then as a list of tasks. Out past the perimeter, forty thousand kilometres of lawful, patient, courteous preparation was learning the wrong lessons from her city on purpose. "Log the night as routine perimeter activity. Then show me the tide gauges."

Because the sea was also still out there, and the sea, at least, had never pretended its probes were accidents.

LUNAR ACCORD — MEDITERRANEAN ZONE, HARBOUR HOUSE / Jamie Han

The room above the boat repair yard held three clocks, and Jamie Han had set each of them to a different world.

The first ran on Sydney time, for the infrastructure-anomaly feed that reached her through two relay hops and a fishing cooperative's logistics account — orbital filings, thermal signatures, the perimeter probe documentation that had started arriving that morning with Tomasz Nowak's austere annotations attached. The second ran on Neo Singapore time, because everything she moved went through the Gardens Relay's treaty channel eventually, and the Relay's maintenance windows were the tides her entire architecture navigated by. The third ran on local time, Barcelona's, and was the only one with an alarm set, because even an intelligence network was carried by people, and people had to eat.

She worked standing, sleeves pushed back, the harbour noise coming up through the floorboards — winches, gulls, a hull being ground down to fair metal — and the noise was not a nuisance; the noise was cover, and she had chosen the room for it. Three threads, three panes, one operator. She moved between them with the unhurried economy of someone who had stopped finding her own competence interesting a long time ago.

Thread one: a safe house in the Gulf Crescent reporting Corporate Union academic-security teams conducting exit interviews that were neither exits nor interviews — researchers on watch lists being reminded, gently, that their clearances were also their tethers. She logged names, cross-matched two against the asylum pipeline, flagged one for extraction assessment, and routed the report onward, stripped and re-authenticated, through the Gardens Relay to Luna Haven.

Thread two: the probe documentation itself, which was not hers to analyse but hers to make survivable — split, encoded, sent by three routes of which the Relay was only the visible one, so that the attack map Sienna TeAriki and Tomasz Nowak were building would exist in enough places that no single strike could unbuild it. Since two nights ago, that principle had stopped being tradecraft and become the weather: some engineer in São Verde had published a suppressed archive to ten thousand nodes at once, and Jamie had felt the whole clandestine world shift around the fact of it. You could no longer kill a truth by finding its keeper. She did not know Daniel Cruz's name. She had already incorporated his lesson.

Thread three she kept in the corner pane, small, the way you kept a thing you were trying not to stare at: a clearance request, eight months old, moving through the Lunar Accord's emergency research asylum programme on its final day. A materials scientist. One person, no strategic weight, a rounding error in the systems Jamie spent her nights against. She had shepherded the application through four bureaucratic near-deaths without the applicant ever knowing she existed, and she could not entirely have said why this one — except that somewhere under the tradecraft she had already decided what all of it was for, the threads and the relays and the clocks, and had been executing toward it ever since with the particular focus of someone who no longer needed the decision explained to her. Networks did not protect themselves. Protection was not an abstraction. It came down, always, to whether one specific person got to walk through one specific door.

The corner pane updated. CLEARANCE GRANTED — SEAWALL STATION, PROCESSING.

Jamie looked at it for three seconds, which was the longest she had looked at anything all day. Then she closed the pane, reset the local alarm, and went back to thread one, because the terrible went on being ordinary, and making it survivable was the work, and the work was going well.

LUNAR ACCORD — MEDITERRANEAN ZONE, COASTAL SEAWALL / Ines Almeida

For eight months the word no had arrived in fourteen different formats, and so at first she did not recognise its absence.

Ines Almeida stood at the processing window of the Seawall station with her single case at her feet and watched the officer read the display, waiting for the familiar choreography — the sympathetic pause, the referral code, the invitation to reapply when her institutional obligations were clarified, which meant never, because the Corporate Union did not refuse its researchers permission to leave; it simply arranged for permission to remain perpetually in process. Eight months of being neither kept nor released. Her contract suspended but not dissolved, her publications frozen in review, her clearance revoked and yet somehow still binding, an entire architecture of soft custody that never once touched her and never once let go.

"Dr Almeida." The officer turned the display toward her. "Your clearance came through this morning. Emergency research asylum, Lunar Accord programme. You're the first, actually — the programme's first formal grant. There'll be a transit allocation and a residence assignment; the details follow you to the hostel." A small pause, not procedural. "You can go through."

The gate was four metres away. It stood open onto the seawall promenade — no barrier, no scanner arch, just warm gold sandstone and the flat blue weight of the Mediterranean beyond it — and Ines discovered that she had prepared, over eight months, every document, every contingency, every argument, and had not prepared at all for the possibility of walking four metres.

She walked them.

The Seawall took her in the way it took the sea: without ceremony. The adaptive masonry ran away from her in a long breathing arc, gold courses of engineered sandstone stepped and curved against the water, each block minutely angled by machinery she could feel as a faint patient hum underfoot — a wall that did not resist the sea so much as continuously negotiate with it, which was, she thought, the first piece of infrastructure she had stood on in years whose entire purpose was not leverage but care. Far along the arc, hazed by distance and salt light, the retrofitted spire of the Sagrada Família stood against the sky with its monitoring arrays folded among the old stone spires, a cathedral that had been made into an instrument without being made into less of a cathedral.

She set her case down and stood at the parapet, and was precise with herself, because precision was the habit that had kept her intact: nothing vast had happened. One clearance. One researcher, whose work on resonance-tolerant materials mattered to perhaps forty people alive. The blocs moved fleets tonight; the sea moved itself; her entire deliverance would fit in a footnote. But she had spent eight months inside a system that could not say yes, and she was now standing on one that had been engineered, block by angled block, to say it continuously — and she found she could measure the difference in her own body, in shoulders that were lowering by degrees on no instruction of hers.

Behind her the gate stayed open. That was the thing she kept turning to check, and it was the thing she would remember when the programme that began with her had a history: not the crossing, but the gate afterward — open, gold, unguarded — as if her leaving had been, all along, the most ordinary thing the world could have permitted.

LUNAR ACCORD — LUNA HAVEN, MERIDIAN LEVELS / Sarin Yelena

The rift arrived on schedule, because Sarin Yelena had worked out their schedule nine days ago.

Not from intelligence — nobody sent the Corporate Union's test calendar to a bioengineer — but from the substrate itself, which flinched before every event the way the Preserve's grazers had moved off the corridor before the seam found its voice. A tightening in the interface layer, forty minutes out. A thin detuned note under the Research Levels' ambient hum that she suspected only she could hear, and only because the filaments at her temples and forearms had long since stopped being equipment and started being how she listened.

"Here it comes," she said. "Clear the array floor. Anchor stations, both of you, and stay on them this time."

The two technicians moved — quickly, but not in panic, because Sarin was not in panic, and the room had learned to take its interior state from hers. That was itself a design decision. The substrate read the room. A frightened room made a brittle interface. So she stood at the centre of the environmental array in the low bioluminescent teal of the Levels, sleeves rolled, silver filament lines already warming along her forearms, and made herself the calmest object in it.

"Confirming," the Levels system said, conversational even now, because Luna Haven built its voices to be company. "Dimensional shear forming, induced signature, point of origin external — matches the weapons-test profile. They're rifting us from a distance again, Sarin. Small one, this time. Barely a postcard."

"It's not a postcard, it's a stopwatch." She knelt and laid both forearms against the interface substrate's access margin. "They're timing how fast this colony can catch what they throw. Log everything — they're measuring us, and Tomasz will want the measurement measured. It's why he asked me across." Twelve days ago the request had come through the Meridian access point in Tomasz Nowak's flat, unornamented syntax — signals suggest induced rift events; you are the only proven anchor; the rifts will land on this side — and she had crossed the same day, because a man who wasted no words on ceremony had used three clauses on her, and that was a siren by his standards.

The rift opened.

It was a miniature of the cascade — she felt the family resemblance immediately, the same wrongness in a minor key, a seam of undirected dimensional energy shearing into the Levels' substrate two floors below the research array — and the eleven minutes at the Lattice Threshold had been terror and prayer and improvisation, and this was Tuesday. That was the thing no report had captured. She had held a boundary closed once with her nervous system and not known if she would survive it; through the Preserve's long interior season since — short touches at the healthy margins becoming longer holds, panic drilled down into procedure — what had been a desperate asking had become a craft, with a craft's grammar: find the load. Give it somewhere to be. Never hold — distribute.

She found the load. The rift's energy came up through the interface layer like floodwater finding a stairwell, and she met it the way the Seawall met the sea — not resistance, negotiation — taking the surge into the lattice through her forearms and spreading it, fanning it wide across the substrate's deep planes, a hundred small permissible strains in place of one impermissible tearing. The array floor lights dipped. Her jaw locked; the old strain climbed her arms in its familiar silver ache; somewhere behind her a technician was reading stability numbers aloud in a voice that stayed level because hers had.

Four minutes. What had once nearly killed her over eleven now fit, whole, inside four.

"Shear collapsing," the Levels said. "Research Levels stable. Sarin — nothing above deck so much as rattled. They'll have measured a colony that shrugged."

"Good." She stayed kneeling a moment, forearms still against the margin, riding the last of the distribution down. The energy had to go somewhere; that was the craft's one iron law. She had sent it deep, into the old substrate under the habitat's foundations, the dead rock the colony plans called solid — and there, at the far edge of her extended proprioception, at a depth where there should have been nothing but the dumb geological silence of the Moon, the load settled strangely. Not absorbed. Received. The way sound was received by a room rather than a wall.

She frowned, and logged the anomaly for the structural survey — substrate response non-uniform below Archive datum, recommend deep-level assessment — and did not chase it, because chasing anomalies mid-recovery was how anchors got hurt, and because some part of her, the part the Preserve had trained, knew the difference between a door you opened and a door you knocked on.

Two floors down, in the dark under everything, the knock went on being heard.
