The bounded test

Fractured Horizons · I. The voice

07

2 AprilRachelLuna Haven, the CommonsMars delay 6.7 min

THE COMPACT — LUNA HAVEN, THE COMMONS / Rachel Iverson — Mars 6.7 min

Rachel took the Commons steps at her own speed and left the floor.

The steps were long and shallow, cut for a sixth of a gee, and she had been warned about them twice on the shuttle and once at the lock. She went up the first three as she went up the gallery stairs at the Battery, fast, and on the fourth her body kept going.

For a long, stupid second she hung in the warm air with her bag swinging out to one side. Then the handrail was in her hand and her shoes came down, slowly, one after the other.

A girl of ten or so, sitting on the rail at the top, had watched the whole thing. She was taller than Rachel already, thin as a broom handle, and she did not laugh. She held up four fingers.

"Four out of ten?"

"For the landing," the girl said, and went back to her game.

The Commons was a cavern made into a square. Its walls had been grown, vaulted up out of the rock in long ribs that gave off a low honey light of their own, with handrails at every height a person might need one and doorways cut tall and narrow into the far side, and everyone in it spoke low, even the children.

The children had a game going in the middle: a dozen of them throwing a soft ball in high arcs that took an age to come down, and running underneath to meet it.

Above them the dome was a lens of shielded glass over the old skylight, and Earth hung in it, blue-white and bigger than Rachel had expected, with a brown haze lying over the Gulf like a thumbprint on a window. She had seen that haze on the morning boards for a week.

Mara Ekeberg was waiting at a table by the wall, standing, one palm flat on the warm surface beside her. Pale braid, dark keeper's suit cut close. She watched Rachel cover the last ten metres at a careful walk.

"Director Iverson."

"Rachel."

Mara left a pause before she answered. Rachel had been told about that too: Haven people left it even face to face, out of habit or courtesy, and nobody had said which.

"No," Mara said.

"I haven't sat down."

"I don't trust Charter faces. I'm telling you now so you don't waste the morning finding out. Every hour you sit in my Commons costs Haven twenty litres, and I'd rather you breathed them on the shuttle home."

Rachel sat down. Mara, after her pause, did too.

"One calibration," Rachel said. "The Lantern sweeps once through your lattice, and it's done."

"What does it do to the walls?"

Rachel had the answer. She had read it on the shuttle until the cabin lights went down. "Foresight's model puts the load under two per cent of the lattice's daily—"

"Foresight's model."

"Yes."

"Foresight has never grown a wall." Mara's hand had not left the surface. It glowed faintly through her fingers, the honey colour of everything in here. "These are my grandmother's walls. When they're sick, people stop breathing. People, Iverson. Have you ever been linked?"

"Lace. Never a lattice."

"Then you don't know what you're asking."

"I know what I'm asking," Rachel said. "I don't know what it costs. That's why I came up here."

Mara looked at her through the pause and a little past it. Then she tipped her head at the junction behind Rachel, the mouth of the Tubes, where a slate board hung at eye height.

"Read that."

Rachel turned. AIR TODAY, it said in chalk, and under it a number. Ninety-four. Somebody had rubbed out a hundred to write it. She could see the ghost.

"It was full yesterday," Mara said. "Your cargo is four days late. Nitrogen and filter beds. The slot slipped on Tuesday, and nobody on Earth will tell us why, so this morning we cut the budget. Every child in that game is breathing six litres in a hundred less than yesterday." The pause. "You came up on the shuttle our cargo should have been on. They found room for you."

Rachel had not known. She had read every page of the Lantern file, and not one line of Haven's manifest, because the manifest was not hers.

It was her bloc's.

She could say the slot was a Signatories matter. She could say freight was another department. Both were true, and both were what the desk would say.

"I didn't know," she said. "I'll find out who did it."

"Everybody says that," said Mara.

· · ·

The board was being changed again when Rachel reached it.

A boy in a keeper's apprentice suit, too big in the shoulders, was rubbing out the ninety-four with the heel of his hand. He wrote ninety-two under it in careful figures, and stepped back to check it was straight.

"Again?" Rachel said.

"Recount." He didn't look round. Then he saw her jacket, the Charter cut of it, and went red to the ears, and went.

She stood by the board and called the Battery.

Joaquín came up at the edge of her eye at his desk, coat on, the flood glass behind him with the season's bets still running down it in grease pencil.

"You're on the Moon," he said. "How's the Moon?"

"Short of air. Find me a cargo slot. Haven supply contract, Continuity freight, slipped Tuesday."

The pause went out and came back. She watched him lean in and read, and watched his face stop smiling while she was still a long way off.

"Ay, no." He read it again. "Rescheduled by the freight office. Reason: Handled. Requested by—" He scrolled. "Nobody. An office. Rachel, it's a Continuity office. It's us."

"I know it's us."

"I'm saying it out loud so one of us has." He rubbed his face. "While you're up there. My cousin. Tall, looks like me but handsomer, works the Ward. If you see her, tell her to call her mother. Her mother calls me instead, and I have a job."

"Tall. Handsome. Ward."

She waited out the distance.

"Handsomer," he said, and the call closed.

Two children came round the curve of the Tubes in long bounding strides and stopped dead at the board. They read it. Then they went on at a walk.

Rachel called the Spire.

Bellamy answered from his office with the city white behind him. He looked like a man who had been expecting her for a day and had slept badly through all of it.

"Rachel. How is Haven?"

"Ninety-two," she said. "Full yesterday. Your freight office slipped their slot."

She watched it travel.

"Not mine," he said. "The Continuity's freight runs on the Continuity's schedule. I'm sorry for the trouble."

"You've got a lattice of your own. In the Spire. Calibrate through that."

Again the pause, and in it his face did not move at all.

"Had," he said. "The line closed in eighty-nine. It's sealed with everything else from that year, and the files and the line are one seal. If I opened one I'd be opening the other, and we'd have every lawyer in the Charter in the room by lunch." He folded his hands. "Haven's is the only lattice licensed for use. We'd all like that slot restored, and I imagine a signed test would help everyone's schedule."

She stood at the junction with the chalk number at her shoulder and took it in. Somebody in the Spire had walked along Haven's wall until they found the one pump on it that still answered to Earth, and put a hand on it, and leaned, and called it scheduling. She knew every pump on her own wall by number. She knew what it took to lean on one.

"Here's my schedule," she said. "The slot goes back on the manifest today. Not on signature. Today. Haven can say no to your test and still breathe. If it isn't back by Haven's evening, my name comes off the review, and you can count the votes again without it."

A second and a bit to Earth. A second and a bit back.

"Of course," said Bellamy.

It came back on the first instant the distance allowed, with no thinking in it at all, as he had said it on the Battery floor the night of the vote. As if it had been ready.

· · ·

The Rows were cooler than the Commons, and the session room at the end of them was the coolest of all.

It was alive. Everything in it had been grown: the walls, the long low couch, the shelf at its head, all of a piece and honey-lit, warm under Rachel's fingers when she touched the couch's arm.

All except one thing. Set into the wall by the door was a steel lever on a heavy hinge, painted in yellow and black stripes and worn bright at the grip.

"Mechanical," Mara said. "No power, no lattice, no system. You pull it and the coupling breaks. Nothing up here can argue with it."

Rachel put her hand on it. It was cold.

She had brought the scope on paper. She laid it on the shelf, two pages, written out on the shuttle and copied clean, with a carbon under them. Mara read it standing, her palm on the wall.

One sweep. Twenty minutes, start to end. Four keepers in the loop, chosen by the lattice keeper. The lattice keeper on the lever and not in the loop. The lever pulled at the keeper's word alone. If it is pulled, the test is over and is not repeated. No second sweep without a sitting of the safety review and a new scope signed here. Every after-report on the four goes to the review unedited.

And at the bottom, in fresh ink, added at the junction an hour before and underlined: Haven's cargo slot restored, 2 April, without condition.

"The slot's back?" said Mara.

"Since an hour ago. Check it."

This pause was longer than the Earth one. Mara's eyes went inward, to her own lace, and came back.

"Why?"

"Because if you say yes with the air cut, it isn't a yes." Rachel kept her hands still on the shelf. "My side did that. I can't prove who, and I won't pretend it was weather. You can say no now and still breathe. It's the only way I'd believe you if you said yes."

Mara looked at her for a long time. Then she put a finger on the fourth line.

"Not in the loop. Why not me?"

"I've watched people make calls with their own skin in it. They're slow." Rachel held her eyes. "The one on the lever can't be the one who wants to stay."

"And when your director wants a second sweep?"

"He comes to my review. My review has a stop written into it that nobody can overrule. I wrote it."

"And if he tells me not to pull?"

"You pull anyway. And I'll sign that you were right to."

Mara read the scope again, all the way through, her lips moving on the numbers. Then she took a pen from her suit, an old one, and signed at the foot. She tore the carbon off in one motion and folded it into her pocket, and her other hand went back to the wall and stayed there, flat, the knuckles pale, as if she had told it something it would not like.

"Somebody taught you that," she said.

"A woman on a sandbar. She kept a list."

"Good." Mara laid her palm flat on the wall again, and for a moment she was not looking at Rachel at all. "Four of mine, then. My choice." She turned back. "You don't hide much, Iverson. For a Charter face."

"I don't hide anything."

"No," said Mara, after the pause. "I'd have seen."

· · ·

At night the Tubes dimmed, and somewhere down them a choir was rehearsing.

Rachel heard it from the Commons. She had brought her handheld up here because the guest cell was the size of a lift and she couldn't think in it. The children were gone, and their game.

Earth still hung in the dome, because from here it never set, and the brown thumbprint over the Gulf had turned into the night side and become a scatter of lights. The voices came up the Tubes in long chords, folded and carried by the rock, until the Commons was full of them.

She opened the test agreement. Mara's signature was on it. Bellamy's was on it, sent an hour after the slot went back. Her own field sat at the bottom, empty.

Chair's signature unavailable. 1 red flag open.

Her rule. Nothing in secret.

She opened the queue.

Two amber notes sat at the top, both from Elena. Engineering annex, objections: trace held at site, not shown to the review. And: Page 40, live coupling: precedent requested, not supplied. Rachel read them twice and set both to chase in the morning.

The red flag sat under them.

Hard-stop channel opened. No stop order logged. Transmission: Mars relay, calibration band, 17 March, 20:26. Billed to: Lantern safety review. Authorising credential: E. Vázquez, Foresight.

Sixteen days. Fourteen of them on the pumps, one on a shuttle, one here, and the review had not sat since that Thursday. The transmission had been in the log all along, small and grey in the margin, where anyone on the review could have read it. Nobody read margins. The flag was what made somebody read them, and flags came to the chair.

She opened the transmission.

It was three words long.

Who are you?

She sat with it under the dome while the choir went round again.

Talk back to what? the councillor from the outer boroughs had asked, the night of the vote. Bellamy had given her a good answer that wasn't one, and Rachel had filed it. Elena hadn't filed it. Elena had sat alone in the review room and built an answer by hand, and sent it on Rachel's channel, the one with the red line round it, the one Rachel had made the whole review stand on.

Foresight listened. It did not speak. Elena had written the training that said so.

If the flag stood, the review would have to sit on it. Her rule. The signature would wait for the sitting, and the sitting would wait for a city that had only just reopened its schools, and Mara's four, whoever she chose, would wait in the Rows on a scope that had been true when Mara signed it, and Mara, who had signed because nothing was hidden, would read the minutes with everyone else.

Elena would be off the review by the end of the sitting and out of Foresight by the end of the week. And the review would lose the only member who had read page forty.

Rachel looked up at Earth.

The choir held a long chord and let it go.

She touched the flag.

Clear flag? Clearing will be logged to the chair.

Of course it would. Elena had built the log.

"Bloody good log," Rachel said, under her breath.

She cleared it. The margin took it, small and grey: Flag cleared — R. Iverson, chair. The queue was empty. If Elena ever read her own margins, she would find Rachel's name under hers. Nobody else was there to see it, and she was the review.

She signed the test.

Her lace offered to call New York. She put Elena's name in the field and looked at it until the overlay dimmed itself to spare her eyes.

Then she turned the handheld face down on the table, and did not call.
