The Narrow Gate · Chapter 21

The Martian gate

The Narrow Gate · II-B. The old story

21

26 JuneElenainside the PreserveMars delay 21.7 min

TREATY STATION — MARS, THE PRESERVE / Elena Vázquez — Earth 21.7 min

The first thing Mars gave her was a river.

She was in it, or under it. The difference had stopped mattering. Water moved through rock above her in a long pull she felt as a hum, as she would feel a train through a platform, and the rock round her was wet and warm with it, and full. She had read the survey cores. She had read that Hellas once held a sea. None of the cores had told her it would feel like being carried.

Close by, two minds met.

She knew they were minds because she could feel the edges of them. They came toward each other through the wet as people come toward each other across a room, and touched, and for a moment each was in the other: what one saw, the other saw, and what one was afraid of, the other knew. Then they drew apart. Still two. Each a little different, and glad of it.

That was all it was. Meeting. Nobody had built it. It was how they said good morning.

"—worse. That was the big one." Ines's voice, a long way up, as if it came down a well. "Thirteen. You're fine. Fourteen. The tent held. My nerves didn't."

Her cheek was on rock. She knew that, distantly. Cold through the membrane under her hip, the tent's smell of wet stone and the heater's scorched dust, a hand on her chest through the suit's soft front, and the count.

"Your lace is writing," Ines said. "Every second of you, on my release. If this ends up in an inquiry I want it noted I was charming." The heater ticked. "Fifteen. And I'd like a chair. One with arms."

She wanted the beginning. She had come for it. Who built the boat, and why, and how it went wrong, step by step, so she could lay each step against a page she already had.

Mars gave her the drying instead.

The hum thinned. The pull through the rock slowed, and grew heavy, and began to sting. The rivers above went to brine as Vila Verde's reservoir had gone down through its rings, one dry summer at a time, except that here there was no rain to come back. Over the rock the sky thinned. She felt it as pressure going out of the world, a lid lifting off a pot, and the cold came lower every year. The wet drew in from the top. The minds drew in with it, closer, and the meetings that had cost nothing began to cost.

She had seen this. Kaelin had told her on the hall's roof, in pieces, with his hands. The first going out. The call.

"I know this part," she said, or thought, into the rock. "Show me the start of it."

The plain of slow light lay round her, and the attention in it, very far off and very tired, did not answer. The terms were short. She was spending them on what she already had.

Then, out of the silence, a small thing. A hand, hers, glove off, the back of it held up to a cage light. Grey marks on the skin.

What is on your hand?

She had written them in the tent with the air up, while Ines fought the socket cap, because she always wrote things on her hand, and because she had wanted them with her.

"Times," she said. "Fourteen. Nine. Eleven. Eight. Forty. How long five people go on working after they've gone home."

The silence changed. She felt the attention come round to her for the first time since she had laid her palm on the reader a week ago. Then it opened.

· · ·

She was standing at an edge.

That was the first thing she knew about the place Zhenyth had put her: it was a place for standing at, not in.

In front of her the wet opened into a pool, a narrow one, held in by rock on every side, and minds went into it. Not all of them. A few at a time. They went in and joined there, as two minds had joined in the river, except that here it was five, eight, a dozen, and it held. They thought together. She could feel what they thought about: which seams to close, where the last warm water should go, who could be reached in time and who couldn't.

They were saving people. She knew the feeling of a room doing that. She had stood at the foot of the Spire's boards for years.

Then they came out. Each one drew back to its own edge and went home, and another went in.

There was a pulse in the brine, made, regular, that everyone could hear. Somebody had built a clock. The turns were counted on it. In for so many pulses. Out for half as many. And her own place, the place at the edge, ran on a shorter count than any of theirs, because from here she held all their edges at once.

She felt one lean. A mind in the pool, too far in, its edge going soft against the others. She knew what to do with that before she knew she knew. She reached, and lifted, and it came out, and was itself, and a little ashamed, and went home.

Elena knew this room.

The reader on forty and twenty, a shorter clock than the chairs, because the reader holds every edge and tips first. An operator lifted out when they lean, whatever the work in hand. She had laid Kaelin's photograph of the March rota against her ridge-dish passes until she could have drawn it in the dark.

She had not expected to stand in it.

"Rachel," she said into the rock.

And under the clock, said to everyone who went in, as plain as a notice on a wall: This closes when the cold passes.

She heard it in Rachel's voice, though it was in nobody's. It closes on the third of August. On my name.

"Twenty to." Ines, up the well. "Elena. That's a lot of rock coming off the far wall. I'm watching it come." Grit hit the membrane above them and slid, a long hiss. "Twenty-one a minute. You're climbing. Talk to me."

She couldn't. She was holding the edge.

"Right. I've seen the cable, it's fine, and he's gone off the band. That's less fine. The blue's flickering all down the near wall." Ines's hand on her chest pressed and lifted, counting. "Short, they said. This isn't short. Up. Come up now."

Elena found her own hand, a long way down, lying on cold membrane. She closed it. She lifted it off the floor as far as her arm would go and held it there, a fist.

Ines said nothing for a while. Then, flat: "Sabi."

The hand on her chest stayed.

· · ·

The pulse in the brine counted a season, and the cold came down on the pool's rock and stayed. She felt it settle, through the watcher's edge. Another season. The clock went on. Minds went in on their turns and came out, and the ones who came out came out thinner. Not by much. A mind would draw back to its own edge and go home, and where a day had been there was a smooth place, and it didn't notice. Its friends did.

Aur'k in shelter four, writing in pencil the sentences he was about to lose.

The pool was kept open. Nobody chose to keep it open. It was only that every time the cold might have been said to pass, there was another reason, a seam about to go, a district of the wet too far off to reach any other way, and the pool was the one thing that could see far enough. For now, said the notice under the clock, and then it said nothing about closing at all.

Then she felt a mind go home, and the pool go on thinking with it.

It was faint at first. The shape of one mind's judgement still moving in there after the mind had drawn back to its own edge, a few pulses, then more. Fourteen. Nine. Eleven. Eight. She did not need to look at her hand.

Forty, at the head of the row.

She felt herself, the watcher, feel it too. She felt the watcher look at it a long time, and say nothing, because it was right every time.

The warmth came then.

It came from behind her and a little to the left, where she had been listening for a year.

Rain. Rain on a flat roof in Veracruz, on tin, the gutter running over, and under the rain many voices, glad, and one of them a laugh she knew. It didn't say anything. It didn't need to. It leaned toward her, as it leaned in the rain on the hall's roof, warm at the back of her head, and it was so glad she was here.

Her head went left. Up the well, in the tent, she felt her real head turn on the rock toward it, and she did not turn it back.

The hungry one knows her.

Zhenyth closed a door between her and it. She felt them do it, far off and very near, and hold it. And out at the rim of the plain, past the edge of what Zhenyth was showing her, a light went out.

Then another.

She knew what she was looking at. Aur'k had told her what each one cost, and put a figure for each in the tally in his square hand. A hand's width. A forearm's length. Tissue that doesn't grow back, spent so a stranger could stand at the edge of a pool three thousand million years drained and watch.

"The wall's going dark," said Ines. Her voice had changed. "Elena. By the tent. There goes another."

The rain leaned on the door. The door held, and another vein went dark to hold it.

She could come up. She had what she had come for, nearly. She had the gate, and the clock, and the promise, and the smooth places, and the tails. She could lay any of it in front of Rachel and Rachel would call it bleed, and be half right, because Elena had built half the line it came through and everybody knew what she listened for.

She didn't have the end.

She kept her fist up.

"Eighteen past." Ines, close, her breath loud in the tent. "The hour's coming. Elena. The hour."

On any other night the hour lifted the ward's traces and dropped them again. Tonight the rock was already moving.

Her fist stayed up. Ines didn't say anything else. Her hand stayed on Elena's chest, and Elena felt it press, and lift, and press.

· · ·

The watcher reached for one more.

A mind was leaning in the pool, far in, its edge going soft. It was the work. She reached for it as she had reached a thousand times, and took hold, and lifted.

Its edge did not come up out of the pool. It went on. It was folded into the edges round it, and those into the ones round them, and there was no place where it stopped and the pool began.

She reached for the way out, for it, for anyone. The way out was where it had always been, beside the clock. It had been there every turn of every season.

It wasn't there.

Nobody had taken it away. She felt that clearly, the watcher's horror and her own: no hand had closed it. The pool had only grown, a turn at a time, warm and right and full of everyone, until there was nowhere left to come out to. The ones who had gone home had been coming back in through their own smooth places for a long time, and calling it going home.

She, the watcher, turned to look at her own edge.

It went on and on.

The clock in the brine kept counting. In for so many. Out for half as many. Nobody went out. The pool called, warm and loud, in many voices under one, Come in. Nobody goes out alone in here, and the watcher stood at an edge that wasn't there any more, and went on standing at it, and watching, because that was the watcher's work, and the work was all that was left of being one.

Behind the door the rain laughed.

She had it now. All of it, in order.

Far up the well the roof groaned, a long sound in the stone, and Ines said, "Collar. Your collar, give me your—"

Short, said Zhenyth.

It was not a request. Zhenyth took her by the edge, as she had taken a hundred minds by theirs, and lifted.

The last she felt of the plain was one more light going out at its rim. Then Ines's hand pressing down hard on her chest, harder than any count, all its weight at once.

· · ·

Dark.

Her own breath, close and loud. That was the first thing: she could hear her own breath, and it came back at her off curved glass, an inch from her mouth.

She was in her helmet.

She had not put it on. She lay on her side with her cheek against the inside of her visor, not the rock, and the collar seal was made, and the suit's air was running, a small cold hiss at her neck. Someone had put her helmet on. Someone had sealed it, and checked it, and set her head down again where the blue had been thickest.

She opened her eyes. Her lamp had come on with the seal, and lit a hand's breadth of red membrane lying slack across her visor, grit on it. The tent was down. Through the slack of it the wall beside them was dark.

She could not move her legs. Something lay across them, heavy and soft, and above that, wedged, the hard edge of rock.

She turned her head inside the helmet, as far as it would go.

Ines was across her. Her helmet was on too, its lamp pressed against Elena's shoulder, the visor a hand from her own. Across Ines's back, from the shoulders down past where the lamp could reach, lay a slab of the roof, dark wet rock with one thread of blue still burning in it.

Ines's eyes were open. Her lips were moving behind the glass, on a count.

Her glove lay flat on Elena's chest plate. It had not moved. She was waiting for the breath.

Elena breathed.

"There you are," said Ines.
