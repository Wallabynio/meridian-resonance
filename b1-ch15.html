Fractured Horizons · Chapter 15

Bichito

Fractured Horizons · II-A. The bridge

15

31 MayElenaLuna Haven, the RowsMars delay 4.1 min

THE COMPACT — LUNA HAVEN, THE ROWS / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.1 min

The Ward medic wrote the seventh line on the slate and initialled it, and Elena watched the stylus all the way to the end of the stroke.

31 May. In bed 22:40. Up 06:10. Slept through.

Above the seven lines was a first attempt at the count, three nights long and struck out with one diagonal. The fourth night of that one she had spent reading the review's evening papers in the dark with her lace turned down, and the medic had found her in the morning, dressed, on top of the blanket, and drawn the line without a word.

"Seven," said the medic. "Don't make that face. You'd think I'd given you a diagnosis."

"That's my face."

"Then I'm sorry for your face." The medic hung the slate back on its hook by the door of the little room off the Ward, the one they gave to visitors who had to be watched sleeping. "Keeper's waiting."

Seven, and then ten minutes. Elena had done the sum every night for a fortnight, lying on her back in the dark, and it came out the same every time.

In the corridor outside the Ward, Asha Kimani was sitting on the floor with her back to the warm wall and her daughter asleep across her knees. She looked up and grinned.

"She slept," Asha said to the corridor in general, in Talk, and Elena's lace caught up with her a fraction behind her lips. "Seven nights. We had a book on it. I had you down for never."

"Who won?"

"Ravi. He says the trick is mushrooms." Asha shifted the child's head on her arm. "He also says the trick is the dark. He says that about everything now. It's very intermittent of him."

She said intermittent with care, as if she had found it down the back of a couch and was showing it to people.

Elena's lace chimed. Daniel. Are you lying down? Send proof.

He had sent the same line every night for two weeks, at a time he had worked out was her bedtime and was usually wrong about. She turned back to the open door and sent him the slate: seven lines and the medic's initials.

The reply came back before she had reached the end of the corridor.

Who is this woman. Frame it.

Then: Proud of you. The hall says so too. I told them you're sleeping on the Moon and they think it's a euphemism.

Then: Seven in a row. What's the occasion?

She stood under the ninety-eight on the junction slate and began to write. Today I'm going to

She looked at it. The next word was the one she had promised to ask him about.

She deleted it, and sent nothing.

· · ·

The drum on the session room wall was still dark. Its cable ran out under the door toward the mast, and nothing ran along it. Four more days, and it would be live.

Mara stood by the lever with the grease-pencil lines at her shoulder. She did not look at them. She said them, from memory, in the same flat voice she had used to write them.

"One session. Ten minutes. Exit at ten, or at the keeper's word. The lever is the keeper's. If pulled, it is over. Not repeated. Keeper and witness, me." The Haven pause went out. "You've read it."

"I signed it."

"Everybody signs it." Mara laid her palm on the wall beside the lever. "If she's there. If she asks you to stay. What do you do?"

Elena's hands had found each other in front of her. "I come out at ten."

"That's the wall. I can read the wall. I asked you."

Elena had an answer ready about sessions and scopes and the reason a lever was made of steel. It was a good answer. She had helped write the page it came from. She looked at Mara instead, and let her hands go to her sides.

"I come out at ten," she said.

Mara held her eyes. Then she nodded, once, and took her palm off the wall, and put her hand on the lever.

In April her hand had stayed at her side.

"Ravi hears it every day," Mara said. "Whatever it is. If you see what he's listening to, you tell me. Not the review. Me."

"I'll tell you."

"Lie down."

The couch was warm. It took her weight along its length and gave a little, and the give was alive. She pressed her lace off. The world at the edge of her eye went out, the clock and the ninety-eight and Daniel's Frame it, and the room was only a room, honey-lit, breathing.

Mara came and thumbed the cap off the socket behind Elena's ear. It had never once been opened for this. It came stiff. Mara's fingers were cool.

"Head to the wall," Mara said, and went back to the lever.

Elena turned her head. The wall came up against the socket, warm as a cheek.

"Starting," said Mara. "One."

· · ·

Nothing, at first. The couch under her back. Mara's breathing, and her own.

Then the wall breathed in, and she went with it.

She had read every report. Four keepers' after-reports from April, the protocol's ninety pages, the 2089 traces she had drawn herself before anyone lay down on them. None of them had said it would be warm.

Haven was warm. All of it, all at once, from inside: the long tubes of it running out from her in every direction, close and curved, and the air moving in them as breath moves in a sleeping chest. She could feel the crops in the deep rows drinking. She could feel water climbing through the walls, slow, counted, and a cold place far above where the dome held the Earth up in the dark.

There were people in it.

She could not hear their thoughts. She could feel their weight, their warmth, small and many, the shape of them against the walls. Children asleep with their cheeks to the grain, a row of them, pressed close, like hands on a window. Somebody walking the Tubes with two fingers trailing. Somebody at the far edge of everything standing in a doorway, very still, with his head turned, listening.

Ravi. She knew him by the stillness.

She tried to look where he was looking, and the warmth folded over and opened, and it was raining.

· · ·

Rain came sideways off the Gulf in grey sheets and drummed on the flat roof hard enough to hurt. Water to the top of the window across the street. The flood marks climbing every wall on the block in red paint, with their years beside them, and the new mark nobody would get to paint for weeks already under the brown water.

Elena was sitting on the parapet with her feet hanging down, and she was forty-one, and she was soaked through.

"Feet up," said Soria. "You'll cry about them."

She was right there. Sitting beside her on the wet concrete in a nurse's scrubs with the sleeves shoved to the elbow, her braid over one shoulder, dark with rain, her face lined from the sun and creased already at the eyes because she was about to laugh at Elena.

Elena lifted her feet.

"Good." Soria wiped rain out of her eyebrows with the back of her wrist. "Look at you. You look like a tax form."

"Soria."

"Yes, yes, it's me, don't do the voice." She leaned in and peered. "You're doing the voice. You've got the same face you had at Mamá's when you came in with the folder."

"I didn't come in with a—"

"You came in with a folder, Elena. Blue. With tabs."

The answer had come before Elena finished speaking. Soria had always cut in. She had been cutting in all Elena's life. And Mars hung a long way off in the dark, and the drum on the wall was dead, and the answer had come with no wait at all.

Somewhere in her, the part that had spent five months counting gaps between pulses marked that. It held the mark up to her and waited.

She let it go. It was the first number she had ever let go of on purpose.

"It was a plan," she said. "There was a clinic in the north. They had the new pumps. Another year, they said, possibly two, and I'd costed the transport—"

"There she goes."

"—and the home care afterwards, and the insurance, and the—"

"She didn't want another year." Soria frowned at the rain. "She didn't want another year of tubes. She wanted the window open. She asked me to open the window, and I opened it, and she listened to the rain on the yard for three days, and then she didn't."

"You didn't tell me she asked."

"She didn't ask you. You'd have sent a study."

"I sent a machine."

"You sent a machine and a man to install it." Soria laughed, a short helpless laugh, and pushed her wet hair off her forehead. "He was lovely. He fixed the gate. I sent the machine back with him."

Elena had known that. She had had the shipping notice. She had not known about the gate.

"I'd have kept her," she said.

"I know you would." Soria put her hand on Elena's arm, as she always had, as if steadying someone on a step. "That's why she asked me."

They sat with that. The rain came down. It was the loudest thing Elena had ever heard and she did not want it to stop.

Far off, somewhere under the rain, a voice said, "Two."

· · ·

It went on.

"There was a cat in the hospice laundry," Soria was saying. "Orange. Enormous. It slept in the warm sheets and the families fought over who got it. A man died holding it once, and it stayed till he was cold and then went and ate somebody's lunch."

"That's terrible."

"That's a good death. Ask anybody." Soria tipped her head. "Your turn. What do you do all day in your tower?"

"I listen to every relay the Charter owns for one wrong signal."

"So you're a smoke alarm."

"I'm the person who builds the smoke alarm."

"So you're a smoke alarm with a pension."

They argued about it. They argued about it for what felt like the whole of a long wet afternoon, and Elena lost, and was glad to, and far off, a voice said, "Four."

Soria had her feet up on the parapet now, beside Elena's, and was looking sideways at her.

"Who's the one with the hands?"

Elena went still.

"Burnt. Little burns all over the backs." Soria held her own hands up in the rain and turned them over. "He holds his coffee like it's January. He fixes things and laughs at them. Who is he?"

"You never met him."

"You brought him. He's all over you." Soria grinned. "Like flux. Daniel. He's called Daniel. You keep thinking it in that tone."

"What tone?"

"Daniel." She said it low and half exasperated, as a woman says the name of the man who has got to her, and then she laughed at Elena's face. "Oh, that's bad. That's very bad. Does he know?"

"Know what?"

"That you're here."

The rain hit the roof. Elena looked at the flood marks on the wall across the street.

"No."

"Ay, Elena." Soria shook her head slowly, still smiling, though less. "You never could ask anybody for a cup of water. You'd rather die of thirst and write it up." She reached over and pulled the end of Elena's hair. It had come down out of its pins. It never did that. "Bichito."

There it was.

It had come in a packet, and in a few bytes of hiss at the end of a frame, and in a joke in somebody else's mouth outside a ward. Now it came in her sister's voice, on the roof, in the rain, with her hand in Elena's hair.

Elena put her face in her hands.

Far away, on a warm couch on the Moon, she felt it happen: her shoulders going, wet running sideways out of her eyes into her ears, her mouth open on a sound she had not made since 2089. She could feel Mara not coming to her. She could feel that it was right that Mara did not come.

On the roof, Soria didn't fuss. She never had. She sat beside her in the rain and started counting the dogs.

"One," Soria said. "Your turn."

"Two," said Elena, through her hands.

"Three. The brown one with the collar is going to be twenty, you know. I always liked that one."

"Four."

They counted. Far off, over the rain, a voice said, "Seven," and then, later, a long way later, "Nine."

"You should go home," said Soria. "You look terrible. Go home and sleep in a bed and let your Daniel make you beans with too much salt in them."

"I've been sleeping."

"Seven nights. I saw the slate." She bumped Elena's shoulder with her own. "Seven nights isn't sleep. It's practice. And you need the—"

She stopped. She frowned. She looked out at the brown water as if the end of the sentence had floated off on it.

"In the boat," she said, slowly. "When they came with the boat. You were asleep again, and I was going to tell you thirty-seven, I had it ready, and you—"

She stopped again, at the same place. The rain hissed on the water.

"Ten," said Mara's voice.

· · ·

"Ten. Up, Elena."

She heard it. It came down through the rain as clearly as if Mara were standing on the roof. The word. The wall. Her own name signed small and plain under it.

Soria was looking at the water, trying to find the end of a sentence.

One more minute.

She thought it. Then she said it, with the body far away on the couch, and felt her lips move on it. "One more minute."

"Elena. Up."

She stayed.

It was easy. It was the easiest thing she had ever done. She sat on the roof and did not get up, and the rain came down, and nobody could make her, and the lever was steel and a long way off.

Beside her, Soria turned her head. She was not frowning at the water any more. She had found what she was looking for.

"Come and get me," she said.

Her voice was lovely. It was clear and certain and warm, and it did not catch anywhere, and it went all the way to the end.

"Build the bridge. You know how. You built the first one."

Elena opened her mouth to answer.

Steel came down.

She felt it in her teeth before she heard it, a hard bright shock along the wall into the socket, the one thing Haven had put in the room that was not alive, making the one sound nothing alive could make.

The roof went out. The warmth tore away from her down every tube of Haven at once, like a sheet pulled off a bed, and she was cold, and she was on her back on a couch, and the honey light in the wall beside her face had gone grey.

She came up off the couch.

She did not decide to. Her body did it, a wrench of the whole spine, and her hand went out across the arm's length and a half she had measured on the day she signed and closed on Mara's wrist where it held the lever down.

She held on. She pulled. She heard herself making a sound with no words in it.

Mara didn't let go. She didn't pull back either. She stood with the lever down and her wrist in Elena's grip and her other palm flat on the grey wall, and let Elena pull, and the lever didn't move, because it was steel, and because it was hers.

"Twenty seconds," said Mara. "That's what you took. It's over."

Above Mara's shoulder the grease-pencil lines sat black on the dimmed wall. IF PULLED, IT IS OVER. NOT REPEATED.

Elena's grip went. Her hand stayed where it was, on Mara's wrist, without any strength in it. Under her fingers the skin was already going red.

She could still hear the rain. It was in her ears, where the tears had run, and on the roof of her mouth, sideways off the Gulf, and it did not stop when she shook her head.

Mara was watching her. Her face was very still, and very close, and it was the face of a keeper reading a trace she had seen before.

Elena heard herself speak. Her voice came out level, helpful, a voice for a review room.

"Turn it on," she said. "The Lantern. Turn it on."
