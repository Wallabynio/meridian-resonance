The Narrow Gate · I. The countdown

08

14 MarchElenaVila VerdeMars delay 17.6 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE, THE COOPERATIVE / Elena Vázquez — Mars 17.6 min

The rota came up the north line in nine pieces and arrived in the wrong order. That was how Elena knew it had come the long way.

The hall's oldest handheld chirped each piece in from the roof as it landed. By seven she had them all. Daniel's mother put her coffee down on the rota table and leaned over the screen with her glasses on their cord, and the three of them looked at a photograph of a kitchen table in New York.

A sheet of paper, folded in half and opened again. Beside it, the edge of a salt cellar and a printed photograph of a scratched grid with a washer in one corner. Under the picture, the one line Kaelin had sent with it: Back under the salt by seven. K.

Mãe studied the board, and the salt, and the fold in the paper.

"He put it back square," she said. "Look. Square to the salt."

"He's a spacer," Elena said. "They put things back."

"Then he can come again."

Four chairs and a reader. A clock column down the left, New York time. Names in the boxes: LIN Y., RAMAN D., MOSS A., REYES J. At the head of every block, AUR'K — READER, in his own column: forty minutes on and twenty out, the last forty of the night closing on the change with the chairs.

Raman she knew, from two corridors away in Foresight: a man who said the worst outcome aloud in meetings so he could stop thinking it. Reyes she knew better. She thought of the four chairs under their clocks, and Joaquín's hand on the inner door, and moved on.

She added an hour to every line. Three days ago it would have been two. New York had put its clocks forward on Sunday, and Vila Verde never did.

The Gate ran on the Spire line. The Spire line had kept her sister on every machine for six hours after her heart stopped. She wanted to see a chair go on working after it was empty. That was all the rota was for.

She opened the roof notebook where the passes were, between the roofs and their faults.

Eleven nights on the ridge dish since February. Daniel's shoulder on the hand crank, a relay over the western ridge, ten minutes of the Mars band and then hiss. She set the rota beside them, logoff by logoff.

None of them met. The floor changed chairs on the hour. The relays high enough for the dish had crossed the valley at twenty past and ten to, all month.

"So," she said.

"So you need a different ear," Daniel said. He had a board between his knees and the iron cooling on its stand.

"I need every pass. All night."

Mãe had not sat down. She was looking at the mesh map on the wall, the north line running off the top onto its second sheet and its third.

"The cousin at the switchboard," she said in Portuguese, slowly, so Elena would get all of it. "Yesterday a man from the network office asked him which licence the ridge mast is on. Whose name." She drank. "The cousin told him he would look. He is still looking. He is slow when he wants to be."

The fan in the doorway pushed the morning's one cool hour round the room.

The ridge mast was on the rota, under the cooperative's name, in his mother's own marker. The hall had signed it. Somebody had read it.

Elena closed the notebook. She already knew where the other ear was. She had walked past it every day since the summer, down below the old line's embankment, where the town went for water before it went for anything else.

She did not say so. She said, "I'll think about it," and Daniel looked up from the board, and back down, and she let him.

· · ·

The hall's screen had been built to show the weather, and the old man's box had been built to steal the football, and on Wednesday night, for the first time since Elena had come, both did what they were for.

The home side under lights, the picture a second behind the sound and grainy from the ring. Forty people on the hall's floor, the cistern room's children on the stairs. The bakery man had brought his own cup to bang. He banged it at the kick-off, at a corner, at a throw-in that went the wrong way.

When the goal came in the seventieth minute he banged it so hard the handle came off, and he went on banging it without one.

Daniel shouted at a defender by name, as if he could hear. The old man sat in the front row with his arms folded and told everybody near him that the feed had been his idea in 2084 and nobody had believed him. The children did the full air ring at the screen, at each other, at Elena.

She made it back. She had the notebook open on her knee under the table's edge, and on a roof page she had drawn the pump house's controller from memory: the grey box on its post, the panel on its top tilted at the sky, the keeper's lock, the cable going down into the concrete.

She had drawn an arrow on the panel: it turned on its own, relay to relay, for the water authority, with more gain than the ridge dish would ever have. It didn't need a crank.

At the final whistle the hall went out into the street with the noise, and Daniel didn't. He sat down beside her on the bench with his elbows on his knees and looked at the page.

"That's the reservoir," he said.

"It's the controller."

"Were you going to tell me after?"

"Yes," she said. "After. So you could say you didn't know."

"I don't want to say I didn't know." He said it as flatly as he quoted the price of solder. "The route. The vault. Haven. I've sat on a roof and been told what it cost when it was already spent. I don't want to hear about this one. I want to be in it."

"It's water, Daniel." She kept her voice down, though the hall was empty. "If they find a tap on that box, it isn't the mast licence. It's the allocation. It's every tank on this hill. It's your name."

"That's why." He put his finger on the keeper's lock in her drawing. "The cooperative keeps that pump. My mother's licence, my key. It opens on my key every month for the filters, and the authority gets a line that says the keeper opened it. It gets that line every month." He sat back. "You were going to break it open."

She had been. A pry bar and a story about the filters.

"A broken door gets an inspector," he said. "A keeper's key gets a line."

"A line with your name on it."

"Yes."

She looked at him. Solder burns on the backs of his hands, black hair standing up where he had pushed it, and the thing he had asked her for in a dozen different words. Ask me next time.

She had asked him about the dish, and only ever about the dish.

"All right," she said. "Bring the key."

· · ·

The pump house stood under the old line's embankment, where the reservoir's shore had been before the dry years moved it. The water lay fifty metres off now, down a slope of mud still wet from the season and drying pale at its top edge, ringed white on brown with every level it had ever been.

The frogs had come back with the rain and were shouting about it. The air smelled of mud and warm algae and the creosote on the sleepers above them.

The controller stood on its post outside the pump house door, the only thing down there nobody in Vila Verde had made. Grey Continuity housing, the authority's stamp worn to a ghost, its panel tilted at a point of light Elena couldn't see. Inside, the pump turned over in its sleep.

At 23:00 the controller's light blinked twice. The level report, going up to the ring.

"Next one at midnight," Daniel said. "If it's late, they send a man."

"Then it won't be late."

He put his key in the keeper's lock. The housing opened with a click that sounded, to her, louder than the frogs.

Inside it was clean and dull and old, and she loved it at once, as she had loved every honest machine. The receiver in its own shielded box behind the panel's feed. The level logger below it. The radio that spoke for both. Nobody had touched any of it since it was built except to change a filter and swear.

She held the handheld's light. He did the work, because his hands were steadier at a cable than hers and because it was his town's water.

He opened the receiver's box with a screwdriver ground thin for this and nothing else, found the line from the feed, and laid the tap across it. A coupler the size of a thumb, the old man's design, taking a sliver of whatever the panel heard and handing it to a node they had wrapped in a bread bag and taped under the housing's lip.

The first seating was a hair off. The receiver's own light flickered.

"Hm," Daniel said, gently, and took it all out and did it again. It flickered again. The coupler wanted holding while the pass came over, until the receiver had the relay and the tap had the receiver, and Daniel's hands were in the box to the wrist with the feed.

"I'll hold it," Elena said, and did, two fingers flat on the coupler against the line, with the relay crossing somewhere over the reservoir in the black.

Her head was bent over the box, an inch from the feed, and the Gate was on the band it carried now, four chairs under a harbour, its frames coming over with the rest. The warmth at the back of her head felt them before she did. It turned, slowly, like a sleeper turning toward a voice in the next room, and it was so gentle, and she was so tired.

She let it.

"Elena."

Daniel's voice, very close. His hand over hers on the coupler, wet from the box. The receiver's light was flickering. On the handheld in her other hand the clock read 23:31.

The last thing she remembered it reading was 23:27.

"You went away," Daniel said. "With your eyes open. I said your name four times, and then I took my hands out of the feed." He didn't let go of hers. "We've lost the seating."

"It needed holding."

"Not by you." He looked at her face as he looked at a joint he didn't trust. "What did you hear?"

She didn't answer. He heard that too.

He did it again with his own two fingers on the coupler, and hers on the light, held well back. The pass was going over. At 23:46 the receiver's light came steady and the bread-bag node went green, fourteen minutes before the blink.

She looked at her fingertips. The coupler had left two white marks on the skin where they'd pressed. She looked at them, and at him, and had nothing to tell him that he hadn't watched.

Daniel sealed the box. He closed the housing. He turned his key, and the controller logged it, KEEPER — DOOR CLOSED, in letters that would sit in its memory until morning and then go up to the ring with the rest of the day.

At midnight the light blinked twice. On time.

He sat down on the concrete step with his back against the pump house wall and breathed out, and the frogs went on shouting.

"Pronto," he said.

"Pronto."

She sat next to him. Behind them, the pump turned over. In front of them the reservoir lay black under the ring, and somewhere up in that black a relay was crossing, and for the first time the valley was hearing all of it.

· · ·

From the hall's roof she watched the first pieces leave.

The bread-bag node's light went green. The standpipe node at the bottom of the low street answered it. Then the roof above the shop where she had waited out the rain in February, and the school roof, and the bakery's.

Each one took its piece and passed it on, so that no one roof held the whole of it. Green and green and green, up the far side of the valley in terraces, along the ridge to the mast and beyond it, north, toward towns on the map's second sheet.

She stopped watching the handheld and looked up.

The whole hillside, which the Continuity's map showed as a grey blank between two named streets, was lit and ticking. A second sky under the first one, with the ring's beads crossing it. Past the ridge, off the edge of the map's last sheet, a roof she had never seen came up green, and then another beyond it, towns answering towns. The Gate's map, somewhere under the Battery, showed nothing here at all.

Daniel lay beside her on the warm panels with his eyes shut. "Is it working?"

"It's working."

"Bom."

She called the pieces home pass by pass and laid them against the rota.

The Gate's frames were the Lantern's frames. The Lantern's were the Spire line's, and the Spire line's were hers. In 2087 she had argued for one field in the header that nobody else wanted: the head a frame came from, so a fault could be traced to its chair. It was still there.

LIN Y. off chair one at midnight New York, with the whole block on the same change. One o'clock here. She found the pass that straddled it. Chair one's frames, small and steady, a few millimetres at a time: Lin's rhythm.

At one o'clock they should have stopped. The relief had sat down in chair one at the change, and her frames came in under her own session number. These still carried Lin's.

They thinned. One frame in ten, then one in thirty. Still small careful corrections, still Lin's shape. Fourteen minutes after Lin Yao, by the rota, had stood up and unhooked and gone home.

She checked which way they were going before she let herself have it. Up. Outbound from the floor, stamped by the line's own clock, after the chair was empty. Anything Mars said back came down, and came late, and she knew to the minute how late. These weren't answers. They were a chair still asking.

She went down the row. Raman, chair two, off at the same change. His frames came crowded and ahead of themselves, a man counting before anyone else. After his logoff they went on, faint, for nine minutes.

Moss, chair three. Eleven.

Reyes, chair four. Eight, and stronger than any of them: full frames, certain, as if he had never stood up.

She sat very still. Then she went to the head of the row, AUR'K, his last forty closing on the change, the man who left his rota under the salt.

Forty minutes.

Fainter than the others. Longer than all of them. His frames went on at the head of the row, small and exact, at the edges of things, after the rota said he had gone home.

She wrote the numbers in pencil on the back of her hand, because the notebook was on the far side of Daniel. Fourteen. Nine. Eleven. Eight. Forty. They were tiny. Anyone who needed them to be noise could call them noise. She had built the threshold that would have called them that.

Then, between one pass and the next, she slept.

She didn't mean to. She hadn't slept a night through since June. She went down into it as into warm water, on the panels in the warm dark, all at once, and it was the deepest sleep she had had in nine months, and somewhere at the bottom of it there was rain on the roof where she and Soria had grown up, and she knew whose sleep it was.

She woke at a quarter to five with the handheld still in her hand. Daniel hadn't slept. He sat with his back against the parapet and the keeper key on its string in his hand, turning it over, watching her.

At 04:58 the controller's morning beacon went up from the pump house: the day's levels and the day's log. The tap heard it go. The tap heard everything the panel heard.

KEEPER: ROCHA, D. — DOOR OPENED 23:04. DOOR CLOSED 23:58.

Daniel read it over her arm. "That's me," he said, as if he were checking a name on a rota, and put the key back in his pocket.

She had known it would go. She watched it go anyway. Up to the ring, down to the authority, and on to whoever had asked the switchboard about the mast. She thought of a pod at the Battery's kerb with her name on its strip, keeping her pace through the rain. Rachel's desk would read this line. Now it would read his name next to hers.

He didn't look away from it. Neither did she.

There was one more pass before dawn. She ran it against the rota out of habit. Chairs off and fading. Nothing new. Then, deep in the traffic, under everything, one frame that didn't fit.

The field she had fought for in 2087 was empty. It came from no chair at all. The session field kept the number of whoever was on the line. It held a number nobody had used since August 2089.

Thirty-one.

Her head turned a little to the left, toward nothing, as it did. She made herself turn it back. She decoded the frame's residue by hand, because her hands knew how.

Bichito.
