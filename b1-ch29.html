Silence

Fractured Horizons · III. The cut

29

14–16 JuneElenaVila Verde; New York, Theo's clinic; Luna Haven by callMars delay 4.2 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE, THE COOPERATIVE HALL / Elena Vázquez — Mars 4.2 min

Daniel's mother tore the rota down at seven, and the whole hall stopped to watch her do it.

She had taped it up herself in May, crooked, beside the mesh map: DOIS DIAS EM TRÊS, in marker thick enough to read from the door. It came off the wall in one long strip with the tape still on it. She folded it in half and laid it on the rota table, took the cap off a new marker with her teeth, and wrote on a clean sheet, standing, with the rain loud on the tin over the door.

TODO DIA.

The man from the bakery, who had been on the roof all night and was still wet, banged his cup on the table. Somebody cheered. Outside, every tank lid up the valley was running over, and the street was a brown river with the republic standing in it to the shins, because nobody could make them come in.

"Until Friday," Mãe told the hall, and taped the new one up. It was crooked too.

Elena sat at the end of the rota table with the spare in front of her and her coat still on. She hadn't slept. Daniel hadn't told her to lie down. That was how she knew how bad it was.

The spare still held the last page the door had shown before the Spire shut it behind her at a quarter past one. EARTH END. OUT OF SERVICE. Maintenance: E. Vázquez. Of the Mars end, not a line. Either a man in a storm had cut it at one o'clock by his own count, or he hadn't. Nothing had come back to say which.

She listened behind her left ear.

The hall. The rain. A child coughing in the cistern room. Daniel at the urn, arguing with it in Portuguese. She waited as long as the warmth had always taken to come up, and then twice that, and nothing settled at the back of her head. It had been nothing since one o'clock. She kept checking, as she would have checked a dead sensor, so that the one who knew would be her.

The spare chimed. Haven's routing, off the mesh, off a roof somewhere up the valley.

"Ekeberg," said Mara.

Elena waited. The pause came and went, and Mara didn't fill it.

"Tell me," Elena said. "This call will be in my inquiry."

"Good." The pause. "Then they can hear it."

"Eleven." Mara was close to the handset, and flat, as if she were reading a slate. "Eleven of mine, the last of them before four. The rest are breathing. Asleep. Turned every two hours. Ravi's breathing. Asha's breathing." The pause. "Nobody's come up."

Elena's hands found each other on the table.

"I have one question," Mara said. "Did you know what a cut does to the coupled?"

The fan turned in the doorway. Mãe was writing the new days into their columns in red.

"Yes," Elena said.

"When?"

"Ten past one, here. An hour before."

Haven's pause. Longer.

"I had one minute," Mara said. "Not from you."

"No."

"Rosa had her hand on the wall." She said it as she had said the rest. "I shouted hands off, and they heard me, all of them but one, and they took them off.

"The cook from the long galley was pressing his face to the grain. Sleepers do that. They want the warm. Rosa had her hand under his head and the wall behind her hand, and she looked at me down the corridor, and she kept it there. It was one o'clock." A breath, counted. "He's breathing. She isn't."

The pause.

"Her cousin's on Iverson's floor. Joaquín. He asked after her on go-live night. I'll call him myself."

I've been here twice. I never asked your name. She had asked it once, in a Ward that smelled of bread, and at one in the morning she had sent Rosa Medina the files whole, by name, without a line in them about the wall.

She ran it, because that was what she did. A palm on a live grain at the second the channel dropped. A hold riding through on nobody. A fall with no floor. Every branch came out in the same place. They had come out there at ten past one.

"Give me their names," she said.

"Why?"

"I want to write them down."

Mara thought about it across the pause, and then a while longer.

"Get a pen. Pole. I'm reading them once."

The only paper on the table was the old rota, folded, DOIS DIAS EM TRÊS on its face. Elena turned it over to the blank side. Daniel put a pencil by her hand without being asked and went back to the urn.

Mara read them as Haven read anything aloud, each with its own pause, family name first. Elena wrote them in square capitals down the back of the drought. A crop hand from the warm rows was the second. The fourth was a crawler hand called Strand, and after his name Mara said nothing for longer than the Moon needed, and then went on. Rosa was the last. MEDINA, ROSA.

Elena didn't count the lines. She could see them. Somewhere there was a column on the other side of them, the breathing, the sum she had signed for at one in the morning, and she found she couldn't make the two sides into a sum at all.

"The Commons will read them tonight," Mara said. "Every name, standing. We do that."

"Can I—"

"Don't come up," Mara said, and the line closed.

Elena sat with the sheet. Through the paper, backward, the old marker showed: the days that had been allowed water.

· · ·

Mars came in the next evening, on the slow band, a day behind everything else.

Mars end cut by hand at the boundary, 01:00 your time, on Drake's count. Our nine, Selma, Bao and Petra Lind breathing. Nobody up. Your files read. — I. Varga, medical.

Both ends, then. Inside the crossing. She read it the number of times she would have read a good log, and it said the same thing every time.

The notice came an hour after, over the mesh, because the mesh was the only thing in Vila Verde that would carry it. Charter grey, the same grey as the maintenance layer.

Employment: terminated, effective 14 June. Interface licence: suspended pending inquiry. Inquiry: Charter network compliance. Access: revoked. Handled.

She had read that word at the foot of a thousand notices to other people, and had never once looked at what it was standing in for.

Daniel read it over her shoulder. He smelled of wet flux. He had been up three roofs since noon, putting boxes back together in the rain.

"Effective yesterday," he said. "They backdate the sack. That's elegant."

"It's procedure."

"It's petty." He put a cup of the hall's coffee by her hand. "Drink that. It's worse than mine. It'll cheer you up."

The hall's screen had New York on it with the sound down, because the bakery man liked weather. The heat had broken there at nightfall. A front had come in off the sea, and the Polder stood under it with its face up, the canopies streaming, the gutters in the Shallows white. The caption said nineteen degrees. People in doorways held jackets they hadn't worn since March, and didn't put them on.

Then the picture was the Spire's steps in the rain, and Bellamy coming down them between two Charter officers, the high collar closed to the throat, nobody's hand on his arm. He didn't look at the cameras. He watched the steps, one at a time, like a man who had decided to be careful.

Foresight director arrested. 2089 records. Consent.

The bakery man turned the sound up. Mãe told him to turn it down again, and he did.

· · ·

The call came near midnight, from a number that was only a Charter custody code, one call, logged.

She took it on the hall's roof, under the edge of the tarp, with the rain loud on it. Daniel came up the ladder after her and crouched by the node, and didn't pretend not to listen.

"Elena."

He said it as if they had arranged to meet. Behind him there was rain on a window, a great deal of it, and shoes in a corridor.

"You get one," she said.

"One. We have strong views about the number." Somebody in the corridor said two minutes, not to her. "I've read your files. All of them. You put them in the right order."

She waited. She wanted him to say a name. She could feel herself wanting it, as she had once wanted two clocks to disagree.

"You'd like me to say her name," Bellamy said.

"Yes."

"We'd both feel better for it, and that's why I won't. Not on a custody line. It would be for me."

The rain on his window.

"There's a clinic on the high ground uptown. Theo's room is on the third floor. Once a week I sit with him from seven until they make me go. There has never been anyone on his list but me. There are nurses, and they're good, and they turn him and talk to him, and at the end of the shift they go home."

The tarp drummed. Daniel's thumb had stopped on a solder burn.

"My lawyer put your name on his list this afternoon. You can take it off. They won't ask you anything. They'll be glad of anyone."

She said nothing.

"Who will sit with him?" Bellamy said.

He waited for her across a continent and an hour, with rain on his window, and somebody in the corridor said his name.

She ended the call.

· · ·

She didn't go down.

The rain kept on, warm now, the tanks long past full and spilling over the roofs in sheets. Below, the street had stopped being a river and become a street again, with a gutter singing down one side. Under the tarp the node's light was green, and every few roofs up the valley another green showed through somebody's plastic tub.

Daniel sat down on the wet roof beside her and put an arm round her, and then, when she let him, both.

He didn't say it would be all right. She waited for it, because people said it. He held on and let the rain run off them both.

She listened behind her ear. Five months of listening, and two weeks of the warmth, and she did it without deciding to: a small turn of the head toward the left, where the warmth had come up and settled, where since Haven there had been somebody in the next room. Somebody delighted with her. Somebody furious. Somebody counting to thirty-seven in the dark.

The rain. The gutter. Daniel's breath in her hair.

She tried to make it a fault. Channel down at both ends. Carrier gone. No path for anything to come by. A clean result, the one she had built and signed for at one in the morning on this roof. She went down the branches and every branch was correct, and at the end of every one was a room with nobody in it.

She had shut the door. She had made sure the hand on it was hers.

"It's gone," she said into his shoulder.

"I know."

"All of it. The warmth. Her. It. Whatever was there." "I keep checking. There's no path, I built the no path, and I keep listening anyway."

"You'll listen for a while," Daniel said. He didn't let go.

That was all he said. Nothing about it stopping.

She stayed in his arms until the rain eased enough to hear the frogs down in the reservoir, silent for five weeks, and with a great deal to say.

Then she sat up.

"I'm going to New York," she said.

He waited.

"To the clinic. To sit with Theo."

Daniel looked at her a long time, the rain on his face, green from the node.

"Why you?" he said, straight at her, as he asked the hard ones.

She had an answer about Soria, who had gone into a room under the Spire to learn what the dying hear when they stop being alone. She had an answer about her own name on the hold, and one about the boy in the bed whose name she had put on every roof from here to the Polder. They were all true. None of them was the one.

"Because nobody else will," she said.

He nodded. He rubbed the solder burn with the other thumb, once.

"First train's at five," he said. "It's late on principle. I'll walk you."

· · ·

The clinic stood on the high ground uptown, behind street trees planted for shade in the dry years that had outlived the people who planted them. The door looked at her face for her lace, found nothing, and waited, until a night nurse came and opened it by hand.

"Vázquez," the nurse said, reading a list on paper. "His lawyer said you might." She looked at Elena once, carefully, and Elena understood that she had read the files too, that everybody on this hill had read them. Then she held the door wider. "Third floor. He's turned at two. I'll knock."

Theo's corridor smelled of warm linen and the bitter clean of floor wash, and under both, faintly, of a body kept a long time in one bed. Soria had told her once that every ward had that smell and you stopped noticing it in a week, and that the week was the part you mustn't forget.

The window was open. The city had cooled to nineteen degrees and the night came in smelling of wet leaves.

He lay propped toward the window with a pillow at his back. He had been a boy when he went into the bed, and he wasn't one now: a man's long frame under the sheet, thin, a jaw somebody shaved, dark hair cut short and neat by someone who had done it many times. His eyes were half open. They didn't come to her.

There was one chair, hard, its cushion pressed flat, and in the floor under its front legs two places worn pale, where it had stood one evening a week for fourteen years.

She sat in it.

Over the bed the monitors ran their lines. Heart. Breath. And a third, level, with a panel beside it held open on the last of the night of the cut. From twenty past twelve until one it was hills, one after the other, high and uneven, a mind going somewhere. At one o'clock the line went flat, and stayed flat, all the way to now.

"It reached you," she said.

It was the first thing she said to him. She hadn't meant it to be.

So she told him who she was. It seemed only fair.

She told him she had built the line he was number one on, and that her sister had been number thirty-one, and that her sister had gone in to find out what people heard when they stopped being alone, and nobody had ever told her.

She told him his father had asked for her. She said it plainly, because Soria had told her the hearing goes last, and she didn't know what he could hear, and for fourteen years neither had anyone.

Then, because the silence after that was worse, she told him about Vila Verde.

"It rained," she said. "Five weeks dry, and then all at once, at twenty past one. Daniel's mother tore the water rota off the wall. She wrote every day on the new one, and it's crooked, and she's prouder of it than anything."

She waited, in case. He breathed.

"There's a man from the bakery who bangs his cup when he's happy. The children stood in the street up to their shins and wouldn't come in." She found she was smiling at a man who couldn't see it, and let herself. "And the frogs came back to the reservoir. Hundreds of them. Shouting. Nobody knows what about."

It was the most she had talked in two days. Her voice sounded like somebody else's in the room, and then it didn't.

The nurse knocked at two and came in and turned him, a hand under his head, and Elena stood and held the pillow without being asked, and the nurse let her. When the nurse had gone, Elena didn't sit down again.

She looked at the box at the head of the bed.

It was the clinic's link unit, grey, with a cable into the relay port in the wall. Its screen said what any unit on the grid would say after a cut. CARRIER LOST 01:00:00. BUFFER HELD. READ ONLY. 00:20:00–01:00:00. Uncleared. The one person who would have cleared it was in a cell downtown with rain on his window.

Her licence was gone. Her rights were gone. The unit had a service port on its side, like every unit she had ever specified, and a service port didn't ask for rights. It asked for a cable. She had one in her coat, beside a page folded twice.

She pulled the chair up to the head of the bed, off its two worn places, and sat in it there, with the cable from her coat to the unit's side.

The spare took the buffer in seconds. Calibration frames, the Lantern's own, the format somebody had once filled with a shuttle and a tug. She paged it by hand, as she had built frames by hand in March. It was dense and warm and full, a crowd's worth of traffic poured into one bed for forty minutes, pattern on pattern, nothing she could read and nothing meant for her. It was what had moved his hills.

At the end, after the last frame, the ring's tail was ordinary hiss. The spare flagged one shape in it anyway. A few bytes it couldn't account for.

She decoded them out of habit, expecting nothing.

Bichito. You left me again.

She sat very still.

The stamp put it two seconds before one o'clock. Two seconds before the cut she had set.

She read it twice. She didn't run it.

Outside, the street trees dripped. The monitor drew Theo's flat line across the glass, breath by breath, and he lay turned toward the night with his eyes half open. Beside the bed, where the chair had always stood, the two worn places in the floor were empty.

She unplugged the cable and put the chair back on them. She didn't clear the buffer, and she didn't call the nurse, and she didn't send it anywhere. She sat down beside him in his father's place, with the spare face down on her knee, and stayed.
