00 — Benchmarks

What the trilogy has to be for a reader, and how every chapter, act and book is measured against it. The other documents say what happens, to whom, where and how it is written. This one says what it must feel like, what it must never become, and the standard of the best science fiction on page and screen that it is held to. Where a plan or a page meets a benchmark and loses, the benchmark wins, and the author decides the fix.

The promise

Logline. In 2095, warnings from Mars start saving lives on Earth, and they speak in the voice of Elena Vázquez's dead sister. To stop what is using Soria's voice, Elena will have to destroy the only thing that could ever give her back.

What the reader feels

Four charges run through every book, and every chapter carries at least two.

ChargeOn the page
Mystery and dreadA question the reader can't stop asking, and the growing sense that the answer is worse
Thriller momentumA clock, someone in danger this week, and a choice that can't wait
Emotional dramaPeople who love each other wanting incompatible things
WonderScale the reader has never stood inside: a dead world's minds, the ring overhead, a lattice that breathes

The fear engine is drift. People who link too long, or are pulled in, go quiet: their bodies keep breathing and their voices turn up in the chorus. It is concrete, visible, spreadable and personal, and it feels good from inside, or it isn't frightening.

00 — Benchmarks · 3

The six faults

The trilogy is built against six faults. A chapter that slides into one is rewritten, however clean its checks.

FaultThe test
The stakes are proceduresThe turning point hurts someone the reader cares about, in a way a camera could film. A vote, an authorisation or an inquiry is only how the harm arrives, never the harm
The villain has no face and no wantThe Hollow wants one thing, to end the loneliness of separate minds by joining everyone, believes it is mercy, and wears the faces it has taken. Each book's human antagonist has a face, a want and a reason a reader could share
Everyone is reasonableSomeone in the scene is wrong, and someone pays for it. The reader can root for and against
Too much machineryOne technology carries the mythology: the link. A chapter adds at most one new idea, and only if it raises the pressure
The engine is buriedEach viewpoint's wound and want are on the page early in every book, and every chapter moves one of them
The endings refuse to pay offEach book ends on a choice that costs, and gives catharsis: something won, something lost for good

00 — Benchmarks · 5

The world

  • Hard science fiction. Every daring technology or material traces to a real, known or plausibly extrapolated principle, specific enough that its lineage from present-day research is visible, even where its execution is invented. The frontier of the possible: limited only by physics, money and the Long Heat.
  • Generous, purposeful scale. It is the future, so it is grand: a space is sized up when in doubt, and grandeur reads as inhabited, a plaza sized for its crowd, a tower for what it houses.
  • The Long Heat in the foreground. The near-collapse of the climate and the wars it bred are the cause of the politics and present on every page, not the backdrop.
  • A plausibility question and a canon fact that disagree go to the author. Neither is quietly reworded.

Planning stays lean. The Story Bible, the records and the site exist to serve the drafting. A planning document that has outgrown what drafting uses is cut back, and the cut goes to the author as a suggestion.

00 — Benchmarks · 6

The benchmark read

Every chapter, act and book is read against this document, and the result is recorded in the Benchmark reads table of Manuscripts/Continuity.md.

Every chapter. In the scene plan, then again by the cold reader:

  1. Danger: who is in danger this week, and from what.
  2. At risk: which of the people the reader loves, a principal or a supporting-cast member, is in danger in their body this week. A week in which only strangers, papers or careers are at risk is a week the reader can put the book down.
  3. Charges: which two or more of the four the chapter carries, and the scene for each.
  4. Nearest fault: which of the six faults the chapter is nearest, and why it doesn't fall in.
  5. The question: who is holding on, and what holding on feeds.
  6. Weakest pillars: the one or two pillars the chapter reaches least, and what would lift them.
  7. Pull: the line a reader would quote, and where attention sagged.

Every act, at the act read: the act's reversal hurts someone; all four charges appear; every pillar is reached somewhere in the act; the full-voice moment and the wide shot land.

Every book, at its epilogue: its row in section 1 holds, and its ending pays off.
