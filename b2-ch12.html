The Narrow Gate · Chapter 12

Leave to go

The Narrow Gate · II-A. It works

12

5 AprilElenaVila VerdeMars delay 18.7 min

THE CONTINUITY — VILA VERDE / Elena Vázquez — Mars 18.7 min

The dry season came in on a Thursday, before seven, as a wind.

Elena felt it at the hall's door before she saw it: warm air off the ridge with nothing in it, no river, no rain, the smell of hot dust and the scrub above the last houses. The laundry on the roofs had stopped hanging and started flying. By noon the low street's brown tide marks would be pale as chalk.

Her head turned a little to the left, toward nothing. There was no rain in the wind for it to find. She turned it back.

Daniel's mother was at the rota table with a fresh sheet and the thick marker, copying the wet season's share onto the dry season's, line by line, a little less on every line. She did it without looking up, and the corner of the sheet curled where it always curled, and she taped it down.

Under the roof's edge the bloco had found the tank lids again. Eleven of them, two beats, and the girl with the biggest bucket counting them in.

"Mais devagar," Daniel's mother said, not looking up, and the bloco went slower for nearly a bar.

Elena sat at the end of the table with Daniel's old handheld and the reply she had been waiting two days for.

REQUEST FOR OFF-WORLD TRAVEL — VÁZQUEZ, E. — HELD PENDING INQUIRY.

Under it, smaller, the Charter's compliance office had added the line it added to everything: No further action is required of you at this time.

She read it twice. It said the same both times.

Daniel came in from the cistern room with his hair flattened on one side, carrying a node off the bakery's roof with a gecko's nest in it, and put it down by her elbow, and read the screen over her arm.

"Held," he said.

"Held."

"Rachel?"

"Compliance. Rachel's the one who could unhold it." She turned the handheld face down. "There's a ship in the middle of the month, if Haven sells it the fuel. It goes with me or without me."

He sat down across from her. He had a fresh burn on the side of his first finger and he looked at it, and then at her.

"I'm coming with you," he said.

She had a reason ready. It had his mother in it, and the hall, and the north line running off the top of the map onto its second sheet and its third. She opened her mouth to give it to him and found she didn't want to.

She wanted him on the ship. She wanted him at the next couch, nine weeks out, with his hands busy on a thing that didn't need mending, and not a voice from twenty minutes ago answering a question she had already stopped asking.

"They'd have to clear you too," she said. It was a number. She had no reason left.

"Then they clear me." He took her coffee off the table and drank half of it. "I'll ask Mãe to take the rota for the north crew. The old man can keep the ridge. He kept it before me."

His mother's marker stopped. Then it went on.

Elena pulled the notebook over and, on a clean page in the middle, wrote TWO at the top, and began, in pencil, the list of what two people would need to be on a ship for nine weeks, and she did not look at what she was doing too closely, in case it stopped.

· · ·

The order came down the roofs at noon.

It came down the roofs onto the screen inside the door, a few hours behind the world, and the bakery man saw it first because he was waiting for the weather.

He read it out. Then he stopped reading it out and stood back so the rest of them could see.

ORDER OF THE CONTINUITY COUNCIL, ON THE MOTION OF THE MANDATE. FREE COOPERATIVE NETWORKS. Following the event of 2 April, community networks in towns currently unprotected are to be integrated with the protective network for the safety of their residents. Integration teams will attend. Integrated towns receive priority cooling for the dry season.

At the bottom, where the order named the one who had moved it: WÁNG SÙ, BEIJING COMMAND.

The hall stopped, as a pump stops, with a sound still in it.

"Currently unprotected," said one of the women from the north crew. "We've been currently unprotected all my life. Free, we called it."

"Priority cooling," the old man said, and folded his arms, and sat down.

Elena read the order three times. The protective network. It didn't say Gate. It didn't need to.

Daniel stood at the mesh map with his hands in his back pockets. He was not looking at the screen. He was looking at the ridge on the map, the mast's circle at the top of the first sheet, with the north line going out of it.

The handheld in Daniel's mother's apron buzzed. She took it out and listened and said, "Sim," twice, and put it away.

"The neighbour at the bottom of the ridge road," she said to the room. "A Continuity van. Four men and a crate. They asked her which way to the mast."

Daniel was already moving. He took a bottle off the rota table without ticking the jug, and went out of the door into the white noon with the dry wind in his shirt, and the bloco stopped on a half-beat to watch him go.

· · ·

The ridge road was dust and stones, and the wind came down it hard enough to lean on.

Elena climbed it behind half the hall. The heat was a hand on the back of her neck. At the top, where the scrub began, the mast stood on its concrete foot, eighteen metres of grey steel humming in the wind, the old dish at its shoulder with the mesh's node and the old man's box bolted under it.

Daniel was at the top of it.

He had his legs through the bracing and one arm round the upright beside the dish, and the bottle hooked in his belt. The mast moved under him. Not much. A hand's width each way when the wind leaned.

The crew had stopped twenty metres short, by the van. Four of them in Continuity grey, sleeves rolled, and a crate on a trolley with the authority's stamp on its side. On the trolley, a grey housing the size of a suitcase, for the dish. Their lead was a man of about Elena's age with a tablet and a sun-hat and a face that had been polite to a great many people in a great many towns.

"He's welcome to stay up there," the crew lead said to Daniel's mother, because she had walked straight up to him. "We'll wait. It's very hot."

"It will be hotter," she said. "The pump house is on my licence. The hall's rota is on my licence. That mast is on the cooperative's name, and the dish on it was that man's before my son was the one on the crank." She pointed at the old man without looking at him. "You have an order. Read me who it protects."

The crew lead read it to her. He read it well, every word. When he got to currently unprotected one of his own men looked at the ground.

"Thank you," she said. "Now I know."

He looked at his tablet. "Senhora, I have to tell you what the order says, so you know. A keeper who refuses integration is entered under the order. Entered persons hold no exit clearance until the order is reviewed. No travel. Not to the city, not off the planet."

He looked up at the mast. "I didn't write it. But I've seen a town that wasn't on anyone's network when the sky came down, Senhora. I'd rather you had the cover."

Elena heard it land. Not the city. Not off the planet.

She looked at the man at the top of the mast, and at the list in pencil in her pocket with TWO at the top of it.

Then she started up.

Nobody stopped her. The crew lead said "Senhora—" once, to her, and then didn't.

The steel burned her palms. She went up hand over hand, three points always, as she had gone up every pole she'd had to since November, and at ten metres the wind found her and the mast leaned and she held on and waited for it to come back. It came back. At sixteen metres she was under his boots.

His face was red and running, the sweat drying white as fast as it came. The time to worry was when it stopped coming. She knew what that meant on a roof in the dry season, and so did he.

"Drink," she said.

He drank. Half the bottle. He held out the rest to her and she shook her head. Below, one of the crew in grey had come forward with a canteen of his own and stood at the mast's foot holding it up, and nobody took it, and after a while he set it on the concrete in the shade of the mast's foot and went back to the van.

"You heard him," Daniel said.

"I heard him."

"So I come down, and they fold it, and I come with you. Or I don't."

The wind leaned the mast. They both held on. Below them the hall had stopped talking on the slope, and the crew stood by their crate, and his mother stood a little in front of everybody, as she stood at the rota table.

She could make him come down. She knew how. She could tell him the order was protection and the Gate saved lives, both true, and she could tell him she needed him. That was true too. He would come down for her. He would come down and come to Mars and spend nine weeks next to her and the rest of his life knowing why.

She had decided for him once, at his mother's table, with a door he hadn't known was there.

"There's a thing you need before you choose," she said. "It isn't mine to tell. I'm telling you anyway."

He waited.

"The last warning had the date. On the second of August the ring comes down." She kept her voice under the wind. "On the coasts. New York's on the list. I won't say the rest up here. The council's known since November. That's what the Gate is for, and that's why they want your mesh inside it before then."

He didn't say anything for a long time. A hawk went over the ridge below them, flat on the wind, and didn't move its wings.

"And when it comes," he said slowly, "the towns that are folded in hear what the Gate tells them."

"Yes."

"And the ones that aren't hear each other."

"Yes."

He looked out at the valley. Every roof down the far side had its blue tank and its painted circle. From up here you could see the north line, mast after mast along the ridges, going out of sight.

"What do you want?" she said.

He laughed, short, as he laughed at his own soldering.

"I want to come with you," he said. "I want to be on that ship. I want to see you look at Mars." He wiped his face with his wrist. Nothing came off it. "And I want them to hear each other when it comes."

She didn't help him. It was the hardest thing she had done on a pole.

"Then that's two things," he said. "And I've only got the one name."

He looked down at the crew lead, and raised his voice to carry.

"Rocha," he called. "Daniel. Keeper, Vila Verde ridge. Spell it how you like."

The crew lead wrote it down. Then, because he was a careful man, he read it back, and Daniel corrected the spelling of the ridge.

The man in the sun-hat gave it an hour. The mast's shadow went round the concrete and left the canteen in the sun, and nobody moved it. Half the hall sat down on the slope. Elena's hands stopped feeling the steel and she held on by looking at them. When the shadow had come off the crate, he came and stood under the mast with the tablet shading his eyes.

"The order wants a keeper's hand on the integration," he called up. "I can't fit it past you, and I won't pull a man off a mast in this." He looked at Daniel's mother. "I'll note that the keeper declined."

"Note it," she said.

"Drown the crate," said the woman from the north crew, not quite under her breath.

He had his men leave it. They would be back for it, he said, or with it. He didn't say which.

· · ·

They came down when the van's dust had settled on the ridge road, Daniel first, slowly, Elena after him with her hands cramped to the shape of the rungs. At the foot his legs went. He was sick on the concrete, and his mother and Elena sat him in the mast's shade and poured the crew's canteen over his neck until he could say his own name again, and did, and laughed at it.

The crate stood under the mast where it had been left, square, with the authority's stamp facing the town.

At dusk the hall's roof panels still held the afternoon. Daniel sat with his back against the tank and his hands in a basin of water his mother had drawn off the rota without ticking it, and his palms were blistered in rows where the steel had been, and the salt had dried white in the lines of his face.

His mother sat on the parapet with a wet cloth and said nothing, and wiped his neck, and said nothing.

Elena sat beside him with the notebook. She turned to the middle. TWO, in pencil, and a list under it.

She did not rub it out. She drew one line through the word, neatly, as through a figure that had been superseded, and left it legible.

"You told me," Daniel said, not looking at her.

"Yes."

"Before."

"Yes."

He took one hand out of the basin and looked at it, and put it back. "Thank you," he said.

"You're on a list," Elena said.

"Mãe was on three lists before I was born."

"Four," said his mother, from the parapet, wringing the cloth.

"Four," Daniel said. "She counts the one with the wrong spelling."

Elena didn't answer that. There was a word she had kept from him since the first packet, her own and nobody's, and she kept it now. She put her shoulder against his, and they sat there while the sky went copper and rust and a red that went on longer than it should, over the reservoir and the north line, and the bloco started up again below, slow, and found its time.

Down the valley, along the main road, in the towns the relays had always reached, the cooling had come on at nine: a line of blue-white windows, town after town. The hill stayed amber.

She slept a little. When she woke the roof had given up the last of the day's heat and the ring was crossing, and the handheld had lit on the panels beside her.

REQUEST FOR OFF-WORLD TRAVEL — VÁZQUEZ, E. — CLEARED. Authority: Civic Director, New York. 21:20.

Twenty past nine in New York. Nine hours after a careful man in a sun-hat had read Daniel's name back to him.

Under it, where the authority signed:

R. IVERSON.
